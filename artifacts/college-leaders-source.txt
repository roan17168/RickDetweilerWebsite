1202: Search description: For presidents, provosts, deans, directors and trustees. A two-minute check on whether budget, incentives and messaging support the college experiences linked to graduates' lives.
1203: Landing
1204: Label: For college leaders
1205: Heading: Your mission statement makes a promise. Does your budget keep it?
1206: An analysis of the mission statements of 241 colleges found them promising much the same things: leaders, engaged citizens, lifelong learners, fulfilled and successful people. Interviews with 1,000 graduates show which experiences are linked to those outcomes. Many of them cost little. All of them depend on what the institution rewards. And they matter most for the students who arrive with the least: in the study, several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families.

1235: Result: Market-first
1236: Your institution's choices follow enrollment and employment signals. That keeps the doors open. The risk is competing on first-job outcomes, where specialized programs will always look better in year one.
1237: What the research says to you. In the study, major was unrelated to longer-term success, and the first-job advantage of specialized preparation faded. Breadth of study and close involvement with faculty were what related to success over time. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families. That is a market argument you are not yet using.

1243: This year. Survey alumni ten and twenty or more years out with questions on their adult lives: not only success, but also civic involvement, leadership, further education, cultural involvement and personal fulfillment.
1244: Result: Mission-first
1245: Your institution knows what it stands for and says so well. The open question is whether students experience it, and whether you could show that they do.
1246: What the research says to you. The outcomes named in most mission statements were linked to specific experiences: professors who know students, conversation outside class, mentoring, breadth of study, engaging teaching, and involvement in the college community. Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Where most professors knew students' first names, graduates were 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. A mission is delivered through those experiences or not at all.

1253: Result: Experience-first
1254: Your institution organizes itself around what students experience. The evidence supports that choice. The work is to protect it under budget pressure and to prove it to people outside.
1255: What the research says to you. Graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively as adults. The book notes that many of these practices cost little in new money. They rest on how faculty and staff spend their time, how requirements are written, and how teaching is done. But faculty time is the largest cost a college has, so the real question is what faculty are asked to stop doing. Costs rise when the institution asks faculty to do all of this on top of everything they did before. The checklist below marks which practices are budget neutral, which need time or money moved from elsewhere, and which need new investment.

1263: The retention case (shown under every result)
1264: The experiences linked to graduates' lives are also linked to whether students stay. Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to persist when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of the institution. George Kuh found that first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research, service learning and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year, with the largest benefits for underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students. Every student who stays is a student who can graduate, and tuition the institution does not have to replace.
1265: Other research (shown under every result)
1266: Alexander Astin studied about 25,000 students at 309 institutions. He found the peer group to be the single most powerful influence on students' development, with interaction with faculty next, and found that a heavy institutional emphasis on research can be associated with lower student satisfaction and weaker development.
1267: Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, reviewing decades of research, concluded that differences between colleges explain less than what students do once enrolled. Engagement is the lever.
1268: Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to stay when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of the institution. The experiences that are linked to good lives later are also the ones that keep students enrolled now.
1269: Gallup-Purdue found that only 3% of graduates strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.
1270: It has been done (shown under every result)
1271: Heading: It has been done, at every kind of institution
1272: George Kuh and colleagues studied 20 colleges and universities whose graduation rates and student engagement were higher than predicted for the students they enrolled. They included research universities, regional universities, liberal arts colleges and historically Black universities. Three examples from their study:
1273: A liberal arts college: Ursinus College. Every first-year student took a two-semester Common Intellectual Experience course. Students lived near classmates from their section, some classes met in the residence halls, and advising was shared by teams of faculty and staff.
1274: A research university: the University of Kansas. The university made undergraduate teaching a visible priority, with awards and rewards for good teaching, faculty "ambassadors" linked to its teaching center, and most undergraduate courses were kept at 30 students or fewer.
1275: A small college: Alverno College. Student affairs staff worked as partners in learning, with outcomes for life outside class defined to match the abilities the college expects every graduate to develop.
1276: Other institutions in the study included the University of Michigan, George Mason University, the University of Texas at El Paso, Fayetteville State University and Winston-Salem State University. What the twenty shared was not wealth or selectivity but six conditions: a mission that is lived, not only stated; an unshakeable focus on student learning; clearly marked pathways to success; a habit of continuous improvement; campus settings adapted for learning; and shared responsibility for educational quality.
1277: These examples describe the institutions in the early 2000s, when those studies were done.
1278: Note: Examples are as described in Student Success in College (2005). Please check the details against the book before publishing, or replace them with institutions you know.
1279: Institutional checklist (tool)
1280: Heading: Eight practices, many of which cost little in new money
1281: What it's for. The checklist estimates how many of your students actually get the experiences the research links to graduates' lives, names who owns each one, and sets one next step for each. Most of these practices depend more on how time and requirements are arranged than on new money.
1282: Who should complete it. A small group works best: the chief academic officer, the chief student affairs officer, the head of institutional research and someone from finance. Allow one meeting to gather data and one to complete the form.
1283: How to use it
1284: Gather the data first. Possible sources for each row are listed below. Where you have no data, say so: "unknown" is a finding. For the row on course practices, sample rather than review every course: for example, one section from each department, or NSSE items alone.
1285: Estimate the share of students who get each experience, as a percentage. If you can, break it out for commuters, transfers, part-time and first-generation students, who are often least likely to get them.
1286: Rate each practice on this scale:
1287: Widespread: most students get it, roughly three-quarters or more.
1288: Uneven: some do, often depending on major or on a few people.
1289: Rare: few students get it, roughly a quarter or fewer.
1290: Unknown: no reliable data.
1291: Name an owner. One office or person responsible for each practice, not a committee.
1292: Mark the cost. Is the next step budget neutral, a reallocation of time or money, a policy change, or a new investment? The table below gives the usual answer for each practice.
1293: Choose one next step and a date for each practice.
1294: Report the results to your board once a year, alongside enrollment and placement figures, and repeat the checklist annually. Share them publicly once you can show where you stand and what you are doing about it.
1295: Using it for accreditation. Accreditors ask institutions to show that students achieve the outcomes the institution promises. The checklist, the three senior-survey questions in the Mission-first result and the alumni survey below can all serve as evidence in a self-study or assessment plan.

1403: Use or adapt:
1404: "A graduate's first-year earnings tell you about a first job, not a working life. Americans now hold nine or more jobs by their late thirties, and employers say the skills they weigh most in hiring are communication, problem-solving and adaptability. Studies that follow graduates for decades find that the earnings advantage of attending a famous college fades, and that what students experience in college, professors who know them, a mentor, broad study and teaching that makes them think, is linked to how their lives turn out. Those experiences matter most for students from families with little money. We are measuring how many of our students get them, and we are working to make sure every student does. [Your request.]"

1408: Adult life
1409: Leadership. In the past five years, have you led a team, organization, project or group at work or in your community?
1410: Civic involvement. Did you vote in the most recent national election? In the past year, about how many hours did you volunteer? About what share of your income did you give to charities or causes?
1411: Continued learning. In the past year, have you taken a course, earned a credential, or read or studied regularly to learn something new?
1412: Cultural involvement. In the past year, how often did you attend concerts, theater, museums or talks, or read about or discuss the arts and ideas?

1424: For enrollment and admissions leaders (shown under every result)
1425: Families will ask harder questions. The student and parent pages of this site suggest questions for visits and calls: who does the talking in class, what a first-year student can be in charge of, how many professors will know a student by name, and the graduation rate for students who receive Pell Grants. Prepare tour guides and counselors with specific, recent stories. On the student scorecard, a website claim alone counts as "Don't know yet."
1426: Families will expect plain talk about price. The site tells families that published prices are rarely what they pay, and that they may ask for an aid offer to be reviewed. Be ready to explain your aid clearly.
1427: The retention case funds this work. The same practices that are linked to graduates' lives are linked to students staying. Use the retention case above with your budget committee.
1428: Citing the research. Cite it as Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021).
1429: A meaningful life in a world of change (shown under every result)
1430: Nearly every college mission statement promises more than employment: leaders, citizens, lifelong learners, people who live with purpose. That is the oldest promise of American higher education, an education for the common good that serves both the graduate and society. Public confidence in that promise is fragile. In 2026, 38% of Americans said they had a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in higher education, down from 42% a year earlier. This study gives you evidence to keep the promise and to show it: the experiences linked to graduates' income are the same ones linked to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning and fulfillment. Your graduates will live through constant change: Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. An institution that aligns its mission, budget, promotion criteria and reporting with those experiences prepares graduates for a whole life, and gives the public a reason to trust it again.
