[137] How to use this document
[138] This document holds the recommended text for every page of the revised site. Each chapter is one page or one audience pathway. Text is written to be pasted into the site as it stands.
[139] Note: Shaded notes like this one are for you and the developer. They are not site text.
[140] What changed from the draft site
[141] Every audience now follows the same path: a short quiz, a result, essential questions, and one tool to take away.
[142] Every quiz answer leads to a written result. No one is shown a default.
[143] No one picks an outcome. Results come only from quiz answers.
[144] Every claim about the study matches the book. "Proves" has become "is linked to." The study is described the same way on every page.
[145] Findings from other researchers are added where they support the same point, and are always credited to them.
[146] The site leads with the college experience, not the label "liberal arts."
[147] Every audience section ends with a closing on a meaningful life in a world of change, written for that audience, and a clear end point with next steps.
[148] Every tool (scorecard, plan, self-audit, checklist, brief) has full instructions and a complete form.
[149] The two student pathways are written in plain language for their readers, with one rounded number per finding and short excerpts from graduates quoted in the book.
[150] The site is named What College Is Really For, and the home page headline no longer addresses only the student.
[151] A new page, Understanding College, explains the kinds of American colleges, where the liberal arts idea came from, what employers say, and why prestige and rankings are weak guides. Its findings carry numbered endnotes.
[152] A photo and graphics plan sets out ten photos and six simple graphics, with placement, alt text and permissions.
[153] After testing with two student readers, one the first in her family to go to college and one whose sister attends an Ivy, Choosing a College now opens with what college really costs and a guide for students who are the first in their family. The quiz has an optional question about cost, belonging, getting in and not getting a first choice. There is advice for large and selective universities, phone-first rules and a more recent jobs figure.
[154] After testing with two parents, one who did not go to college and has little money and one who expects to pay most of the price, the Parents section now opens with what college really costs for both kinds of family, a parent's timeline, honest information on loans and other paths, and a plain definition of "liberal arts." An optional question adds panels on well-being, AI and jobs, merit aid and more. The About and Research pages now describe the author's background.
[155] After testing with three professors, at a liberal arts college, a research university and a nonselective comprehensive university, the Faculty practice cards now give versions for small classes, large courses, students who work or commute, and part-time faculty, and examples for STEM and professional programs. They also name who carries the mentoring load. The self-audit asks students. The Research page explains how the analysis worked, and the descriptions of kinds of colleges are fairer to research and comprehensive universities.
[156] After testing with a dean, a vice president for enrollment and a president, the College Leaders section now makes the retention case, shows examples from institutions of every kind, and marks the usual cost of each checklist practice. It adds a row on who teaches first-year courses, a one-page board summary, a message for legislators and donors, an alumni survey template, sample promotion and tenure language, and a block for enrollment leaders. Quiz questions 3 and 5 are reworded.
[157] After testing with three college students, the first in her family at a comprehensive university, a sophomore at a liberal arts college and a first-year at a research university, the In College cards now give versions for big universities, students who work or commute, students already doing well, and set-requirement programs such as engineering. New blocks cover "When college gets hard" and "Questions students ask," the plan lets students choose any three moves, and quiz question 5 is softened.
[158] After testing with a Republican state legislator, a Democratic state senator and a Democratic U.S. senator, the Policymakers section now leads with three findings, makes the quiz optional, frames early earnings as a floor rather than a ceiling, gives findings with endnotes, and adds the completion case, what the study covers, a federal section ("In Washington"), report-request and hearing language, and a leave-behind. The Research page adds study details: who did the research, who funded it, how graduates were chosen and where they went to college.
[159] After the author's review, findings are given again as "percent more likely," the book's percent relative effect, and the Research page explains how to read them. The closing block on every audience section is rewritten around the site's central idea: college at its best prepares people for a meaningful and successful life in a world of change, for their own good and the common good. Each version is written for that audience's own hopes. The plan to collect recent graduates' words has been dropped.

[196] How the numbers are shown (all pages)
[197] Findings are shown as "percent more likely": how much more likely graduates who had an experience were to report an outcome than graduates who did not, for example "24% more likely to report a higher income." This is the book's percent relative effect, and every figure comes from the book's appendix tables. "How to read these numbers" on the Research page explains it.
[198] A meaningful life in a world of change (closing block on every audience section)
[199] Every audience section ends with a short closing on the same idea, written for what that reader cares about most: college at its best prepares people for a meaningful and successful life in a world of change. That is the old idea of an education for the common good. It benefits the graduate, through good jobs and income and a life that feels worthwhile, and it prepares the graduate to contribute to society as a leader and an involved citizen. The job figures show that change is constant; they are one piece of evidence, not the main point. Each version starts from that audience's own hopes: students' reasons for going to college, parents' hopes for their children, why professors teach, the promises in college mission statements, and the three kinds of public return policymakers look for.
[200] Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs.
[201] The Bureau of Labor Statistics does not estimate how many times people change careers, because there is no agreed definition of a career change. The site therefore speaks of jobs, not careers.
[202] Note: Sources: Bureau of Labor Statistics, National Longitudinal Surveys, "Number of Jobs, Labor Market Experience, Marital Status, and Health" (NLSY97 release, May 5, 2026, reissued July 17, 2026) and the NLS questions-and-answers page; World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025 (January 2025). This figure replaced the earlier one for Americans born in the early 1960s (12.7 jobs from ages 18 to 56), which student readers took to be about their parents' generation. Your change list asked for the number of times people change careers. BLS states that it has never estimated this, so I used its job counts instead. Figures often quoted online for career changes do not trace to a reliable source.

[243] What the research found
[244] Heading: Three things the graduates had in common
[245] 1. Learning didn't stop at the classroom door. These graduates talked with professors outside class, about coursework and about everything else. They had a mentor whose influence lasted, and they took a real part in campus life alongside other students. Of everything the study measured, this involvement with faculty and students outside class was the experience most consistently linked to every kind of life outcome.
[246] 2. They studied widely, and major mattered little. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults. Major was unrelated to longer-term success and to most other life outcomes, from leadership to a fulfilled life.
[247] 3. They were challenged to think. Graduates whose courses involved discussion, writing, and questions with no single right answer were more likely to keep learning and to describe their lives as fulfilled.
[248] Link: See the research
[249] Six outcomes
[250] Heading: Six ways college shows up in adult life
[251] Leadership. Taking responsibility and moving a group forward.
[252] Civic Involvement. Voting, volunteering and giving to causes and community.
[253] Continued Learning. Staying curious and able to learn new things.
[254] Cultural Involvement. Keeping ideas, arts and other cultures in your life.
[255] Fulfillment. A life that feels meaningful to the person living it.
[256] Personal Success. Position, income and a career that keeps growing.
[257] Closing
[258] Heading: The college matters. What happens there matters more.
[259] These findings held across every type of college and student in the study.
[260] A meaningful life in a world of change. The oldest purpose of American college is also the most practical: an education for the common good. It helps each graduate build a good life, with work that pays and work worth doing, and it prepares each graduate to give back as a leader, a neighbor and a citizen. In the study behind this site, the two went together: the same experiences were linked to higher income and to leadership, volunteering, continued learning and a fulfilled life. And the world keeps changing. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. An education that teaches people to learn, to think and to work with others prepares them for all of it, and for a life that matters to them and to the people around them.

[278] What college really costs
[279] Heading: The price on the website is not the price you'll pay.
[280] Most students pay less than the published price, often much less. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the published tuition. Some colleges with high published prices cost students from lower-income families less than a public university does. You can find your real price before you apply.
[281] Net price calculator. Every college must post one on its website. Enter your family's information and it estimates what you would pay after grants. Search for the college's name and "net price calculator."
[282] College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov), from the US Department of Education. It shows each college's average yearly cost for families at your income level, and how many of its students graduate.
[283] Federal Student Aid: the FAFSA (studentaid.gov/h/apply-for-aid/fafsa). This is where you fill out the federal financial aid form that colleges use. It is needed for most grants, work-study jobs and loans.
[284] First in your family to go to college?
[285] You're not behind. Many students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it. These five steps matter most:
[286] Talk to your school counselor this year. Say: "I want to go to college, and I'm the first in my family. What should I be doing now?" Counselors expect this question.
[287] Try three net price calculators, including at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
[288] Ask about fee waivers. If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free. Your counselor can request the waivers.
[289] File the FAFSA (the federal student aid form) in the fall of your senior year. It opens each fall for the next school year. File early, because some aid runs out.
[290] Keep every path open. Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home. The questions on this site work for all of them.
[291] Can't visit? Most colleges offer virtual tours and online information sessions, and many will put you in touch with a current student by email or video. Some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit; search for the college's name and "fly-in program."

"First in your family" follows "What you get." Both also appear in short form as panels on the result page when a student picks them in the optional quiz question.

[345] Result: The Leader (Leadership)
[346] You want to be the person people count on when something needs to get done.
[347] What the research found. Graduates who became leaders had something in common: professors who took an interest in them outside class. Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. What they majored in made no real difference.
[348] What that looks like at a college
[349] Professors who know students by name and talk with them outside class.
[350] Students who get to run things: clubs, teams, projects, campus jobs.
[351] Students with different backgrounds and views who work together.
[352] A graduate says
[353] "Each student has value; students are not just a number. The faculty and staff get to know you by name."
[354] David, nonprofit leader and state legislator
[355] Ask out loud
[356] Ask a student: "Is there a professor who's pushed you to do something bigger than you planned?"
[357] Ask a student: "In your classes, who does most of the talking, the professor or the students?"
[358] Ask admissions: "What can a first-year student be in charge of here?"
[359] Ask admissions: "How do first-years end up talking with professors outside class?"
[360] Or find out without asking
[361] Look at the clubs on the college's website or social accounts. Are any first- or second-year students in charge?
[362] Sit in on a class if the college offers it. Count how many students speak.
[363] Other research agrees. Alexander Astin followed about 25,000 students at 309 colleges. He found that the people around you shape you more than anything else in college, with time spent with professors close behind.
[364] Try this one thing. On your next visit, ask one student to name a professor who changed what they thought they could do.
[365] Result: The Community Builder (Civic Involvement)
[366] You want what you learn to matter to someone besides you.
[367] What the research found. The study checked whether graduates vote, volunteer and give to causes. Graduates who had been closely involved with professors and other students outside class were 42% more likely to volunteer more as adults, and 56% more likely to give a larger share of their income. Their major made little difference.
[368] What that looks like at a college
[369] Courses that work with real community groups, beyond reading about them.
[370] Service that lasts a semester or more, so trust has time to build.
[371] Students who live and work with people unlike themselves.
[372] A graduate says
[373] "…college provided more than a degree—it provided me with knowledge, purpose, and a sense of civic responsibility."
[374] John, former state senator and member of Congress
[375] Ask out loud
[376] Ask a student: "Have you worked on something that helped people off campus? What was it?"
[377] Ask a student: "Is service here mostly one-day events, or can you stick with one thing?"
[378] Ask admissions: "Which classes work with community groups?"
[379] Ask a student: "How often do you work with people who see the world differently from you?"
[380] Or find out without asking
[381] Search the college's news page for "community partner." Are students doing the work, or posing for the photo?
[382] Check whether the service office lists programs that run all year.
[383] Other research agrees. When Richard Light interviewed Harvard students, they said most of what they learned from people different from themselves happened outside class, in residence halls and shared activities.
[384] Try this one thing. Ask a college for one example of a project where students did useful work with a community and had to learn from the people they were helping.
[385] Result: The Lifelong Learner (Continued Learning)
[386] You want to keep learning all your life, so you can keep up in an ever-changing world of knowledge, challenge and opportunity.
[387] What the research found. Graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively as adults. Studying well outside their major was linked to continued learning too.
[388] What that looks like at a college
[389] Room to take classes that have nothing to do with your major.
[390] Small first-year classes where students do the talking. Colleges call these seminars.
[391] Assignments where you argue a position, get comments, and try again.
[392] A graduate says
[393] "My college experience, from the first day, became one of constant discovery about myself, the world we live in, and how one can use one's education to work for changes that improve the lives of others."
[394] Pilar, founder of a microfinance organization
[395] Ask out loud
[396] Ask a student: "What class got you into something you never expected to like?"
[397] Ask a student: "Do tests here mostly ask you to remember things, or to figure things out?"
[398] Ask admissions: "Can I take a small discussion class in my first year?"
[399] Ask admissions: "How much of my schedule can be outside my major?"
[400] Or find out without asking
[401] Look up the first-year course list. How many classes have 20 or fewer students?
[402] Read the graduation requirements. How many courses are yours to choose?
[403] Other research agrees. Gallup-Purdue surveyed 30,000 graduates. Having a professor who made them excited about learning was one of a handful of experiences tied to doing well at work and in life later.
[404] Try this one thing. Find one class you'd take even though it has nothing to do with your career plan.
[405] Result: The Cultural Explorer (Cultural Involvement)
[406] You want college to make your world bigger: more ideas, art, history, places and kinds of people.
[407] What the research found. Graduates who took discussion classes in their first two years were 37% more likely to keep arts, ideas and other cultures closely in their lives as adults. Spending real time with people from other cultures and beliefs was linked to it too.
[408] What that looks like at a college
[409] Classes in literature, history, art, philosophy and languages that everyone takes, not only majors. Colleges call these the humanities.
[410] Talks, concerts, films and exhibits that ordinary students go to.
[411] Study abroad and arts programs that students can afford.
[412] A graduate says
[413] "The faculty members with whom I interacted nurtured in me a true love for the world of ideas."
[414] Larry, professor of language and culture
[415] Ask out loud
[416] Ask a student: "What's something you saw or heard here that you'd never have found at home?"
[417] Ask a student: "Do regular students go to the talks and shows, or just the same few?"
[418] Ask admissions: "Will I take classes across art, history, science and other fields, or can I skip most of them?"
[419] Ask admissions: "Can most students afford to study abroad?"
[420] Or find out without asking
[421] Read the student newspaper. What do students write about besides sports and parties?
[422] Look up what share of students study abroad. Most colleges publish it.
[423] Other research agrees. The National Survey of Student Engagement counts real contact with people different from yourself, and learning that goes beyond the classroom, among the experiences that do students the most good.
[424] Try this one thing. Look at a college's events calendar for a normal week. Pick three you'd want to go to, then ask a student if people really show up.
[425] Result: The Meaning Seeker (Fulfillment)
[426] You want a life that feels like yours: work and people you care about, and a sense of why.
[427] What the research found. Graduates who talked about big questions of right, wrong and meaning in most of their classes were 59% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, whatever they majored in. When professors made students test their own arguments, they were 149% more likely, about two and a half times as likely.
[428] What that looks like at a college
[429] Classes in every subject that ask what's right and what matters.
[430] Professors who ask you to find the weak spot in your own argument.
[431] An adviser, the person who helps you plan your courses, who also asks what you want from life.
[432] A graduate says
[433] "Although earning a degree was satisfying, benefits that I had not foreseen were even more fulfilling: a heightened awareness of the world outside family, friends, and community…"
[434] Annis, author of more than twenty books
[435] Ask out loud
[436] Ask a student: "Is there an adult here who knows what you care about, not just what you're studying?"
[437] Ask a student: "Do your classes ever get into what's right or what matters?"
[438] Ask a student: "What happens here when people disagree?"
[439] Ask admissions: "Does my adviser help with more than picking classes?"
[440] Or find out without asking
[441] Read a few course descriptions outside your likely major. Do any ask a question you'd want to argue about?
[442] Find out how advisers are assigned. Is it one person for four years, or a different office each time?
[443] Other research agrees. Gallup-Purdue found that graduates who had a professor who cared about them as a person, and a mentor who encouraged their goals, saw their odds of thriving later in life more than double.
[444] Try this one thing. On a visit, ask a student to tell you about one professor, coach, adviser or staff member who really knows them. Listen for a specific story.
[445] Result: The Career Builder (Personal Success)
[446] You want college to lead somewhere: a good job, your own money, and a career that keeps growing.
[447] What the research found. Here is the surprise, and it matters most for students from families without much money. In the study, a graduate's major had nothing to do with long-term success. Graduates from lower-income families who took more than half their classes outside their major were 33% more likely to report a higher income. Across all graduates the figure was 24%.
[448] What that looks like at a college
[449] Room to take plenty of classes outside your major and still finish on time. You can still choose nursing, business or engineering; make sure you also take courses outside it. That is what paid off later.
[450] Internships and big projects that most students do, with pay or funding.
[451] Career help that starts in the first year.
[452] A graduate says
[453] "…my liberal arts education did not prepare me for one particular job or profession; it prepared me for any that I would choose once I truly found my passion."
[454] Deborah, senior officer of a major corporation
[455] Ask out loud
[456] Ask admissions: "How many students do an internship, and are they paid?"
[457] Ask admissions: "Can I take a lot of classes outside my major and still graduate in four years?"
[458] Ask a student: "When did you first talk to the career office?"
[459] Ask a student: "Have you met graduates or employers through the college?"
[460] Or find out without asking
[461] Search for the college's "outcomes" page. Does it show where graduates are years later, or only at graduation or six months?
[462] Look at the required course list for a major you like. How much room is left over?
[463] Other research agrees. Gallup-Purdue found that an internship that used what students learned in class, and a project lasting a semester or more, were both tied to doing better at work after college.
[464] Try this one thing. Ask a college for a real example of a graduate whose path changed after the first job, and what part of college helped them adapt.
[465] Panels from the optional question (shown at the top of the result when chosen)
[466] Paying for it. The price on a college's website is not what most students pay. Find your real price before you rule a college out: try its net price calculator, and look up its average cost for families at your income on College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov). The FAFSA, at studentaid.gov, is the form that decides most grants. See "What college really costs" on the Choosing a College page.
[467] First in your family. You're not behind. Many students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it. Start with the five steps in "First in your family to go to college?" on the Choosing a College page, beginning with a conversation with your school counselor.
[468] Belonging and finishing. Feeling connected, in class and out, is one of the biggest reasons students stay and finish. Ask a college: "What happens for first-year students in their first six weeks?" "Is there a program or group for first-generation students?" And ask admissions for the graduation rate of students who receive Pell Grants, the federal grants for lower-income students. Colleges report it to the government.
[469] Getting in. Apply to a balanced list: two or three colleges where you are likely to be admitted and can afford to go, several in the middle, and a few reaches. Your counselor can help you sort them. Many good colleges admit most of the students who apply, and the experiences on this site can be found at all kinds of colleges.
[470] Not your first choice. Many students don't end up at their first choice, and they do well. In the study behind this site, what graduates experienced in college was linked to how their lives turned out, and those experiences were found at every kind of college. When decisions arrive, use your scorecard. Then read the In College section: it is a plan for getting those experiences wherever you go.

[650] A meaningful life in a world of change (shown under every result)
[651] Ask first-year college students why they came, and most say to get a better job: 82%. Even more, 87%, say they came to learn more about things that interest them, and 80% want a broad education and an appreciation of ideas. You don't have to choose. The experiences on this page were linked to all six outcomes in the study: a successful career and a good income, and also leading, giving back, staying curious and a life that feels like your own. You'll need all of it, because the world won't stand still. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. A good college prepares you to build a life you're proud of, and to make the people and the community around you better off too.

[723] The five cards (shown under every result)
[724] These links held for every kind of major, engineering and other professional majors included, when students had the experiences. And several were strongest for graduates who grew up with less money.
[725] 1. Time with a professor outside class
[726] What the research found. Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Among graduates late in their careers, the link between these relationships and success was strongest for those who grew up with less money.
[727] Why it helps now. Professors and teaching assistants are how students get help in time to pass a hard course. A professor who knows your work can also write a real recommendation and point you to research, jobs and people.
[728] A graduate says
[729] "…the professors were accessible and could share valuable insights. They also recognized my passion for medicine, so they directed me toward appropriate research opportunities and work experiences."
[730] Angela, trauma surgeon and director of a surgical residency
[731] Start small. Office hours are set times when a professor is in their office for any student. No appointment needed, and no problem needed either. Go once with a real question: "Can you help me understand one thing from Tuesday's class?" Next time, try: "I liked last week's topic. What should I read next?"
[732] At a big university. Start with your teaching assistant; TAs are often the way to the professor. Go to office hours in the first weeks of the term, when there is no line. Look for the small classes, such as first-year seminars, honors sections and writing courses, where professors learn names.
[733] Short on time? Email the same question, or stay two minutes after class. Many professors also hold office hours by video.
[734] Already doing this? Ask one professor to be involved with something that interests you: a project, a research question or an independent study.
[735] Other research agrees. Richard Light, after years of interviewing Harvard undergraduates, gave students one piece of advice above others: get to know one faculty member reasonably well each semester.
[736] 2. Courses outside your major
[737] What the research found. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families. Major had nothing to do with long-term success.
[738] Why it helps now. It's the cheapest way to find out what you're good at before you commit to a path.
[739] A graduate says
[740] "…a well-rounded liberal arts education prepared and enabled me to quickly adapt and transition to other career options."
[741] Theressa, physician
[742] Start small. At your next registration, choose one course only because it sounds interesting.
[743] Engineering, nursing or another program with set requirements? Use whatever electives you have on something far from your field, or sit in on one lecture. The links in the study held for engineering students too, when they had these experiences.
[744] Already doing this? Look for a course that connects two of your interests, or a minor far from your major.
[745] 3. Classes that make you think
[746] What the research found. Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled: about two and a half times as likely.
[747] Why it helps now. These are the classes that give you something to say in an interview.
[748] A graduate says
[749] "…taught me three critical things that have made all the difference: How to Learn, How to Think, and How to Communicate."
[750] Deborah, senior officer of a major corporation, on her professors
[751] Start small. In your next paper, add one paragraph that begins: "The strongest argument against my view is…"
[752] In science, math or engineering. On your next problem set or design, add one line: "Where could this go wrong?" Or explain why your approach is better than another one that also works.
[753] At a big university. When a course offers a choice of sections, discussion groups or labs, take the smaller one, and speak at least once each week.
[754] 4. Being part of something on campus
[755] What the research found. Graduates who took an active part in campus organizations were 35% more likely to volunteer more as adults. Leading or joining campus groups was also linked to leadership and fulfillment later in life.
[756] Why it helps now. It's where most students find their people.
[757] A graduate says
[758] "In retrospect, I probably developed most of my leadership and crisis management skills as a dorm resident assistant."
[759] Angela, trauma surgeon
[760] Start small. Go to one meeting of one group and ask: "What do you need help with?"
[761] At a big university. Pick two: one group connected to your field, such as a design team or a professional society, and one that has nothing to do with it.
[762] If you work or commute. An on-campus job puts you beside the same people every week. Ask the student employment office, and ask the financial aid office whether you have work-study, a federal program that pays for campus jobs. A regular study group works too. One hour in the same place beats five separate events.
[763] Already doing a lot? Do fewer things more deeply. Lead one group rather than attending four.
[764] Other research agrees. Alexander Astin's study of about 25,000 students found that the more students put into college, in and out of class, the more they got from it.
[765] 5. Real conversations with people who see things differently
[766] What the research found. Graduates who often talked with people whose values and backgrounds differed from their own were 38% more likely to keep learning actively as adults, and 26% more likely to be leaders.
[767] Why it helps now. Every job involves working with people who see things differently.
[768] A graduate says
[769] "[I] learned to interact with students of all socioeconomic, racial, and ethnic backgrounds."
[770] A graduate interviewed for the study
[771] Start small. Ask a classmate: "How did you come to see it that way?" Then listen for five minutes before you answer.
[772] When disagreement feels risky. Choose a setting where it is expected: a class discussion, a debate or dialogue group, or a project team that agrees on ground rules first.
[773] Short on time? Do it inside a class discussion or group project you already have.
[774] When college gets hard (shown under every result)
[775] The cards above are about getting more out of college. Sometimes the job is getting through it. The same people, professors, advisers and staff, are how most students do.
[776] Struggling in a class. Go to the professor or teaching assistant the first week you feel lost, not the week before the exam. Find the tutoring or learning center. It is usually free, and most students who use it are not failing.
[777] Keeping your financial aid. Federal aid requires "satisfactory academic progress": usually a minimum grade point average and finishing enough of the credits you attempt. Renew the FAFSA every year. If your grades or your family's situation change, talk with the financial aid office early.
[778] Money or family emergencies. Many colleges have emergency grants, food pantries or short-term loans. Ask the dean of students office.
[779] Feeling like you don't belong. Many students feel this, especially in the first year. Look for a first-generation student group or a mentoring program; they exist to help with exactly this.
[780] Stress, anxiety or feeling low. The counseling center is usually free for students. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.
[781] Thinking of leaving. Talk with an adviser or a dean first. A lighter course load or a leave of absence can keep your options open, while leaving without a plan can mean losing aid and still owing loans.
[782] Questions students ask (shown under every result)
[783] "What are you going to do with that?" If you are studying history, philosophy or another field without one obvious job, here is the answer. Employers say the skills they weigh most in hiring are communication (64%), problem-solving (62%) and adaptability (60%). A major prepares you for a field. These experiences prepare you for the world of change.
[784] "Why not let AI write it?" Software can write a paragraph arguing against your view in seconds. The research is about what happens when you do it yourself: graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Use AI the way your professor allows, and do the thinking yourself.
[785] "Can I afford an internship?" Ask the career office whether the college funds unpaid or low-paid internships and research. Many do. Paid research positions and campus jobs in your field count too.

[849] A meaningful life in a world of change (shown under every result)
[850] College is more than getting through classes to get a job. It's where you find out what you care about, who you want to become and what you have to give. Graduates in this study who got to know professors, studied widely, were pushed to think and got involved were more likely, decades later, to earn more, to lead, to volunteer, to keep learning and to say their lives felt fulfilled. You're building those things now, every time you go to office hours, take a course just because it interests you or show up for a group. The world will keep changing around you: Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. What lasts is the person college helps you become, and what you do with it for yourself and for others.

[869] What college really costs
[870] Heading: The price on the website is rarely the price families pay.
[871] Most families pay less than the published price. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the published tuition. Some grants are based on what a family can pay, and some on a student's record.
[872] If money is tight
[873] You may pay little or nothing. Some colleges with high published prices give the most aid, and can cost families with low incomes less than a nearby public university.
[874] Find your real price in about fifteen minutes with each college's net price calculator. Every college must post one; search for the college's name and "net price calculator."
[875] College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov), from the US Department of Education, shows each college's average yearly cost for families at your income, its graduation rate and its graduates' typical debt.
[876] Be wary of any college where many students leave with debt and no degree. College Scorecard shows both.
[877] If you will pay most of the price
[878] Look for merit scholarships. Many colleges offer them to strong students whatever a family's income. Many net price calculators include them; where one doesn't, ask the financial aid office.
[879] Look at honors colleges within public universities: small classes and close contact with professors, at a public university's price.
[880] Compare the full four-year cost, not only the first year, and ask whether a scholarship continues if grades dip.
[881] Take a generous offer from a less famous college seriously. For a capable, motivated student, the research below suggests the name on the diploma matters less than what happens there.
[882] First time doing this? A parent's timeline
[883] You don't need to have gone to college to help your child get there. Here is what parents do, and when.
[884] Junior year. Talk with your child's school counselor, in person, by phone or by email. You can say: "We're new to this. What should we be doing this year?" Try three net price calculators together, including one at a college you assume is too expensive.
[885] Summer before senior year. Help your child make a balanced list: a few colleges where they are likely to be admitted and that you can afford, several in the middle, and a few reaches. If money is tight, ask the counselor about fee waivers; most students who qualify can apply to colleges for free.
[886] Fall of senior year. File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens for the next school year, usually by October 1. Some aid runs out. You and your child each create an account, and with your permission your tax information is brought in directly from the IRS. Some private colleges also ask for the CSS Profile. A parent without a Social Security number can still create an account and complete their part.
[887] Spring of senior year. Compare financial aid offers side by side: grants are free money, work-study is a campus job, and loans must be repaid. If an offer seems low, call the financial aid office and ask whether it can be reviewed. Colleges do adjust offers.
[888] By May 1. Most colleges ask for an enrollment decision by this date.
[889] About loans, honestly
[890] Student loans are in your child's name. For 2026–27, a first-year student can borrow up to $5,500 in federal loans, and up to $31,000 over all of college. They have lower interest rates and more protections than other loans.
[891] Parent PLUS loans are in the parent's name. For loans made on or after July 1, 2026, parents can borrow up to $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total for each child, at a fixed interest rate of 9.07% plus a fee of about 4.2%. The parent must repay them; they cannot be moved to the child later. Borrow only what you are confident you can repay before you retire.
[892] A common rule of thumb: keep your child's total borrowing below what they expect to earn in their first year of work.
[893] The cheapest offer is not always the cheapest college. A college where students finish in four years can cost less than one where they take five or six.
[894] Other paths
[895] College is not the only good route. Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college can lower the cost of a degree; check in advance that credits will transfer. Apprenticeships and trade programs lead to skilled work, often with pay while training. Military service can help pay for college later. The questions on this site apply to any college your child considers, on any of these paths.
[896] What "liberal arts" means. You will see "liberal arts" on this site. It does not mean art, and it has nothing to do with politics. It means an education that includes study across many subjects, sciences included, alongside a major. Most American bachelor's degrees include some of it. Understanding College explains more, and it is worthwhile to take a little time to understand the differences among colleges and the experiences they offer that will have a positive impact on your child’s adult life.

[933] Result: You're looking for security
[934] You want your child to be able to support themselves, and you do not want debt without a payoff. That is a responsible place to start.
[935] What the research says to you. In the study, college major was unrelated to longer-term success, and the head start a specialized degree gave for the first job faded. What paid off over the longer term was breadth of study. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families.
[936] That does not mean having to avoid a practical major. Your child can still choose nursing, business or engineering, though their requirements may leave less room for courses outside the major. The point is to take courses outside the major as well.
[937] How this was measured: graduates reported their current household income, which the study grouped into higher and lower levels.
[938] Ask the college
[939] How much room do students have to take courses outside the major and still finish in four years?
[940] What share of students complete an internship or a long project, and is there funding for students who cannot work unpaid?
[941] Where are graduates ten years out, not merely six months out?
[942] What is the four-year graduation rate for students like mine (for example, high school or standardized test record; students who receive Pell Grants; first in their family to attend college; etc.)?

[944] Which two subjects outside your likely major are you curious about?
[945] Who at this college would you go to for advice?
[946] Other research agrees. Gallup-Purdue's survey of 30,000 graduates found that an internship that used classroom learning, and a project lasting a semester or more, were tied to stronger engagement at work.
[947] Try this one thing. Call or email the career office and ask for the story of one graduate whose career changed direction, and what helped them.
[948] Result: You're looking for growth
[949] You want your child to come out able to think, learn and handle change. The research is specific about what builds that.
[950] What the research says to you. Graduates who studied broadly were 29% more likely to keep learning actively as adults. Graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely, and those who wrote papers in most of their classes were 32% more likely.
[951] How this was measured: continued learning means continuing to read, study and learn as an adult.
[952] Ask the college
[953] In a typical first-year course, how much do students write, and do they get feedback they must respond to?
[954] Can first-year students take a seminar of twenty or fewer?
[955] How do you know students are learning to think, beyond grades?
[956] How often do students change majors, and what does it cost them in time?
[957] Questions to leave with your child
[958] What is one thing you believe now that you would like a class to test?
[959] What would you study if jobs were not a concern?
[960] Other research agrees. Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini reviewed decades of studies on how college affects students and concluded that what a student does in college, and how much effort they put in, matters more than which college they attend.
[961] Try this one thing. Encourage your child to sit in on an ordinary class, in person or online if the college offers it, and count how many students speak.
[962] Result: You're looking for purpose
[963] You want your child to find a direction in life, people and work that matter to them. The research treats this as a real outcome, and it can be looked for.
[964] What the research says to you. Graduates who discussed philosophical and ethical questions in most of their classes were 59% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Where most professors knew the student's first name, they were 67% more likely. With a college mentor whose influence lasted, they were 34% more likely.
[965] How this was measured: fulfillment means describing one's life as meaningful and satisfying.
[966] Ask the college
[967] How many professors will know my child by name by the end of the first year?
[968] Does advising cover goals and direction, or only course requirements?
[969] Where in the curriculum do students discuss ethics, meaning and hard human choices?
[970] How do students from different backgrounds end up in real conversation with each other?
[971] Questions to leave with your child
[972] Which adult at your school now knows you best? What would the college version of that person be?
[973] What kind of person do you want to be at 30?
[974] Other research agrees. Gallup-Purdue found that when graduates had a professor who cared about them as a person, made them excited about learning, and encouraged their goals, their odds of thriving later in life more than doubled.
[975] Try this one thing. Ask a current student, on a visit or by email through the admissions office, to describe one adult on campus who really knows them. Listen for a specific story.

[1072] The five practice cards (shown under every result)
[1073] These practices have a significant relationship to important life outcomes for all students, and they matter even more for students with the least. In the study, several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families. George Kuh's research on high-impact practices found the same pattern: underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students benefited most from practices like these, and were the least likely to take part. Vincent Tinto's research ties students' connection with faculty to whether they stay and finish.
[1074] 1. Know their names
[1075] Why it matters: graduates whose professors mostly knew their first name were 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Knowing students by name was also linked to adult civic involvement.
[1076] Ask yourself: how many names do I know by week four?
[1077] Small class: use name cards or a seating chart for the first month.
[1078] Large course: use the photo roster, learn the names of students who speak, and ask teaching assistants to learn their sections. Use a name every time you call on someone.
[1079] Students who work or commute: arrive five minutes early and greet students by name as they come in.
[1080] Part-time faculty: names cost nothing and need no office. This is the most portable of the five.
[1081] 2. Talk with students outside class, and not only about the course
[1082] Why it matters: graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Graduates who often talked with professors outside class about non-academic subjects were 37% more likely to volunteer more as adults. Alexander Astin's study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found student-faculty interaction to be among the strongest influences on students' development.
[1083] Ask yourself: which students have I spoken with one-to-one this term?
[1084] Small class: require one ten-minute office-hours visit from every student in the first three weeks.
[1085] Large course: hold small-group office hours for eight to ten students at a time, and ask teaching assistants or learning assistants to do the same.
[1086] Students who work or commute: offer video office hours, early-morning or evening times, and the five minutes after class. When a student asks about the course, ask one question about their plans.
[1087] Part-time faculty: use a short video meeting or the minutes after class, and ask the department for a place to meet students. Whether part-time faculty have the time and space for this is an institutional question; see the College Leaders checklist.
[1088] 3. Make class time active
[1089] Why it matters: graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively as adults, and 37% more likely to stay closely involved in arts, ideas and other cultures. In science, engineering and mathematics, a review of 225 studies found that students in traditional lectures were about one and a half times as likely to fail as students in classes that used active learning.
[1090] Ask yourself: who did most of the talking in my last class?
[1091] Small class: open each class with a question, and take answers before you give yours.
[1092] Large course: pose a question, have students answer, discuss it with a neighbor and answer again. Richard Light's work with Harvard faculty popularized the one-minute paper at the end of class as a simple way to hear from everyone.
[1093] Students who work or commute: build the reading into class. Read a short passage together, then discuss it, rather than assuming it was done at home.
[1094] Part-time faculty: all of these work in any room, with no extra preparation beyond the question.
[1095] 4. Give assignments with no single right answer
[1096] Why it matters: graduates whose coursework often involved questions with no single right answer were 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, and 25% more likely to keep learning actively. Being required to write papers in most classes showed similar links.
[1097] Ask yourself: which of my assignments could two good students answer differently?
[1098] Humanities and social sciences: replace one quiz with a short paper that takes a position.
[1099] Science, math and engineering: set a design problem, or ask students to explain why their approach is better than another one that also works.
[1100] Professional programs: use a case with competing goods. In nursing, a patient's wishes against a protocol. In education, a classroom dilemma. In business or accounting, a judgment call in reporting.
[1101] 5. Ask students to examine their own thinking
[1102] Why it matters: graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled: about two and a half times as likely. This was the largest difference in that part of the study.
[1103] Ask yourself: do my students ever have to argue against themselves?
[1104] Any course: add one line to every assignment: "State the strongest objection to your answer."
[1105] Science, math and engineering: "Where could this calculation or design go wrong?"
[1106] Professional programs: "What would a skeptical colleague say about this decision?"
[1107] Bring the big questions in, whatever you teach. Graduates who discussed philosophical and ethical questions in most of their classes, regardless of major, were 59% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. This is about what happens in a chemistry, nursing or accounting course as much as a humanities one. In an education course, ask what school is for. In nursing, ask who should decide.
[1108] Who carries this work. Mentoring, advising and conversations outside class take time, and that time is not shared evenly. Studies of faculty workload find that women faculty do more of this kind of service than men, and faculty of color often describe extra mentoring and service demands, sometimes called "cultural taxation." The work is rarely counted in review. If these practices matter, departments and institutions need to recognize, share and reward them. The College Leaders checklist includes rewarding this work.

[1190] A meaningful life in a world of change (shown under every result)
[1191] Most professors did not choose this work only to cover content. They chose it to make a difference in students' lives, and the evidence says they do. Gallup found that graduates who had a professor who cared about them, made them excited about learning and encouraged their hopes were more than twice as likely to be engaged at work and to be thriving in their lives. In this study, the same kind of teaching was linked, decades later, to graduates who led, volunteered, kept learning and found their lives fulfilling. Much of what any course covers will change within your students' lifetimes: Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. What your teaching builds in them will not: the ability to learn, to think, to work with people unlike themselves, and to contribute to the common good.

[1263] The retention case (shown under every result)
[1264] The experiences linked to graduates' lives are also linked to whether students stay. Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to persist when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of the institution. George Kuh found that first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research, service learning and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year, with the largest benefits for underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students. Every student who stays is a student who can graduate, and tuition the institution does not have to replace.
[1265] Other research (shown under every result)
[1266] Alexander Astin studied about 25,000 students at 309 institutions. He found the peer group to be the single most powerful influence on students' development, with interaction with faculty next, and found that a heavy institutional emphasis on research can be associated with lower student satisfaction and weaker development.
[1267] Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, reviewing decades of research, concluded that differences between colleges explain less than what students do once enrolled. Engagement is the lever.
[1268] Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to stay when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of the institution. The experiences that are linked to good lives later are also the ones that keep students enrolled now.
[1269] Gallup-Purdue found that only 3% of graduates strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.
[1270] It has been done (shown under every result)
[1271] Heading: It has been done, at every kind of institution
[1272] George Kuh and colleagues studied 20 colleges and universities whose graduation rates and student engagement were higher than predicted for the students they enrolled. They included research universities, regional universities, liberal arts colleges and historically Black universities. Three examples from their study:
[1273] A liberal arts college: Ursinus College. Every first-year student took a two-semester Common Intellectual Experience course. Students lived near classmates from their section, some classes met in the residence halls, and advising was shared by teams of faculty and staff.
[1274] A research university: the University of Kansas. The university made undergraduate teaching a visible priority, with awards and rewards for good teaching, faculty "ambassadors" linked to its teaching center, and most undergraduate courses were kept at 30 students or fewer.
[1275] A small college: Alverno College. Student affairs staff worked as partners in learning, with outcomes for life outside class defined to match the abilities the college expects every graduate to develop.
[1276] Other institutions in the study included the University of Michigan, George Mason University, the University of Texas at El Paso, Fayetteville State University and Winston-Salem State University. What the twenty shared was not wealth or selectivity but six conditions: a mission that is lived, not only stated; an unshakeable focus on student learning; clearly marked pathways to success; a habit of continuous improvement; campus settings adapted for learning; and shared responsibility for educational quality.
[1277] These examples describe the institutions in the early 2000s, when those studies were done.

[1429] A meaningful life in a world of change (shown under every result)
[1430] Nearly every college mission statement promises more than employment: leaders, citizens, lifelong learners, people who live with purpose. That is the oldest promise of American higher education, an education for the common good that serves both the graduate and society. Public confidence in that promise is fragile. In 2026, 38% of Americans said they had a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in higher education, down from 42% a year earlier. This study gives you evidence to keep the promise and to show it: the experiences linked to graduates' income are the same ones linked to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning and fulfillment. Your graduates will live through constant change: Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. An institution that aligns its mission, budget, promotion criteria and reporting with those experiences prepares graduates for a whole life, and gives the public a reason to trust it again.

[1442] Landing
[1443] Label: For legislators and policymakers
[1444] Heading: How should government measure what a college is worth?
[1445] State and federal accountability systems count enrollment, completion and earnings in the first years after college. Those measures set a floor: no student, and no taxpayer, should pay for a program that leaves graduates worse off. But a floor is not a ceiling. Interviews with 1,000 graduates found that what students experienced in college is linked to how their lives went ten, twenty and forty years later, including their income, leadership, civic involvement, and continued learning.
[1446] Three things worth knowing
[1447] Major matters only in the short term. The educational experience matters in both the short and the long term. Specialized preparation helped graduates get their first jobs, and that advantage faded. Over a career, major was unrelated to success in most cases. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults.
[1448] The students who benefit most get these experiences least. Among graduates from lower-income families, the same experience was linked to a 33% greater likelihood of higher income. Other research finds that low-income, first-generation and underrepresented students gain the most from close contact with faculty and similar practices, and are the least likely to have it.
[1449] Most of what matters most for life impact is not expensive. The experiences in question are professors who know students, a mentor, room for courses outside the major and involvement in campus life. They cost faculty time and planning more than buildings, and they are also linked to students finishing college, which governments already pay for.

[1479] Result: Workforce first
[1480] You want public money to lead to jobs. Constituents expect that, and it is a fair test.
[1481] What the research says to you. Major matters for the first job. Starting salaries differ widely by field, and in this study specialized preparation helped graduates get started. The advantage faded. Over a career, major was unrelated to success in most cases. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families. Career preparation and broad study are not rivals. The strongest combination is a career-ready major with plenty of room left for courses outside it.

[1484] Do our funding formulas reward programs for early outcomes only?
[1485] When credit requirements have been reduced, do students have room for courses outside their major?

[1488] Result: Civic first
[1489] You see colleges as part of what holds a community together. The study measured that directly.
[1490] What the research says to you. Graduates were asked how much they give, how much they volunteer and whether they vote. Graduates who were closely involved with faculty and other students outside class were 56% more likely to give a larger share of their income and 42% more likely to volunteer more time. Voting was not linked to these experiences; giving and volunteering were. What graduates majored in made little difference.
[1491] The study also asked how often graduates had talked in college with people whose values, beliefs and backgrounds differed from their own. Those who often did were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults and 38% more likely to keep learning actively. These are the habits civic education aims to build: reasoning, civil disagreement and working with people who see things differently.

[1498] Result: Resilience first
[1499] You are thinking about the economy your state and the country will have in twenty years, not this year's openings. The study speaks most directly to this view.
[1500] What the research says to you. The study interviewed graduates about ten, twenty and forty years out of college. Breadth of study, intellectual challenge, and involvement with faculty and other students were linked to continued learning, to leadership, and to success over time. For example, graduates whose studies spanned many fields were 29% more likely to keep learning actively as adults. The book argues that first-job income, now widely used as a sign of quality, should be supplemented by indicators of longer-term value to the individual and to society.

[1507] The completion case (shown under every result)
[1508] Governments already pay for completion. The experiences linked to long-term outcomes are linked to finishing college too. Vincent Tinto's research found that students who are connected to faculty and to other students are more likely to stay. George Kuh found that first-year seminars, writing-intensive courses, undergraduate research and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year, and that low-income, first-generation and underrepresented students benefited most but were the least likely to take part. Every student who stays protects the tuition, state aid and federal aid already spent on them.
[1509] What the study covers, and what it does not (shown under every result)
[1510] Who was interviewed. 1,000 people with bachelor's degrees, ages 25 to 65, interviewed once by telephone. Six hundred came from a nationally representative sample of graduates; 400 more came from the alumni of twelve liberal arts colleges, added so that graduates with many of these experiences would be well represented. The findings held within both groups.
[1511] Different kinds of students. The links held across gender, race and ethnicity, entrance test scores and family income. Graduates of color were 11% of the sample, so findings for specific groups are less precise.
[1512] Who is not covered. The study did not include students who left college without a degree, students who attended only a community college, or adults who returned to finish a degree later. For those students, research on persistence (Tinto) and on high-impact practices (Kuh), and community college engagement surveys, are better guides.
[1513] Cause. The study shows that these experiences and outcomes go together, and other research affirms these findings. The Research page explains the checks the book made.
[1514] Other research (shown under every result)
[1515] Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, reviewing decades of research on college impact, concluded that what students do in college matters more than which college they attend. Measures of student engagement tell a state more than rankings or selectivity.
[1516] Vincent Tinto's research on why students leave found that students who are connected to faculty and peers are more likely to finish.
[1517] Alexander Astin's study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found that student involvement, with peers and with faculty, drove development more than institutional type or resources.
[1518] Gallup and Purdue University's survey of 30,000 graduates concluded that what students did in college, and how they experienced it, related more strongly to their later work and well-being than where they went.
[1519] In Washington (shown under every result)
[1520] The federal floor. Under the 2025 federal budget law, a program loses eligibility for federal Direct Loans if, in two of three years, its graduates' median earnings four years after completion are below those of working adults aged 25 to 34 with only a high school diploma. The Department of Education will first calculate the test in early 2027. But it measures only the first years of a working life.
[1521] Four ways to add the long view
[1522] Data that follows graduates longer. Federal law bars the Department of Education from keeping a database of individual student records, so federal earnings data come mostly from the first years after college. The College Transparency Act, introduced in the Senate in July 2025 with sponsors from both parties, would create a secure student-level data system that could report outcomes over longer periods.
[1523] Fund the experiences. Federal Work-Study pays for campus jobs that put students beside the same people every week. TRIO programs and the McNair Scholars Program pay for mentoring and undergraduate research for low-income and first-generation students. These are the experiences the research links to long-term outcomes.
[1524] Answer the cause question. This study could not prove cause; a study that follows students from entry can. Federal surveys such as Baccalaureate and Beyond already follow graduates for about ten years. Adding questions about faculty contact, breadth of study and civic life would let them test these findings.
[1525] Count what students experience. Ask that federal reporting or accreditation reviews include a measure of student engagement beside earnings and completion.

[1595] Search description: How American colleges differ, where the liberal arts idea came from, why rankings and prestige are weak guides, and how to choose by evidence instead.
[1596] Opening
[1597] Label: Understanding college
[1598] Heading: Four thousand colleges. One idea underneath almost all of them.
[1599] American colleges look wildly different from one another: a community college down the road, a state university with 40,000 students, a college of 1,500 on a hill. Underneath, most of them were built on the same idea about what an education is for. Knowing that idea, and knowing which colleges still practice it, is the most useful thing a person can learn before choosing one, working at one, or funding one.
[1600] The kinds of colleges
[1601] In plain terms: Colleges are built for different jobs. The label tells you what the place was designed to do, though of course they do vary. It does not tell you what a student will experience there.
[1602] Kind
[1603] What it is
[1604] Built mainly to
[1605] Worth knowing
[1606] Community college
[1607] Two-year public college, open to nearly everyone, low cost
[1608] Start a degree, train for a local career, or transfer to a four-year college
[1609] Small classes are common. Most students commute and many work, so life outside class takes effort to find.
[1610] Liberal arts college
[1611] Smaller four-year college, usually residential, primarily undergraduate
[1612] Teach undergraduates broadly, in close contact with professors
[1613] Fewer majors than a university. Emphasis on the total educational community. Professors are rewarded for the quality of their teaching and out-of-class involvement with students; a moderate emphasis on research.
[1614] Regional or comprehensive university
[1615] Mid-sized public or private university with bachelor's and master's programs
[1616] Educate the region's teachers, nurses, managers and other professionals
[1617] Generally teaching-centered, often with more working and transfer students. Classes may be smaller, so professors can get to know students. Because many students work and commute, experiences outside class take deliberate planning by the university and by students.
[1618] Research university
[1619] Large university with doctoral programs, laboratories and professional schools
[1620] Produce new knowledge as well as teach
[1621] Many courses and majors. Professors balance teaching with research and graduate training, and large introductory courses often use teaching assistants. Honors colleges and smaller programs can offer a close community inside the large university.
[1622] Specialized college
[1623] Art, music, engineering, health sciences, business, religious study, military academies
[1624] Prepare students deeply for one field
[1625] The choice of major is largely made before arriving.
[1626] Career and for-profit college
[1627] Programs aimed at a specific job, often short and often online
[1628] Deliver a credential quickly
[1629] Outcomes vary widely. Check completion rates and debt.
[1630] 
[1631] Other labels cut across these kinds: public or private, religious or secular, historically Black colleges and universities, tribal colleges, women's colleges, online universities. An historically Black college can be a liberal arts college or a research university.
[1632] The point that matters: the experiences linked to good lives after college can be found at every kind of institution. The odds of finding them without having to hunt are not the same everywhere: at liberal arts colleges these experiences are usually built into how the college works, and in the study liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. At larger or more complex institutions they are often available too, but students may need to seek them out through honors programs, knocking on professors’ doors, and pursuing involvement in cocurricular programs.
[1633] One idea, refined over millennia and nearly four centuries old in America
[1634] In plain terms: The American college was never designed as job training. It was designed to prepare people to think, to take part in public life and to keep learning as the needs of the individual and of humanity change over time. That design is called the liberal arts, an education for the common good which benefits both the individual and society, and it is the root of almost every American bachelor's degree.
[1635] Ancient roots. Liberal arts education began in Greece about 3,500 years ago and evolved through Rome, North Africa and the Middle East, and Europe. Its purpose was to educate people for the common good: to benefit both the individual and important needs of society. Over that long span the subjects studied steadily grew, but the approach always involved a close relationship between teachers and students.
[1636] 1636 to 1769: the first American colleges. Nine colleges were founded in colonial America, beginning with Harvard. Their founders brought the model of Oxford and Cambridge: broad study, in a community where students and teachers lived and learned together.
[1637] 1810: a different model appears. Prussia created a new kind of university to serve the state. Students chose a specialty early and trained for it. The model spread across Europe and, through its empires, most of the world. It is why students in many countries still apply to study one subject from the first day.
[1638] 1828: America decides. Yale's faculty asked whether American colleges should copy the Prussian universities. Their answer, known as the Yale Report, was no. A republic like the United States, they argued, needs widely educated citizens, not a trained or specialized few:
[1639] "Our republican form of government renders it highly important that great numbers should enjoy the advantage of a thorough education.… Merchants, manufacturers, and farmers, as well as professional gentlemen, take their places in our public councils. A thorough education ought therefore to be extended to all these classes."
[1640] This "thorough education" was to include the full range of human knowledge, and it was to happen in a learning community where contact between students and professors was "frequent and familiar." Their report became the template for the hundreds of colleges founded as the country moved west.
[1641] 1862 onward: the model stretches. The Morrill Acts created the land-grant universities to advance agriculture and engineering, and still required "liberal and practical education." Johns Hopkins, founded in the 1870s, brought research and doctoral study to America, and placed them after an undergraduate liberal arts degree, not in place of it.
[1642] The result is something close to unique in the world: an education that comes before a person specializes or starts a profession. In the United States a future engineer, nurse or accountant was still expected to spend part of a bachelor's degree on writing, history, science and ideas outside the field. Unlike in most of the world, an aspiring physician or lawyer had to complete an undergraduate course of study before beginning professional education. General education requirements at a comprehensive or research university are the liberal arts tradition, surviving under another name.
[1643] What "liberal arts" means, and what it doesn't
[1644] In plain terms: "Liberal arts" is not a list of majors. It is a way of being educated: what you study, and how.
[1645] Three common mistakes
[1646] "Liberal arts means the humanities." It never did. The tradition has always included mathematics and the sciences. A physics major at a liberal arts college is getting a liberal arts education.
[1647] "Liberal arts means an education for nothing in particular." Liberal arts means an education not tied to one job, which is a different thing. In the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.
[1648] "Liberal arts has something to do with politics." "Liberal" here comes from the Latin for "free." It meant the education suited to a free person who must make their own judgments.

[1672] And one purpose runs through all six: the education should serve the common good. That is, it benefits both the individual and society.
[1673] The research behind this site found that the second half of that table, the context, was the most consistently linked to how graduates' lives turned out. A college can list every subject in the catalog and still not deliver an education that has a long-lasting impact on graduates' lives.
[1674] The pressure to make college pay off fast
[1675] In plain terms: College is expensive, so people reasonably ask what it pays. The trouble starts when the only answer anyone measures is the paycheck.
[1676] Families are encouraged to compare starting salaries by major. Rankings count earnings soon after graduation. Lawmakers tie funding to job placement. Colleges respond the way any organization responds to what they are measured on: they add narrow career programs, enlarge majors until there is little room for anything else, and trim the parts of college that are hard to count, such as student time with faculty.
[1677] Each step is understandable. Together they move American colleges toward the model the Yale faculty turned down in 1828.
[1678] The evidence suggests the trade is a poor one. In the study behind this site, specialized preparation helped with the first job, but that advantage faded. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher incomes later. Richard Detweiler has put the point this way: "Strengthening graduates' readiness for the job market is not only appropriate, it is essential. But it is a floor, not a ceiling."
[1679] I asked a group of students to describe the person they hoped to be ten years from now. One answered quietly: "I want to be someone my community can count on. Someone who gives back what was given to me." Around the table, nearly 20 students offered their own versions of the same answer, and not one mentioned salary, status or job title.
[1680] — Richard A. Detweiler, "What Students Know About College That Critics Don't," Inside Higher Ed, September 2, 2026
[1681] The case for the liberal arts college
[1682] In plain terms: The experiences that matter can happen anywhere. At a liberal arts college, they are the standard, not the exception.
[1683] The study behind this site interviewed graduates of every kind of institution. The experiences linked to leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and success were linked to them everywhere. A student at a large university who seeks out professors, studies widely and gets involved can have all of them.
[1684] The difference is in the odds:
[1685] Twice as likely. In the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide the six experiences that have a long-term impact on graduates' lives.
[1686] 45% against 24%. In a Gallup-Strada national study, 45% of graduates of colleges with fewer than 5,000 students said "my professors care about me as a person." At larger institutions, only 24% did.
[1687] 3%. In the Gallup-Purdue study of 30,000 graduates, only 3% strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.
[1688] The reasons are structural. A liberal arts college doesn't have graduate students competing for faculty time. Professors are rewarded for teaching and advising. The scale is small enough that students and faculty cannot avoid one another. As the book puts it:
[1689] "...the odds of having these experiences with a long-term life impact is far higher at the smaller liberal arts college."
[1690] For a student who will persistently knock on doors and ask, a large university can deliver the same educational experiences. But that is hard for most young people; it is time-consuming and invariably intimidating. For the many who will not, a place where it happens without asking is a real advantage.
[1691] What employers say
[1692] In plain terms: Employers keep asking for the same things: people who can communicate, solve problems, think critically and adapt. Those are the abilities a broad education builds, and as AI takes over routine technical work, employers expect them to matter more.
[1693] Communication comes first. In the 2026 Corporate Recruiters Survey of more than 600 recruiters of business-school graduates in 39 countries, the skills employers weighed most in hiring were communication (64%), problem-solving (62%) and adaptability (60%). Graduates were least prepared in AI skills, grit, managing people and emotional intelligence.
[1694] The widest gaps are in thinking and communicating. The QS Global Employer Survey 2026 found that the largest gaps between what employers need and what graduates bring were in problem solving, communication and critical thinking. QS's summary of its employer data puts it plainly: graduates "struggle most with critical thinking and judgement, not subject knowledge."
[1695] Analytical thinking is the core skill. In the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on more than 1,000 employers representing over 14 million workers, seven in ten employers called analytical thinking essential. Technology skills such as AI and big data are expected to grow fastest by 2030, but creative thinking, resilience and flexibility, and curiosity and lifelong learning are also expected to rise in importance. Employers expect 39% of workers' existing skills to be transformed or become outdated in the same period.
[1696] Human skills are hardest to automate. The Forum's New Economy Skills report found that tasks drawing on empathy, creativity, leadership and curiosity have only about a 13% potential for transformation by AI. Fewer than half of executives believed schools develop creativity, curiosity or resilience well.
[1697] Breadth counts. In a 2023 survey of 1,010 executives and hiring managers, nearly nine in ten agreed that exposure to a wide range of topics and viewpoints is an important contributor to workforce preparedness. In an earlier survey cited in the book, 80% said every student should acquire broad knowledge in the liberal arts and sciences, whatever the major. And 93% agreed that a candidate's capacity to think critically, communicate clearly and solve complex problems matters more than the undergraduate major.
[1698] The gap is in "soft" skills. In a 2025 survey of 651 company vice presidents and directors in the United States and Britain, 56% blamed weak soft skills for entry-level workers being unprepared, up from 50% a year earlier; among U.S. leaders it was 58%. The National Association of Colleges and Employers found the same gap: about 80% of graduating seniors rated themselves proficient in communication and critical thinking, but only about 54% to 56% of employers rated new graduates proficient.

[1716] Why prestige and rankings are a poor guide
[1717] In plain terms: A famous name opens some doors at the start. For most graduates it changes little after that. And the rankings that measure fame are measuring fame.
[1718] What prestige does do
[1719] It may open early doors. A well-known name on a first résumé may get attention for jobs or graduate school.
[1720] It matters in a few fields. The most selective law firms, investment banks and consulting firms recruit heavily from a short list of colleges.
[1721] It feels good. It's nice to have bragging rights, especially for parents.
[1722] What prestige does not do
[1723] It is not where most leaders come from. In 2023, 88% of Fortune 100 chief executives had not attended an Ivy League college as undergraduates.
[1724] Its advantage is early and narrow. The most cited recent study of elite colleges is by economists Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman (2023). It found that about ten years after graduation, students who attended an Ivy-Plus college were more likely to reach the top 1% of earners, since they were more likely to have positions in high-paying fields such as prestigious law or consulting. However, across all graduates, the effect on average earnings was small and not statistically significant. Studies that follow graduates longer find no lasting advantage. Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger tracked earnings for more than 20 years. They found that the effect of attending a more selective college fell to close to zero once students' own abilities and ambitions were accounted for, though it remained for Black and Hispanic students. Jack Mountjoy and Brent Hickman found that a selective college's earnings premium faded to zero after a few years in the workforce. In the study behind this site, what graduates experienced in college, not where they went, was linked not only to a higher long-term income but, importantly, to how their lives turned out more broadly.
[1725] It does not predict a good life. Gallup-Purdue found little to no difference in well-being or engagement at work between graduates of highly ranked colleges and everyone else. A Stanford-affiliated review, A Fit Over Rankings (2018), concluded that selectivity is not a reliable predictor of learning, job satisfaction or well-being.
[1726] What the rankings measure
[1727] Reputation is built in. In the U.S. News formula, a fifth of a college's score has come from a survey asking presidents, provosts and admissions deans to rate other colleges, most of which they have never visited.
[1728] The data can be wrong. In 2022 Columbia fell from 2nd to 18th after one of its own professors showed that figures it had submitted were inaccurate. Since then, leading law and medical schools, including Yale and Harvard, have stopped taking part.
[1729] They leave out what matters. No major ranking measures whether professors know students, how much students write, or how graduates are living at 45.
[1730] Sadly, three-quarters of college-bound students believe rankings give useful information about colleges. They are being told who is famous, by the famous.
[1731] What does drive long-term outcomes
[1732] In plain terms: How a student goes to college matters more than where.
[1733] Three large bodies of research point the same way:
[1734] The study behind this site. Among 1,000 graduates aged 25 to 65, involvement with faculty and other students outside class, broad study, and teaching that demanded thought were linked to all six adult outcomes. Major was not.
[1735] Gallup-Purdue. Among nearly 30,000 graduates, those who had a professor who cared about them, made them excited about learning and encouraged their goals more than doubled their odds of being engaged at work. Those who had an internship that used their learning, a long project and deep involvement in activities doubled their odds as well. Where they went to college made little difference.
[1736] Fifty years of scholarship. Alexander Astin, Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, Richard Light and Vincent Tinto, studying different students with different methods, each concluded that what students do in college, and with whom, outweighs the type or standing of the institution.
[1737] And this matters most for students with the least. In the study behind this site, the link between these relationships and later success was strongest for graduates who grew up in lower-income families.
[1738] On "fit." Fit is often described as a feeling on a campus tour. A better definition: a college fits when the student is likely to have the educational experiences that are related to their long-term life goals.
[1739] Five things most people get wrong
[1740] "The more selective, the better the education." Selectivity measures who gets in. It says nothing about what happens next.
[1741] "Pick the major that pays." The majors with the highest starting salaries change from decade to decade, and in the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.
[1742] "A big university has more opportunity." It has more offers. Whether a given student gets any, or whether having a huge buffet of choices is actually beneficial to the student, is a different question.
[1743] "The sticker price is the price." Most students pay less than the published tuition, sometimes far less. The figure to compare is the net price after grants and scholarships, and colleges are required to provide a calculator for it.
[1744] "Liberal arts graduates can't get jobs." Employers say the opposite, and the long-term income evidence favors breadth.
[1745] How to read any college in ten minutes
[1746] Whatever kind of college it is, five questions get past the brochure:
[1747] How many professors will know a first-year student by the end of the year?
[1748] How much of the degree can be taken outside the major?
[1749] In a typical class, who does the talking?
[1750] What share of students do research, an internship or a long project, and is there money for those who cannot work unpaid?
[1751] What share of students like this one finish, and what does it cost them after aid?

[1753] Closing
[1754] Heading: Start at the end.
[1755] Most people choose a college, or judge one, or fund one, by starting with what is easiest to see: the name, the rank, the price, the first salary. The evidence says to start at the other end.
[1756] First, decide what the education is for. A life with leadership in it, involvement in a community, curiosity that lasts, work that matters and the means to live well.
[1757] Second, ask what the evidence says produces that. Professors and students involved with one another outside class. Study across many fields. Teaching that makes students think.
[1758] Third, look for the colleges where that happens, and only then choose.
[1759] That is what college is really for.

[1764] Opening
[1765] Heading: Where this comes from
[1766] Everything on this site starts from one study, reported in Richard A. Detweiler's The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021). Here is how it was done and what it can and cannot tell you.
[1767] How the study was done
[1768] Who: 1,000 college graduates from every kind of American college and university.
[1769] Ages: three groups, about ten, twenty and forty years after graduation: 25 to 35, 36 to 45, and 55 to 65.
[1770] How: individual telephone interviews by trained interviewers, using about 100 questions.
[1771] What was asked: what people did and experienced in college, and how they live now. The questions were about behavior, not opinions about whether college helped.
[1772] Study details. The research was carried out by Art & Science Group, an education research and consulting firm in Ellicott City, Maryland, which developed and pilot-tested the interview questions, drew the sample, and managed the interviews and the coding of answers. It was funded by the Teagle Foundation (New York) and the Spencer Foundation (Chicago), with additional support from the Great Lakes Colleges Association (Ann Arbor, Michigan).
[1773] How graduates were chosen. Six hundred graduates were randomly selected from a nationally representative panel of college graduates in the right age groups. The panel was used so that graduates of every type of institution would be included: public and private, large and small, liberal arts, comprehensive and research, selective and non-selective. Because only a small share of graduates nationally have had many of the experiences the study examined, 400 more were randomly selected from the 84,000 living alumni of twelve liberal arts colleges. This widened the range of experiences studied. It was not done to compare types of colleges, and the findings held when the two groups were analyzed separately.
[1774] Who interviewed them. Trained professional interviewers, by telephone, using a structured interview that had been pilot-tested and refined.
[1775] How the results were analyzed. The principal analyses did not compare types of institutions. They compared graduates by their educational experiences, wherever they went to college.
[1776] Where the graduates went to college. 46% graduated from bachelor's colleges, 16% from master's universities and 33% from doctoral universities. 35% attended public institutions and 65% private ones. Enrollment ranged from under 1,500 students (13%) to over 20,000 (14%). 27% attended the most selective institutions, 58% moderately selective ones and 15% non-selective ones. Average entrance test scores ranged from under 1100 on the SAT, or the ACT equivalent (27%), to over 1510 (7.5%).
[1777] Who they were. 58% women and 42% men; 89% white and 11% people of color.
[1778] Full details are in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, chapter 5 (research design and analyses) and appendixes 2, 3 and 4.
[1779] About the author. The author is a graduate of a liberal arts college and of a major research university. He led liberal arts organizations for many years. The study included graduates of every kind of college—big and small, private and public, selective and non-selective, research and liberal arts and comprehensive, regional and national—and its findings held across all of them.
[1780] How the analysis worked. For each of the six kinds of college experience, the study combined graduates' answers to a set of related questions into one indicator, and divided graduates into those with lower and higher levels of that experience. Adult outcomes were divided the same way. The study then statistically compared how often each group reported each outcome. Where the overall difference for a kind of experience was statistically significant, the study went on to examine the individual questions within it, such as having a mentor or talking with professors outside class. The analyses were repeated within groups of graduates defined by gender, race and ethnicity, entrance test scores and family background, including whether they were the first in their family to attend college, to check whether the links held for different kinds of students. A link is reported as substantial only when it was statistically significant and the difference between the groups was at least 20%.

[1828] What it found
[1829] How to read these numbers. A figure such as "24% more likely" compares two groups. For example, if 57% of one group reports a higher income, compared with 46% of the other group, the first group's rate is 124% of the second group's (57 divided by 46). Statisticians call this the incident risk ratio, and the increase expressed as a percentage (24%) is called the percent relative effect. All reported effects are statistically significant at the p<.05 level or better; most are significant at the p<.001 level.
[1830] For leadership and civic involvement, the educational setting mattered most. Graduates who had a mentor or talked often with faculty outside class were 21 to 26% more likely to be leaders. Those closely involved with faculty and other students outside class were 56% more likely to give a larger share of income and 42% more likely to volunteer more. What they studied made no substantial difference. Voting was not linked to these experiences; giving and volunteering were.
[1831] For continued learning and cultural involvement, both the educational setting and the content of study mattered. Broad study, writing, open-ended questions, discussion-based classes and contact with different people were each linked to these outcomes, by 20 to 56%.
[1832] For fulfillment, the strongest links were to intellectual content. Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes: 59%. Being made to examine the strengths and weaknesses of one's own arguments: up to 149%.
[1833] For success, breadth mattered and major did not. More than half of courses outside the major: 24% more likely to report a higher income, and 33 to 34% for those with lower family income or lower entrance test scores.
[1834] The findings were not limited to one kind of college. They held across the types of institutions in the study wherever these experiences were strongly present.
[1835] What it cannot show
[1836] The study interviewed people once and asked them to look back. It could not do a true experiment by assigning people to colleges at random. So it shows that these experiences and these outcomes go together. It cannot prove that one causes the other. The book takes up this question of causality directly and compares the findings with other widely cited and respected research.

[1866] Why this matters now
[1867] Education in the liberal arts tradition has always aimed at the common good: lives that are good for the people living them and good for the society around them. This study shows what that looks like in practice. The experiences it examined were linked not only to graduates' income and careers but to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural lives and fulfillment. Those are the capacities people need in a world that keeps changing. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today's jobs. That is the case for these experiences: they prepare people for a meaningful and successful life, and to contribute to the lives of others.

[1911] Principles
[1912] Few and purposeful. Pictures go where a visitor decides whether to keep reading: the home page, the six audience landing pages, the opening of Understanding College and About the Author. Quizzes, results and tools have no photos, so people can concentrate on answering.
[1913] Show the finding, not the brochure. The study's central result is about relationships, so the best photos show people together: a student in a professor's doorway, a seminar mid-discussion, students working on a project. Avoid posed stock photos of smiling graduates, empty campus lawns and aerial views.
[1914] Every kind of college. Across the set, show community colleges, state universities and small colleges, and a range of students by age, race, background and ability. The site's message is that these experiences can happen anywhere.
[1915] No famous names. Avoid photos where a well-known college's name, logo or landmark is visible. The site argues that prestige is a weak guide.
[1916] Never a face beside a graduate's quotation. The book's quotations are anonymous. A photo next to one would suggest it belongs to that person.
[1917] Numbers become simple charts. On The Research and Understanding College, a few key figures are shown as plain bar charts or a timeline. Each chart shows one idea, is labeled directly, names its source underneath, and has a text version for screen readers.

[1970] Where to find photos
[1971] Colleges you know. Members of the Great Lakes Colleges Association and the Global Liberal Arts Alliance, and the colleges in the study, keep photo libraries. Ask for permission to use a few candid images, with credit.
[1972] Free photo libraries such as Unsplash, as a fallback. Choose candid images and check each license.
[1973] A half-day shoot at one or two cooperating campuses would produce the whole set in a consistent style. That is the strongest option if there is a budget.
[1974] Permissions. Anyone identifiable needs a signed photo release. For anyone under 18, which is likely in P2, a parent or guardian must sign. Keep the releases with the image files. Credit lines go in the footer or on the About page, not on the photos.

[2012] Rules for every chart
[2013] One idea per chart. Bars start at zero, and each bar is labeled with its number. There is no legend to decode and no gridlines.
[2014] Colors. Charts use the page's accent color. Where one bar is the point (as in G3), that bar is in the accent color and the comparison bar is gray.
[2015] Text alternative. Each chart is built as HTML or SVG, not a picture of a chart, so screen readers can read it. It also carries a one-sentence summary for people who cannot see it.
[2016] Readable on a phone. Labels sit above or beside the bars so nothing is squeezed at narrow widths.
[2017] Note: In the page layouts, G1 to G4 are drawn with the real figures, and every photo appears as a marked placeholder with its P number. G5 and G6 are not yet in the layouts; they can be added once you approve this plan.

[2064] Appendix B: Please verify before publishing
[2065] These statements are accurate to the best of my knowledge but were not checked against the original source with a page number. Each should be confirmed.
[2066] Richard Light. That his best-known advice to students is to get to know one faculty member reasonably well each semester, and the description of his interview research. Source: Making the Most of College (Harvard University Press, 2001).
[2067] Richard Light. That most learning from diversity happened outside the classroom. This comes from a Harvard Gazette report on his work.
[2068] Alexander Astin. The sample of 24,847 students at 309 institutions, the peer group as the most powerful influence, and the finding on institutional research emphasis were confirmed against a published summary of What Matters in College? (Felder, 1993), not the book itself.
[2069] Pascarella and Terenzini. The summary that what students do matters more than where they enroll. This is the standard reading of How College Affects Students; a page reference would strengthen it.
[2070] Vincent Tinto. The summary of academic and social integration. Note that his work concerns staying in college, not life outcomes.
[2071] Gallup-Purdue. Confirmed against Gallup's release: a sample of 29,560 graduates; odds of engagement at work "more than doubled" with supportive professors and doubled with experiential learning; 3% strongly agreed they had all six experiences.
[2072] Book page numbers. Every percentage in this document was checked against the text of the book. Adding page numbers on the Research page would let readers check them too.
[2073] Two quotations on the draft site did not turn up in a search of the book and are not used here: "The decision you're making now will shape your life long after your first job is a memory" and "…if the attribute has no impact on the way that people live their lives, then what value does it have?"
[2074] Understanding College: soft-skills gap. The 2013 Adecco figure (44%) was replaced with a 2025 General Assembly survey of 651 company leaders and NACE's 2024 comparison of student and employer ratings. General Assembly is a training company, so its survey is not independent research; NACE's figures come from a nonprofit association.
[2075] Understanding College: George Anders. The quotation summarizes the conclusion of You Can Do Anything. Reed Magazine attributes it to him, but I could not find it in his own text. Please check it against the book.
