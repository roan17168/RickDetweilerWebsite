[190] Pull-out (collapsed): How to read these numbers. A figure such as "24% more likely" compares two groups. If 57% of one group reports a higher income against 46% of another, the first group's rate is 24% higher than the second (57% divided by 46%). Statisticians call this the incident risk ratio, and the increase expressed as a percentage is the percent relative effect. The purpose of this statistic is not to predict how often an outcome will happen (if you do X, then Y will happen Z percent of the time) but to identify the experiences that make an outcome more likely. The bigger the number, the more substantial the effect. Think of it like a bet: if you were told that doing something in particular made you 24% more likely to win, would you do it? All reported effects are statistically significant. The study interviewed people once and asked them to look back, so it shows an association between experiences and outcomes, not proof of cause. However, extensive research by others supports the importance of these experiences, from neuroscience (as reported in the book) to the other studies cited on this site. Read how the study was done.
[191] This study compared graduates who had more or less of each college experience, at every kind of college; it did not compare kinds of colleges. The results held for graduates of all types of colleges, big and small, private and public, prestigious and not, liberal arts and not. The result shows a few findings; What Matters in College shows them all, and The Research page explains more.

[221] What kind of college education lasts a lifetime?
[222] It's not the major. It's not the ranking. It's what happens there.
[223] We asked 1,000 people, ten, twenty and forty years after college, about their college years and about their lives now. The ones who were leading, giving back, still curious, doing well and finding their lives worthwhile had something in common. It had less to do with where they went or what they majored in, and more to do with what happened to them there.
[224] Start at the end (beside the headline)
[225] Most people choose a college by starting with what is easiest to see: the name, the rank, the price, the first salary. Start at the other end.
[226] First, picture the life you want: is it work you enjoy, people who count on you, a mind that stays curious, enough to live well, or a sense that it all means something?
[227] Second, ask what kind of college experience leads there.
[228] Third, look for the colleges where that happens, and only then choose.

[289] For Students Choosing a College
[290] Rankings can't tell you what college will do for you.
[291] Majors, price, location, dorms. They all matter. None of them tell you who you'll be at 28, or whether you'll love your work, have people who count on you, or feel like your life means something. Start with the life you want, then find the colleges that can help you build it.
[292] Does a famous name matter? (short panel under the headline)
[293] Researchers disagree, and both sides have evidence:
[294] For most graduates, less than people think. Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger followed earnings for more than 20 years. The earnings advantage of a more selective college was small once students' own abilities were taken into account.[1]
[295] For some. Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman did find that attending an Ivy-Plus college rather than a flagship public university increases a student's chances of reaching the top 1% of earners and of working at a prestigious finance or consulting firm. Ivy-Plus graduates are also overrepresented among Fortune 500 chief executives. However, the great majority of those CEOs, 88.4%, did not attend an Ivy-Plus college, and graduating from an Ivy-Plus college had little effect on average earnings.[2] [Term: Ivy-Plus means the eight Ivy League universities plus Stanford, MIT, Duke and the University of Chicago.]
[296] What do you want college to do for you?
[297] Five questions, plus one optional. Two minutes. No sign-up, no wrong answers. You'll get your result, questions to ask colleges (including ones you can ask without visiting) and a scorecard for your list.

[303] Always visible: The price on a college's website is almost never the price you'll pay, and you can find your real price before you apply.

[305] Most students pay less than the posted price, often much less. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the posted tuition.[3] [Term: a grant is money you don't pay back.] Some colleges with high posted prices cost students from lower-income families less than a public university does.
[306] Net price calculator: Every college has one on its website. Put in your family's information and it estimates what you'd pay after grants. [Term: net price is the posted price minus grants and scholarships.] Search for the college's name and "net price calculator."
[307] College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov): From the US Department of Education. It shows what families at your income level actually pay at each college, and how many students graduate.
[308] FAFSA (studentaid.gov): The Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the free form that decides federal grants, work-study jobs and loans; most states and colleges use it for their own aid too. [Link: What is the FAFSA? at studentaid.gov] [Term: work-study is a part-time campus job paid for partly by federal aid.]

[313] 2.  Try three net price calculators: Include at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
[314] 3.  Ask about fee waivers: If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free. Your counselor can request the waivers. [Term: a fee waiver lets you skip a fee you can't afford.]

[Term: the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the free form that decides federal grants, work-study jobs and loans; most states and colleges use it for their own aid too. [316] 5.  Keep every path open: Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home. The questions on this site work for all of them.
[317] No counselor, or one with hundreds of students? You can still get help, free:
[318] A teacher, coach, librarian or family friend who went to college. Ask: "Can you help me figure out where to apply and how to pay for it?" Most people are glad to be asked.
[319] Free college advising programs. The federal TRIO programs Upward Bound and Talent Search help students from lower-income families and first-generation students prepare for and apply to college. College Advising Corps places recent college graduates as advisers in many high schools. Ask at school whether either is there, or search for "college access program" and your town.
[320] The colleges themselves. Every admissions office has a counselor assigned to your area. Email them your questions; answering them is their job.

[325] What graduates told us
[326] We talked with 1,000 people who finished college ten, twenty and forty years ago.[4] The ones who were leading things, giving back, still learning, doing well and happy with their lives weren't mostly the ones from famous colleges or "safe" majors. They were the ones who had certain kinds of experience in college:
[327] Professors who knew them. Someone noticed them, talked with them outside class, and stuck with them. Many had a mentor whose influence lasted for years.
[328] Room to explore. They took lots of classes outside their major, including ones they never expected to like.
[329] Classes that made them think. They talked, wrote and argued, and they had to question their own ideas, not only memorize.
[330] Being part of something. A team, a club, a lab, a paper, a cause. Something they showed up for every week.
[331] People unlike themselves. Friends and classmates who saw the world differently.
[332] The first and fourth of these, professors who know you and being part of something, are what the study calls an authentic learning community: a college where students and faculty are involved with one another outside class as well as in it. Of everything the study measured, it was the experience most consistently linked to every part of adult life.
[333] "I got to study the sciences while also jumping into the theater. I got to write my thesis on religion while studying abroad in the U.K. I was surrounded by an eclectic mix of peers who pushed me." — Steve, actor (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 139)
[334] These experiences happen at every kind of college. At some, they're built in; in the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. At others, especially large universities, they're there, but you have to go looking for them.
[335] What these experiences were linked to:
[336] An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were more likely to be leaders, to be civically involved, to keep learning, to be involved in the arts and culture, to feel fulfilled and, over a long career, to be successful.
[337] A mentor whose influence lasted: more likely to lead, to volunteer, to keep learning, to take part in culture and to feel fulfilled.
[338] Room to explore: more likely to earn more and, over a long career, to be successful. Major didn't predict long-term success.
[339] Classes that made them think: far more likely to feel their lives were fulfilling, and more likely to keep learning.
[340] People unlike themselves: more likely to lead and to keep learning.

[342] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers behind this
[343] An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were 25% more likely to be leaders, 42% more likely to volunteer and 56% more likely to give a larger share of their income, 31% more likely to keep learning, 40% more likely to be involved in the arts and culture, and 46% more likely to say their lives felt fulfilling. Finally, over a long career, they were also 32% more likely to say they were successful.
[344] A mentor whose influence lasted: 26% more likely to be leaders, 28% more likely to volunteer, 23% more likely to keep learning, 31% more likely to be involved in culture and 34% more likely to feel fulfilled.
[345] Room to explore (more than half of classes outside the major): 24% more likely to earn more as adults, and 53% more likely to say they were successful and 72% more likely to earn more over a long career. Major didn't predict long-term success.
[346] Classes that made them think: graduates whose professors made them question their own arguments were 149% more likely (about two and a half times as likely) to say their lives felt highly fulfilling and 37% more likely to keep learning.
[347] People unlike themselves: frequent conversations with people of different beliefs and values: 26% more likely to be leaders and 38% more likely to keep learning.

[351] Pull-out (collapsed): If money is tight, this matters even more
[352] Among graduates well into their careers who grew up in lower-income families, those who took more than half their classes outside their major were 72% more likely to say they were successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income. Those who had been part of an authentic learning community (close relationships with professors outside class, and a mentor) were 64% more likely to say they were successful.[5] So when you compare colleges, don't just ask what it costs. Ask whether you'll get these experiences there.

[354] Your college timeline: what to do, and when
[355] Shown open as six steps across the page (two rows of three on a laptop), like the parent's timeline. A printable version is in the parent checklist.
[356] 1.  Junior year: find your helpers. Talk with your school counselor, or if you can't get time with one, a teacher, coach or one of the free programs in the "First in your family" box. Take this site's quiz, and try three net price calculators, including one at a private college you assumed was too expensive.
[357] 2.  Junior and senior years: visit. Visit several colleges, in person if possible and online if not, to get a feel for the kind of college that fits you. Bring the questions from your quiz result, and ask students when a professor last talked with them outside class.
[358] 3.  Summer before senior year: make your list. Build a balanced list: colleges you're likely to get into and can afford, some good matches, and a few reaches. Ask about fee waivers if money is tight.
[359] 4.  Fall of senior year: apply and file the FAFSA. Send applications and file the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens; some aid runs out. Check whether any college also asks for the CSS Profile.
[360] 5.  Spring of senior year: compare offers. Compare financial aid offers side by side on net price (cost minus grants and scholarships), not loans. If an offer falls short, ask the aid office to review it.
[361] 6.  The final choice. Don't decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to the life you want, and ask which college is most likely to give you the experiences that make a lifelong difference.
[362] Can't visit? You can still find out
[363] Most of what you need to know, you can find from home.
[364] Virtual tours and online info sessions: most colleges have both. Ask your quiz questions in the chat.
[365] Talk to a real student: many admissions offices will connect you with one by email or video. Ask the student, not the brochure.
[366] Sit in online: some colleges let you watch a class. Ask.
[367] Read what students write: the student newspaper, club pages and student government notes show what students actually do.
[368] Fly-in programs: some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit. Search for the college's name and "fly-in program," or ask your counselor.
[369] Before you fall for a campus
[370] Dealbreakers and good signs sit side by side.
[371] Dealbreakers first:
[372] Is this a place that will help me become who I want to be, not just get a degree?
[373] Can my family and I afford it all the way to graduation, after grants and scholarships?
[374] Does it have what I might study, with room to change my mind?
[375] Do students like me finish here? [Term: the graduation rate is the share of students who finish in four years, and within six years. Ask for the rates for students like you.]
[376] Will I feel like I belong, safe and supported?
[377] Are housing, health, disability and advising services good enough for what I need?
[378] Why belonging is a dealbreaker: feeling that you belong isn't a luxury; it's essential. When you visit, in person or online, ask students: "When did you first feel at home here, and what helped?" Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen found that a one-hour exercise, in which first-year students read how older students' worries about belonging had faded, reduced the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation. A 2023 study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 colleges and universities found a similar exercise raised the share who completed their first year full-time, especially for students from groups that had historically progressed at lower rates, and most at colleges that gave students real opportunities to belong.[6, 7]
[379] Good signs at any college:
[380] Professors know students and talk with them outside class.
[381] In class, students talk, write, argue and solve problems.
[382] You have room to take classes outside your major.
[383] Students spend real time with people who think and live differently.
[384] You get to do real things: research, projects, internships, performances, service.
[385] Pull-out (collapsed): Words colleges use (plain translations)
[386] Major: The subject you study most.
[387] Elective: A class you choose to take, not one your major requires.
[388] General education: Classes every student takes in different subjects, whatever the major.
[389] Seminar: A small class built on discussion.
[390] Humanities: Literature, history, philosophy, languages and the arts.
[391] Liberal arts: Not a list of majors. A way of learning: lots of subjects, small discussions, writing, and getting to know your professors.
[392] Adviser: The person who helps you choose classes and plan.
[393] Mentor: An adult who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals.
[394] Office hours: Set times when a professor is free to talk with any student, no appointment needed.
[395] Internship: A short job in a field you're curious about.
[396] Common Data Set: A standard report most colleges post online, with class sizes, costs and graduation rates.

[581] Your answers point to personal success. You want college to pay off. The evidence says it does, in a way most people get backwards.
[582] What graduates told us
[583] Here's what surprised people: a specialized program helped graduates get their first job, but that edge faded. Over a whole career, students who explored lots of subjects did better, and major didn't predict long-term success.
[584] That was even more true for students who are most often told to play it safe: those from families with less money and those with lower test scores.
[585] The numbers (collapsed): Across all ages, more than half of classes outside the major: 24% more likely to report a higher income (33% for graduates from lower-income families; 34% for graduates who entered with lower test scores). Over a long career: 53% more likely to report greater success and 72% more likely to report a higher income (72% and 75% for graduates from lower-income families). An authentic learning community, over a long career: 32% more likely to report greater success (64% for graduates from lower-income families). See all the findings: What Matters in College
[586] Questions to ask
[587] What share of students complete an internship or a research project, and is there money for students who cannot work unpaid?
[588] Where are your graduates ten years out, not six months out?
[589] How much of the degree can I take outside my major and still finish in four years?
[590] Find out on your own
[591] Use the federal College Scorecard at collegescorecard.ed.gov for cost at your income level and completion rates.
[592] Run the net price calculator on each college’s own website before you fall in love with a sticker price.
[593] If you can't visit
[594] In an online session, ask: "What share of last year's graduates had an internship, and how did they find it?"
[595] Look up the college's alumni on LinkedIn and see where graduates in your intended major work ten years out.
[596] Try this one thing: Ask the career office one question: "What do you know about your graduates at age 35?" Colleges that track that far are thinking about your working life, not your first job.
[597] On your list
[598] Ask what share of students in your intended major complete an internship or research project, and whether there is funding for unpaid ones.
[599] Ask how alumni networks help graduates find internships and first jobs, and for examples from the last two years.
[600] For community college: ask about transfer agreements with four-year colleges and the earnings of students who transferred and finished.

[604] Paying for it
[605] The price on the website is not the price most students pay. At private nonprofit colleges, about 9 in 10 first-year students receive grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of published tuition.[3]
[606] Run the net price calculator on each college’s own site, and check cost at your income level on collegescorecard.ed.gov. File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens in the fall of senior year.
[607] First in your family
[608] You are not behind. Find one adult to help you this year: your school counselor if you can get time with one ("I want to go to college, and I am the first in my family. What should I be doing now?"), or, if not, a teacher, coach or librarian, a free program such as TRIO Upward Bound or Talent Search or College Advising Corps, or the admissions counselor each college assigns to your area. Ask about application and test fee waivers. Try three net price calculators, including one private college you assumed was too expensive. File the FAFSA early, because some aid runs out.
[609] Belonging, and finishing
[610] Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to stay and finish when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of a college.[13] Ask each college what share of students like you graduate (the graduation rate, measured over four and six years), and what support exists in the first six weeks, when most students who leave decide to. Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen found that a one-hour exercise, in which first-year students read how older students' worries about belonging had faded, reduced the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation.[6] A 2023 study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 colleges and universities found a similar exercise raised the share who completed their first year full-time, especially for students from groups that had historically progressed at lower rates, and most at colleges that gave students real opportunities to belong.[7]
[611] Getting in
[612] Build a list with colleges you are likely to get into and can afford, some matches, and a few reaches. Don't get hung up on prestige: Dale and Krueger found that for most students a more selective college made little difference to later earnings once their own abilities were considered, although it made some difference for Black and Hispanic students and for students whose parents had less education.[1]
[613] If it is not your first choice
[614] The study found that what students did in college mattered more than which college they attended. The five experiences on this site are available at every kind of institution. At larger or less personal places you may have to seek them out, through honors programs, office hours and campus involvement, but they are there. At smaller colleges, and especially liberal arts colleges, they are a normal part of almost everyone's experience; you would have to make an effort to avoid them.

[630] For College Students
[631] Am I getting the part of college that lasts?
[632] Classes are the required part of college. The part that shapes the rest of your life mostly happens around them: professors who know you, classes outside your major, discussions that make you think, and the people and groups you're part of. It's how people find work they love, friends who last, and a sense of what they're here for.
[633] What about getting a job? (short panel beside the headline)
[634] You don't have to choose between a good job and a good life. The graduates we talked with who had these experiences were more likely to be doing well financially as adults, and also more likely to lead, give back, keep learning and feel their lives were worthwhile. A specialized course of study helped with the first job, but that edge faded, and over a whole working life, major didn't predict success.[1] Employers say they look first for people who can communicate, solve problems and adapt.[2]
[635] "My liberal arts education did not prepare me for one particular job or profession; it prepared me for any that I would choose once I truly found my passion." — Deborah, senior officer of a major corporation (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, pp. 162–163)

[641] Five Moves
[642] The five moves that matter most
[643] Shown as five tabs. After the quiz, the moves you haven't made yet come first and are marked "Your next move".
[644] These work for every major, including engineering, nursing and business, and wherever you go to college.
[645] 1. Get to know a professor
[646] Why it matters: Many graduates had one professor who changed things for them: someone who noticed them, talked with them about more than the class, and kept in touch. Those mentors were linked to graduates becoming leaders, giving back and finding their lives fulfilling. [Term: a mentor is someone who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals, not only your grade.]
[647] Start small: Go to office hours once with a real question: "Can you help me understand one thing from Tuesday's class?" Or look up something your professor wrote and ask a question about it. [Term: office hours are set times when a professor is free to talk with any student, no appointment needed.]
[648] If that's hard for you:
[649] At a big university: Start with your TA (the teaching assistant, usually a graduate student who leads your section). They're often the bridge to the professor. Go to office hours in the first weeks, before the lines form.
[650] Online or hybrid classes: Use video office hours, or email one specific question and ask for a ten-minute call.
[651] Short on time or commuting: Stay two minutes after class, or email a thoughtful question.
[652] At a smaller college: Even if you feel intimidated, professors expect students to come by with questions and for conversation. It's part of how they see their job.
[653] 2. Take classes way outside your major
[654] Why it matters: Graduates who explored lots of subjects were more likely to keep learning their whole lives, and more likely to earn more later on, too. Plenty of them found what they loved in a class they never planned to take.
[655] Start small: Save one elective next semester for something you're genuinely curious about. [Term: an elective is a class you choose rather than one your major requires.]
[656] If that's hard for you:
[657] Packed majors (engineering, nursing, accounting, education): Use your required general education classes on purpose. [Term: general education is the set of classes every student takes in different subjects.] Pick the section with the most discussion and writing, in a subject far from your major.
[658] Planning to transfer: Check which classes count at the four-year college you might next attend before you choose. Many transfer as general education.
[659] 3. Find classes that make you think for yourself
[660] Why it matters: The biggest difference in the whole study showed up here. Graduates whose professors made them question their own arguments were about two and a half times as likely to say their lives felt deeply fulfilling.
[661] Start small: In your next paper or problem set, add one paragraph that starts: "The strongest argument against my view is..."
[662] If that's hard for you:
[663] Mostly lectures and tests? Take one seminar next term (a small class built on discussion), or start a study group that argues about the material.
[664] 4. Be part of something
[665] Why it matters: Moves 1 and 4 together are what the study calls an authentic learning community: professors and students involved with one another outside class as well as in it. Of everything the study measured, it was the experience most consistently linked to every part of adult life: graduates who had it were more likely to lead, volunteer and give, keep learning, take part in the arts and culture, feel fulfilled and, over a long career, be successful. Being part of something is also one of the biggest reasons students stay and finish.
[666] Start small: Pick one group, team, campus job, lab or volunteer project, and show up every week.
[667] If that's hard for you:
[668] Commuting or working: Choose one group that meets when you're already on campus, or a campus job with regular coworkers. Showing up every week matters more than joining a lot.
[669] 5. Spend real time with people unlike you
[670] Why it matters: Graduates who got to know people with different backgrounds and beliefs stayed more curious and more connected to the world for the rest of their lives.
[671] Start small: Have lunch with, or team up on a project with, someone whose background or views are new to you.
[672] "We were not just a community but were more like a family. I learned from my mentors, and I too became a leader." — Anne, musician and music teacher (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 146)
[673] What the five moves were linked to:
[674] 1. Get to know a professor: graduates with a mentor or regular conversations with professors were more likely to lead, to volunteer, to keep learning, to take part in culture, to feel fulfilled and, over a long career, to be successful.
[675] 2. Take classes way outside your major: more likely to earn more and, over a long career, to be successful; also more likely to feel fulfilled, keep learning and take part in culture.
[676] 3. Find classes that make you think for yourself: far more likely to feel fulfilled, and more likely to keep learning and take part in culture.
[677] 4. Be part of something: together with move 1, an authentic learning community, linked to all six outcomes: leading, giving back, learning, culture, fulfillment and success.
[678] 5. Spend real time with people unlike you: more likely to lead and to keep learning.
[679] Always visible: Want the specific percentages?
[680] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers behind the five moves
[681] 1. Get to know a professor. A mentor with lasting impact: 26% more likely to be leaders, 28% more likely to volunteer, 23% more likely to keep learning, 31% more likely to be involved in culture and 34% more likely to feel fulfilled. Talking with professors about things other than coursework: 37% more likely to volunteer, 31% more likely to keep learning and 39% more likely to be involved in culture.
[682] 2. Take classes way outside your major. More than half of classes outside the major: 24% more likely to earn more as adults, and over a long career, 53% more likely to say they were successful and 72% more likely to earn more; 20% more likely to keep learning. Breadth overall: 59% more likely to feel fulfilled and 42% more likely to be involved in culture.
[683] 3. Find classes that make you think for yourself. Made to question their own arguments: 149% more likely (about two and a half times) to say their lives felt highly fulfilling and 37% more likely to keep learning. Questions with no single right answer: 61% more likely to feel fulfilled. Writing papers in most classes: 51% more likely to feel fulfilled and 49% more likely to be involved in culture.
[684] 4. Be part of something. An authentic learning community: 25% more likely to lead, 42% more likely to volunteer, 56% more likely to give a larger share of income, 31% more likely to keep learning, 40% more likely to be involved in culture, 46% more likely to feel fulfilled, and, over a long career, 32% more likely to be successful. Taking an active part in campus organizations: 30% more likely to be civically involved.
[685] 5. Spend real time with people unlike you. Frequent conversations with people of different beliefs and values: 26% more likely to lead and 38% more likely to keep learning. Discussing issues such as peace and justice with other students: 27% more likely to lead and 56% more likely to keep learning.

[692] Already doing all five? (shown first for quiz results of 8 to 10)
[693] Most students never get all of these. If you have, the next step is to go deeper and pass it on.
[694] Make something with your name on it: an independent study, an honors thesis, a summer research job or a creative project with a professor. Go in with a one-paragraph idea.
[695] Lead something: take a role with real decisions in a group you already belong to.
[696] Mentor someone: be a peer mentor, tutor or orientation leader.
[697] Cross the line again: take an upper-level class far from your major, or study away for a semester.
[698] Look ahead: ask your mentor to introduce you to two graduates doing work you're curious about, and ask what they wish they'd done in college.
[699] When college gets hard: four things to do first
[700] Shown as a highlighted card in the sidebar, before and after the quiz.
[701] If you're failing a class, overwhelmed, or wondering whether to drop out:
[702] 1.  Tell one professor or adviser right away: Say: "I'm struggling this term and I don't want to fall behind. What are my options?" They hear this every week.
[703] 2.  Use tutoring and academic support: It's free at almost every college. Students who use it are the ones who finish.
[704] 3.  Protect your credits: Dropping one class before the deadline is far better than failing three. Talk to financial aid before you withdraw, because it can change your aid.
[705] 4.  Know that feeling you don't belong is common, and it passes. Most students wonder at first whether they fit in, and for most the feeling fades as they find their people. In research by Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen, Black first-year students who simply heard this from older students earned better grades all the way through graduation; a later study of nearly 27,000 students found the same message helped more students finish their first year.[3, 4] Find one group or one professor and keep showing up.

[787] Situation advice (shown with the result when picked)
[788] At a big university
[789] Start with your TA, who is often the bridge to the professor. Go to office hours in the first weeks before lines form. Look for honors sections, research programs and small seminars, which are where the small-college experience lives inside a large university.
[790] If you commute or work
[791] Stay two minutes after class, or email one thoughtful question. Ask for a five-minute video check-in. For involvement, choose one group that meets at a time you are already on campus. Consistency beats volume.
[792] If your major leaves no free slots
[793] Use your required general education courses deliberately instead of treating them as a checklist. One well-chosen requirement a term is still breadth. In the study, these links held for every kind of major, including engineering and professional programs.
[794] If you are struggling right now
[795] Three things, in order. Tell one professor or adviser this week: "I am struggling this term and I do not want to fall behind. What are my options?" Use tutoring, which is free at almost every college. And protect your credits: dropping one class before the deadline is far better than failing three. Talk to financial aid before withdrawing from anything. And remember that feeling you don't belong is common at first and usually passes; find one group or one professor and keep showing up.
[796] Online or hybrid classes
[797] Use video office hours, or email one specific question and ask for a ten-minute call. In discussion boards, reply to the post you disagree with, not the one you agree with. Look for one group, online or on campus, that meets at the same time every week.
[798] If you plan to transfer
[799] Check which courses count at the four-year colleges you're considering attending before you register; many breadth courses transfer as general education. Ask a professor you know now for a recommendation before you leave. At the new college, go to office hours in the first two weeks: transfer students often say this is when they most needed a contact.

[815] For Parents
[816] What do you hope college will give your child?
[817] Ask parents what they want most for their children as adults, and the answers are remarkably consistent: to stand on their own, to have work they enjoy, to be honest and good to other people, and to have a life that feels worthwhile.[1] A college can help with all of it, or with very little of it. The difference is less about the name or the price, and more about what happens to your child there.

[826] What graduates' lives tell us
[827] We talked with 1,000 college graduates, ten, twenty and forty years after they finished.[2] The ones who were standing on their own, doing work they cared about, leading and helping others, and finding their lives worthwhile had something in common. It wasn't mainly the college's name or their major. It was what happened to them there:
[828] Professors who knew them and talked with them outside class, often a mentor whose influence lasted.
[829] Room to explore many subjects, not only their major.
[830] Classes that made them think, question their own ideas, and write and argue.
[831] Being part of something on campus, week after week.
[832] Professors who know students and real involvement in campus life are what the study calls an authentic learning community. Of everything it measured, this was the experience most consistently linked to every part of adult life, including, over a long career, success.
[833] "My professors were patient and gave unstintingly of their time, tutoring me so that I could pursue my desired major." — Bernadette, research scientist (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 114)
[834] These experiences can be found at every kind of college. At some they're built in; in the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. At others, students have to go looking. That's why the questions you and your child ask matter.
[835] What these experiences were linked to:
[836] An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were more likely to be leaders, to be civically involved, to keep learning, to be involved in the arts and culture, to feel fulfilled and, over a long career, to be successful.
[837] A mentor whose influence lasted: more likely to lead, to volunteer and to feel fulfilled.
[838] Room to explore: more likely to earn more and, over a long career, to be successful.
[839] Classes that made them think: far more likely to describe their lives as fulfilled, and more likely to keep learning.
[840] Always visible: Want the specific percentages?
[841] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers behind this
[842] An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were 25% more likely to be leaders, 42% more likely to volunteer and 56% more likely to give a larger share of income, 31% more likely to keep learning, 40% more likely to take part in the arts and culture, 46% more likely to describe their lives as fulfilled, and, over a long career, 32% more likely to report greater success.
[843] A mentor whose influence lasted: 26% more likely to be leaders, 34% more likely to feel fulfilled, 28% more likely to volunteer.
[844] Room to explore: graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income; over a long career, 53% more likely to report greater success and 72% more likely to report a higher income. A specialized major helped with the first job, but that edge faded.
[845] Classes that made them think: graduates made to examine their own arguments were 149% more likely (about two and a half times as likely) to describe their lives as highly fulfilled and 37% more likely to keep learning.

Those who had been part of an authentic learning community were 64% more likely to report greater success.[3] A college that gives your child these experiences, and that you can afford, may matter most for families with the least.

[852] Paying for College
[853] What will it really cost?
[854] The price on a college's website is rarely what families pay.
[855] Three cards in a row, shown open.
[856] Most families pay less. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and those grants cover more than half of posted tuition on average.[4] [Term: a grant is money that doesn't have to be paid back.] Some selective private colleges cost lower-income families less than a regional state university.
[857] If money is tight, you may pay little or nothing. Use each college's net price calculator early, with your child. [Term: a net price calculator is a form on every college's website that estimates what your family would pay after grants.] Ask the school counselor or the college admissions office about application and test fee waivers.
[858] If you will pay most of it, look for merit scholarships. Many colleges offer substantial discounts based on grades or talent, regardless of family income. [Term: merit aid is based on grades or talent; need-based aid is based on what your family can afford.]
[859] Always visible: Borrowing can make sense, within limits.
[860] Pull-out (collapsed): About student loans, honestly
[861] Rule of thumb: Keep your child's total borrowing below what they expect to earn in their first year of work.
[862] Federal loans first: Federal Direct Loans have fixed rates and income-driven repayment protections. Avoid private loans with variable rates whenever possible. [Term: income-driven repayment sets monthly payments by what the borrower earns.]
[863] Finishing on time saves money: A college where students finish in four years often costs less than a cheaper college where they take five or six.
[864] Loans are not aid. They are cost moved later. Compare offers on net price, not on the total package.
[865] End of pull-out.
[866] Pull-out (collapsed): Other paths
[867] Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college can cut costs sharply. Check out the transfer agreements a community college has with four-year colleges in advance. [Term: a transfer or articulation agreement says which community-college courses a four-year college will accept.] Apprenticeships, trade certifications and military benefits are real routes too. The questions on this site apply to any college your child considers along any of these paths.
[868] End of pull-out.
[869] A parent's timeline: what to do, and when
[870] Shown open as six steps across the page (two rows of three on a laptop). Written for parents who did not go to college as well as those who did.
[871] 1.  Junior year: Meet your child's school counselor with your child. Say: "We want to plan for college. What should we be doing this year?" Try three net price calculators together, including one at a private college you assumed was too expensive.
[872] 2.  Junior and senior years: visit. Visit several colleges with your child, in person if possible and online if not, to get a feel for the kind of college that fits. Ask questions, and encourage your child to ask their own (see "Visiting colleges" below).
[873] 3.  Summer before senior year: Help your child make a balanced list: colleges they're likely to get into and can afford, some good matches, and a few reaches. Ask about fee waivers if needed.
[874] 4.  Fall of senior year: File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens. Some colleges also ask for the CSS Profile, a second aid form; check each college's aid page. [Term: the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the free form that decides federal grants, work-study jobs and loans; most states and colleges use it for their own aid too. File it at studentaid.gov, which also has step-by-step help. It opens each fall for the following school year.]
[875] 5.  Spring of senior year: Compare financial aid offers side by side. Look at the net price (cost minus grants and scholarships), not the loans. If an offer falls short, call the aid office and ask whether it can be reviewed.
[876] 6.  The final choice: Don't decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to the question you started with: what does your child want their life to be, and which college is most likely to give them the experiences that make a lifelong difference?
[877] If your child's school counselor isn't available (many have several hundred students), you can still get free help: a teacher or coach who knows your child; federal TRIO programs such as Upward Bound and Talent Search, for lower-income and first-generation families; College Advising Corps advisers in many high schools; the admissions counselor each college assigns to your area; and the financial aid office at a nearby community college. The federal site studentaid.gov explains financial aid step by step.
[878] Visiting colleges, in person or online
[879] A visit is where you find out whether a college offers the experiences that matter, not only whether the campus is pretty. Visit together if you can, and let your child lead.
[880] Before you go: each of you write down two questions. The quiz results on this site give your child some.
[881] Talk to students, not only tour guides. Ask: "When did a professor last talk with you outside class? What do you show up for every week?"
[882] Sit in on a class if you can, in person or online. Notice who does the talking.
[883] Ask about belonging: "Who notices if a first-year student is struggling or lonely, and what do they do?"
[884] Afterward, ask your child first, before you share your own view: "Could you see yourself there? Who did you meet that you'd want to know?"
[885] Can't visit? Virtual tours, online information sessions and a video call with a current student cover most of this. Some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students; search the college's name and "fly-in program."

[951] What graduates told us
[952] Parents want their children to stand on their own, and so do we. The surprise in the research: a specialized major helped with the first job, but that edge faded. Over a whole career, graduates who studied widely did better, and major didn't predict long-term success.
[953] It mattered most for families with the least room for error.

An authentic learning community, over a long career: 32% more likely to report greater success (64% for lower-income families). See all the findings: What Matters in College

[967] What graduates told us
[968] The graduates who kept learning and growing all their lives often started in small discussion classes early in college, working on questions with no single right answer. Those same graduates were more likely to say their lives felt fulfilling.

[972] Who teaches first-year courses here?

[996] Paying for it
[997] Most families pay less than the published price. At private nonprofit colleges, about 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college, covering on average more than half of published tuition.[4] Compare award letters on net price, which is cost minus free grants, not cost minus loans.
[998] First family through
[999] You don't need to have gone to college to do this well. Junior year: talk with the school counselor (or, if you can't get time with one, a teacher, a TRIO or College Advising Corps adviser, or a college's admissions counselor for your area) and try three net price calculators together. Visit a few colleges, in person or online. Summer: build a balanced list and ask about fee waivers. Fall of senior year: file the FAFSA as soon as it opens. Spring: compare awards side by side on net price.
[1000] If you will pay close to full price
[1001] Ask what the money buys that a cheaper option does not: class sizes, who teaches first-year courses, how many professors will know your child, and what share of students complete research or internships with funding attached. Those are the things linked to outcomes decades later.
[1002] About loans, honestly
[1003] Keep total student borrowing below what your child expects to earn in their first year of work. Use federal Direct Loans before private loans, because of fixed rates and income-driven repayment. And note that a college where students finish in four years often costs less than a cheaper one where they take six.
[1004] Well-being
[1005] Ask what the counseling wait time is in October, not in June. Ask who notices when a student stops attending. Tinto’s work ties persistence to connection, and connection is also what protects students who are struggling.[8] Studies by Gregory Walton and colleagues found that helping new students see that worries about belonging are common and pass improved grades and first-year completion.[9]
[1006] AI and whether the jobs will be there
[1007] The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[10] A 2026 survey of corporate recruiters found employers weigh communication, problem-solving and adaptability most heavily in hiring.[5] Those are what a broad education builds, and they are the parts hardest to automate.
[1008] Other paths
[1009] Starting at a community college and transferring can cut degree costs substantially. Before enrolling, check the community college's transfer (articulation) agreements: written agreements that say which courses each four-year college will accept toward a degree, so credits aren't lost. Apprenticeships, trade certifications and military benefits are real routes. The questions on this site apply to any institution along any of these paths.
[1010] Where to find the numbers
[1011] Net price calculator: on every college’s own website, by law. collegescorecard.ed.gov: average cost at your income level, completion rates and earnings. studentaid.gov: the FAFSA. Common Data Set: search the college name plus "Common Data Set" for class sizes, retention and who teaches.

[1040] Being part of something on campus.

[1209] Check your teaching practices
[1210] Five questions, plus one optional about where you teach. Two minutes. Answer for the course you teach most often.
[1211] The first question appears here. Full quiz text follows this page.

[1214] Evidence-Based Teaching
[1215] Five practices that make a lasting difference
[1216] Shown as five tabs, each with its figure set large. After the quiz, practices rated "Not yet" come first and are marked.
[1217] In this study, specific teaching and mentoring practices were linked, decades later, to graduates who led, volunteered, kept learning, took part in culture, described their lives as fulfilled and, over a long career, were successful.[1] These practices matter for all students, and even more for students with the least. The figures below are the main ones for each practice; See all the findings: What Matters in College has every finding.
[1218] Pull-out (collapsed): Who benefits most
[1219] Several links were stronger for graduates from lower-income families. Among graduates well into their careers who grew up in lower-income families, those who had been part of an authentic learning community (close relationships with professors outside class, and a mentor) were 64% more likely to report greater success, against 20% for those from higher-income families; those who took more than half their courses outside their major were 72% more likely to report greater success and 75% more likely to report a higher income. George Kuh's research on high-impact practices found the same pattern: underrepresented and low-income students benefited most.[2] [Term: high-impact practices are experiences such as first-year seminars, undergraduate research, writing-intensive courses and service learning that research links to learning and to students staying in college.]
[1220] End of pull-out.
[1221] 1. Know their names
[1222] Why it matters: Graduates whose professors knew their names were more likely to describe their lives as fulfilled, to give and volunteer, to keep learning and to earn an advanced degree.
[1223] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers
[1224] Graduates whose professors mostly knew their first name were 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, 51% more likely to give a larger share of their income, 31% more likely to keep learning, 22% more likely to volunteer and 21% more likely to earn an advanced degree.
[1225] End of pull-out.
[1226] Ask yourself: How many names do I know by week four?
[1227] Adaptations across teaching contexts:
[1228] Small class: Use name cards or a seating chart for the first month.
[1229] Large course: Use the photo roster, learn the names of students who speak, and ask teaching assistants to learn their sections. Ask for or use a name every time you call on someone.
[1230] Online or hybrid: Ask students to keep their display name as the name they want to be called, open live sessions by greeting students by name, and reply to discussion posts by name.
[1231] Students who work or commute: Arrive five minutes early and greet students by name as they come in.
[1232] Part-time faculty: Names cost nothing and need no office. This is the most portable of the five.
[1233] 2. Talk with students outside class, and not only about the course
[1234] Why it matters: A mentor, and conversations with professors outside class about coursework and about life, were linked to every adult outcome the study measured: leading, volunteering and giving, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and, over a long career, success. Together with campus involvement, these make up what the study calls an authentic learning community, the only kind of experience linked to all six. Alexander Astin's study of 25,000 students found student-faculty interaction to be among the strongest influences on development.[3]
[1235] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers
[1236] Graduates with a college mentor whose influence lasted were 26% more likely to be leaders, 28% more likely to volunteer, 38% more likely to give a larger share of income, 23% more likely to keep learning, 31% more likely to be culturally involved and 34% more likely to describe their lives as fulfilled; and, over a long career, 24% more likely to report greater success. Graduates who often talked with professors outside class about non-academic subjects were 37% more likely to volunteer, 31% more likely to keep learning, 39% more likely to be culturally involved and 25% more likely to feel fulfilled; about academic subjects, 22% more likely to lead, 29% more likely to keep learning and 32% more likely to feel fulfilled, and over a long career, 39% more likely to report greater success.
[1237] End of pull-out.
[1238] Ask yourself: Which students have I spoken with one-to-one this term?
[1239] Adaptations across teaching contexts:
[1240] Small class: Require one ten-minute office-hours visit from every student in the first three weeks.
[1241] Large course: Hold small-group office hours for eight to ten students at a time, and ask teaching assistants or learning assistants to do the same.
[1242] Online or hybrid: Schedule a ten-minute video check-in with each student in the first month, or with small groups in a large online course. Keep one evening slot for students in other time zones or with day jobs.
[1243] Students who work or commute: Offer video office hours, early-morning or evening times, and the five minutes after class. When a student asks about the course, ask one question about their plans.
[1244] Part-time faculty: Use a short video meeting or the minutes after class, and ask the department for a space to meet students.
[1245] 3. Make class time active
[1246] Why it matters: Discussion-based classes early in college, and a real student role in discussion, were linked to continued learning, cultural involvement and fulfillment. In science, engineering and mathematics, a review of 225 studies by Scott Freeman and colleagues found that students in traditional lectures were about 1.5 times as likely to fail as students in classes that used active learning.[4] [Term: active learning means students do something in class, such as answering, discussing or solving, rather than only listening.]
[1247] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers
[1248] Graduates who took discussion-based seminars in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively, 37% more likely to stay closely involved in arts, ideas and other cultures, and 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Graduates who had a large role in discussion in most classes were 47% more likely to be culturally involved and 51% more likely to feel fulfilled; small first-year classes (twenty to thirty students or fewer) were linked to continued learning (23%) and fulfillment (20%).
[1249] End of pull-out.
[1250] Ask yourself: Who did most of the talking in my last class?
[1251] Adaptations across teaching contexts:
[1252] Small class: Open each class with a question, and take answers before you give yours.
[1253] Large course: Pose a question, have students answer, discuss it with a neighbor, and answer again. Richard Light's work popularized the one-minute paper at the end of class as a simple way to hear from everyone.[5]
[1254] Online or hybrid: Use breakout rooms of three or four with one question and a two-minute report back. In a course with no live sessions, replace "post once, reply twice" with a discussion that requires students to change or defend a position after reading replies.
[1255] Students who work or commute: Build the reading into class. Read a short passage together, then discuss it, rather than assuming it was done at home.
[1256] Part-time faculty: All of these work in any room, with no extra preparation beyond the question.
[1257] 4. Give assignments with no single right answer
[1258] Why it matters: Coursework with no single right answer, and writing in most classes, were linked to fulfillment, cultural involvement and continued learning.
[1259] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers
[1260] Graduates whose coursework often involved questions with no single right answer were 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, 37% more likely to be culturally involved, 25% more likely to keep learning actively and 21% more likely to earn an advanced degree. Being required to write papers in most classes was linked to fulfillment (51%), cultural involvement (49%) and continued learning (32%).
[1261] End of pull-out.
[1262] Ask yourself: Which of my assignments could two good students answer differently?
[1263] Discipline-specific adaptations:
[1264] Humanities and social sciences: Replace one quiz with a short paper that takes a position.
[1265] Science, math and engineering: Set a design problem, or ask students to explain why their approach is better than another one that also works.
[1266] Professional programs: Use a case with competing goods. In nursing, a patient's wishes against a protocol. In business or accounting, a judgment call in reporting.
[1267] Online: Open-ended assignments are also harder to answer with a generic AI response; ask students to apply the question to their own town, job or experience.
[1268] 5. Ask students to examine their own thinking
[1269] Why it matters: Being made to examine the strengths and weaknesses of one's own arguments was linked to the largest difference in the study: graduates were about two and a half times as likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes, in any field, pointed the same way.
[1270] Pull-out (collapsed): The numbers
[1271] Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled (about two and a half times as likely, the largest difference in the study) and 37% more likely to keep learning. Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes, in any field, was linked to fulfillment (59%), cultural involvement (30%) and continued learning (29%).
[1272] End of pull-out.
[1273] Ask yourself: Do my students ever have to argue against themselves?
[1274] Discipline-specific adaptations:
[1275] Any course: Add one line to every assignment: "State the strongest objection to your answer."
[1276] Science, math and engineering: "Where could this calculation or design go wrong?"
[1277] Professional programs: "What would a skeptical colleague say about this decision?"
[1278] "Not just to memorize the facts, but to ask why and how." — Susan, physician, on what her professors asked of her (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 131)
[1279] At a research university: mentoring that counts in a research career
[1280] Card below the five practices; also shown first after the quiz when the reader picks "I teach at a research university".
[1281] The practice most strongly linked to graduates' leadership, a mentor whose influence lasted, is also the one research faculty are best placed to give. It does not have to compete with research.
[1282] Bring undergraduates into the lab or project. Two or three undergraduates working with a graduate student or postdoctoral researcher on part of a real project is mentoring, training and research at once.
[1283] Teach a research course. A course built around a real research question (often called a course-based undergraduate research experience, or CURE) gives a whole section of students the experience usually reserved for a few. It can also produce pilot data.
[1284] Co-author with students when their work contributes, and say so in your annual report.
[1285] Count it. Record mentoring in your tenure and promotion file as part of teaching: students supervised, theses, conference presentations and where those students went next. Many grant programs ask for broader impacts; undergraduate mentoring is one.
[1286] Share it. Ask that advising and mentoring loads be tracked and balanced across the department (see below).
[1287] Who carries this work
[1288] A visible callout, not collapsed.
[1289] Belonging is part of the work. In large first-year courses especially, students quietly wonder whether they belong. Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen found that a one-hour exercise helping first-year students see that such worries are common and pass improved Black students' grades through graduation and cut the racial gap by about half; a 2023 study with nearly 27,000 students at 22 institutions found a similar exercise raised first-year completion.[6, 7] Telling your students, early in the term, that most students feel this way at first and that it passes is a small version of the same message.
[1290] Mentoring, advising and conversations outside class take time, and that time is not shared evenly. Studies of faculty workload find that women faculty do more of this kind of service than men,[8] and faculty of color often describe extra mentoring and service demands, sometimes called "cultural taxation." The work is rarely counted in tenure and promotion reviews. If these practices matter, departments and institutions need to recognize, share and reward them. The College Leaders page includes sample tenure and promotion language that counts this work as teaching.
[1291] "They worked to teach the whole person, not just the journalist I would become." — Alison, journalist (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 159)

[1391] Situation advice (shown with the result when picked)
[1392] In a large lecture
[1393] Use the photo roster, learn the names of students who speak, and use a name, or ask for one, every time you call on someone. Hold small-group office hours for eight to ten students at a time. Pose a question, have students answer, discuss with a neighbor, then answer again.
[1394] With commuting and working students
[1395] Arrive five minutes early and greet students by name as they come in. Offer video office hours and early or late times. Build the reading into class: read a short passage together and discuss it, rather than assuming it was done at home.
[1396] Teaching part-time, without an office
[1397] Names cost nothing and need no office. So does one question at the start of class, and one added line on every assignment. Ask the department for a space to meet students; it is a reasonable request and it is the main structural barrier.
[1398] In science, engineering and mathematics
[1399] Set a design problem, or ask students to explain why their approach is better than another that also works. Add: "Where could this calculation or design go wrong?" Freeman and colleagues, reviewing 225 studies, found students in traditional lectures about 1.5 times as likely to fail as those in active-learning classes.[4]
[1400] Online or hybrid
[1401] Greet students by name in live sessions and reply to posts by name. Schedule a ten-minute video check-in with each student, or with small groups, in the first month. Use breakout rooms of three or four with one question. In courses with no live sessions, ask students to change or defend a position after reading replies, and set assignments that apply the question to their own town, job or experience.

[1403] The practice most strongly linked to leadership, a lasting mentor, is the one research faculty are best placed to give. Bring two or three undergraduates into a project with a graduate student or postdoctoral researcher, teach one course built around a real research question, and record mentoring in your tenure and promotion file as teaching. [1437] Go deeper (three cards that open)
[1438] The retention case. The experiences linked to graduates' lives are also linked to whether students stay. Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to persist when connected to both the academic and social life of the institution.[2] George Kuh found that first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research and service learning were associated with better grades and returning for a second year, with the largest benefits for underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students.[3] Gregory Walton and colleagues found that a brief exercise helping new students see that worries about belonging are common and pass raised first-year completion in a study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 institutions, most for students from groups that historically progressed at lower rates, and most at institutions that offered real opportunities to belong.[4] Every student who stays is a student who can graduate, and tuition the institution does not have to replace.
[1439] Other research agrees. Alexander Astin's study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found peer influence to be a powerful influence on development, with faculty interaction next.[5] Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini's review of decades of research concluded that what students do in college matters more than which college they attend.[6] Gallup-Purdue found that only 3% of graduates strongly agreed they had received all six key supportive and hands-on experiences.[7]
[1440] It has been done, at every kind of institution. George Kuh and colleagues studied 20 institutions whose graduation rates and student engagement were higher than predicted for the students they enrolled.[3, 8] They included research universities, regional universities, liberal arts colleges and historically Black universities. They described shared conditions such as a lived mission, focus on student learning, clear pathways to success and shared responsibility for educational quality. Examples from Student Success in College (2005) describe those institutions at the time of the study; they are not claims about their current practices.

[1454] If you are a dean or a department chair
[1455] Most of the eight practices are decided at your level, not the president's.
[1456] Class size where it counts: cap first-year and gateway courses so a professor can know students, and put experienced faculty in them.

[1460] Part-time faculty: give adjunct and part-time instructors a place to meet students and include them in advising conversations.

[1463] At a research university, a highly selective college, or a smaller or liberal arts college
[1464] The study found these experiences at every kind of institution. The challenge differs by type.
[1465] Research universities: the experiences exist, but often for a minority of students, such as those in honors programs or labs. Count how many undergraduates have a faculty mentor or a research experience by graduation, then set a goal for all of them. Course-based research, first-year seminars taught by senior faculty, and counting undergraduate mentoring in promotion reviews spread the experience without new buildings.
[1466] Highly selective colleges and universities: students arrive well prepared, and the risk is that the institution takes credit for outcomes students would have reached anyway. The study's results are about what students experienced, not admission. Measure whether every student, including first-generation, lower-income and transfer students, gets faculty contact, mentoring and breadth, not only whether they graduate.
[1467] Public influence: research universities and selective institutions shape how legislators, families and the press define a good education. Reporting on long-term outcomes, not only rankings and first-year salaries, helps every institution.
[1468] Smaller colleges and liberal arts colleges: these experiences should already be happening for every student; in the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. The task is to make sure that is true for every student, and to show it. Assess who misses out (often commuters, transfer students, students who work long hours, and some athletes); improve where needed; document both the experiences and alumni outcomes at ten, twenty and thirty years; and communicate the evidence to prospective families, donors and legislators, who rarely hear it.

[1518] A graduate's first-year earnings tell you about a first job, not a working life. [1653] For Legislators & Policymakers
[1654] What does our state need its college graduates to become?
[1655] Ask what a state really wants from the public money it invests in higher education, and the answer is more than a first job. A state needs people who can lead in workplaces and communities; citizens who volunteer, give and take part; a workforce that keeps learning as jobs change; people engaged in the culture and ideas of their communities; residents whose lives feel purposeful; and graduates who are successful over a whole career. These six outcomes are exactly what the study behind this site measured, ten, twenty and forty years after college.[1]
[1656] State and federal accountability systems mostly count enrollment, completion and earnings in the first few years after college. Those measures set a floor: no student, and no taxpayer, should pay for a program that leaves graduates worse off over the longer term. But the public invests in college for the whole of a graduate's working and civic life. So the questions are which college experiences produce the outcomes a state needs, whether students are getting them, and how the state will know: ten, twenty and thirty years out, not only at graduation or in the first few years.
[1657] What a state can do (short panel beside the headline)
[1658] Five levers, most of them already in state hands:
[1659] 1.  Report requests: ask the higher education agency to report graduates' outcomes ten and twenty years after completion (earnings from state wage records, and civic and career measures from alumni surveys), rather than at graduation or in the first few years.
[1660] 2.  Outcomes-based funding: if your formula rewards completion or early earnings, add long-term outcomes as they become available, and in the meantime a measure of the student experiences linked to them, such as faculty contact or completion of a research project or internship.
[1661] 3.  Transfer and credit rules: make sure breadth courses transfer as general education, so community college students don't lose them.
[1662] 4.  Need-based aid: protect state grants that let lower-income students take unpaid internships and research positions.
[1663] 5.  Data systems: link education and wage records, and keep them, so the state can follow graduates across decades, not only into a first job.

[1790] Understanding College
[1791] Four thousand colleges. One idea underneath almost all of them.
[1792] American colleges look wildly different from one another: a community college down the road, a state university with 40,000 students, a college of 1,500 on a hill. Underneath, most of them were built on the same idea about what an education is for. Knowing that idea, and knowing which colleges still practice it, is the most useful thing a person can learn before choosing one, working at one, or funding one.
[1793] The kinds of colleges
[1794] The point that matters: the experiences linked to good lives after college can be found at every kind of institution. The odds of finding them without having to hunt are not the same everywhere: at liberal arts colleges these experiences are usually built into how the college works, and in the study liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them.[1] At larger or more complex institutions they are often available too, but students may need to seek them out through honors programs, knocking on professors' doors, and pursuing involvement in cocurricular programs.[2] [Term: cocurricular means outside class but part of the education: clubs, research, service, performances, student government.]
[1795] In plain terms: Colleges are built for different jobs. The label tells you what the place was designed to do, though of course they do vary. It does not tell you what a student will experience there.

[1799] Research universities
[1800] Create new knowledge and train researchers and professionals, alongside teaching undergraduates.
[1801] Large campuses, thousands of graduate students, faculty evaluated primarily on research and grants. Many have honors colleges and undergraduate research programs.
[1802] Regional & comprehensive universities
[1803] Serve a region with professional and practical degrees, alongside liberal arts programs.
[1804] Medium to large, often commuter-heavy, with master's degrees in business, education, and health fields.
[1805] Liberal arts colleges
[1806] Focus entirely or mostly on undergraduate education across arts, sciences, and humanities.
[1807] Small (usually under 3,000), residential, few or no graduate programs; faculty evaluated mostly on teaching and mentoring.
[1808] Community colleges
[1809] Provide accessible two-year degrees, career certificates, and transfer pathways close to home.
[1810] Non-residential, affordable, serving local communities with open or broad admissions.
[1811] Other labels cut across these kinds: public or private, religious or secular, historically Black colleges and universities, tribal colleges, women's colleges, online universities. [Term: selective describes a college that admits a small share of applicants.] A historically Black college can be a liberal arts college or a research university.
[1812] One idea, refined over millennia and nearly four centuries old in America
[1813] In plain terms: The American college was never designed as job training. It was designed to prepare people to think, to take part in public life and to keep learning as the needs of the individual and of humanity change over time, so as to be successful in ways that benefit both the individual and society. That design is called the liberal arts, an education for the common good, and it is the root of almost every American bachelor's degree.

[1815] Graphic G2: Evolution of the Liberal Arts in America
[1816] 1636, Harvard founded: The colonial model brings broad study in a close community where students and teachers lived and learned together.[3]
[1817] 1810, Prussian specialized model appears: Prussia creates universities designed to serve the state through early, narrow specialization.[4]
[1818] 1828, the Yale Report: Yale faculty famously reject the Prussian model, affirming that a republic needs broad minds, not narrow technicians.
[1819] 1862, Morrill Land-Grant Act: [Term: land-grant universities were founded on federal land grants to teach agriculture and engineering alongside other subjects.] Expanded practical and agricultural education while preserving liberal arts foundations.
[1820] 1876, Johns Hopkins founded: The modern American research university is born, combining German research with undergraduate broad education.

[1822] Pull-out (collapsed): Read full history: From ancient roots to general education
[1823] Ancient roots. Liberal arts education began in Greece about 3,500 years ago and evolved through Rome, North Africa and the Middle East, and Europe. Its purpose was to educate people for the common good: to benefit both the individual and important needs of society. Over that long span the subjects studied steadily grew, but the approach always involved a close relationship between teachers and students.[5]
[1824] Even as American higher education expanded to include engineering, business, and agriculture, an engineer, nurse or accountant was still expected to spend part of a bachelor's degree on writing, history, science and ideas outside the field. Unlike in most of the world, an aspiring physician or lawyer had to complete an undergraduate course of study before beginning professional education. General education requirements (the courses every student takes across different fields, whatever the major) at a comprehensive or research university are the liberal arts tradition, surviving under another name.
[1825] End of pull-out.
[1826] What "liberal arts" means, and what it doesn't
[1827] In plain terms: "Liberal arts" is not a list of majors. It is a way of being educated: what you study, and how.
[1828] Three common mistakes: (three cards in a row)
[1829] "Liberal arts means the humanities." It never did. The tradition has always included mathematics and the sciences. A physics major at a liberal arts college is getting a liberal arts education.
[1830] "Liberal arts means an education for nothing in particular." Liberal arts means an education not tied to one job, which is a different thing. In the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.[6]
[1831] "Liberal arts has something to do with politics." "Liberal" here comes from the Latin for "free." It meant the education suited to a free person who must make their own judgments.

[1861] The research behind this site found that the lower half of that table, the educational context, and above all an authentic learning community, was the most consistently linked to how graduates' lives turned out.[7] A college can list every subject in the catalog and still not deliver an education that has a long-lasting impact on graduates' lives.
[1862] The pressure to make college pay off fast
[1863] In plain terms: College is expensive, so people reasonably ask what it pays. The trouble starts when the only answer anyone measures is the paycheck.
[1864] Families are encouraged to compare starting salaries by major. Rankings count earnings soon after graduation. Lawmakers tie funding to job placement. Colleges respond the way any organization responds to what they are measured on: they add narrow career programs, enlarge majors until there is little room for anything else, and trim the parts of college that are hard to count, such as student time with faculty.
[1865] The evidence suggests the trade is a poor one. In the study behind this site, specialized preparation helped with the first job, but that advantage faded.[8] Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher incomes later.[9]

[1869] The case for the liberal arts college
[1870] In plain terms: The experiences that matter can happen anywhere. At a liberal arts college, they are the standard, not the exception.
[1871] The study compared experiences, not kinds of colleges, and the experiences that mattered were linked to graduates' lives wherever they happened. A student at a large university who seeks them out can have them. The question for a student or family is where they are most likely to happen without having to search for them. On that question, the evidence is consistent.
[1872] The odds are different.
[1873] Twice as likely: In the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide the six experiences linked to graduates' lives. Engaging teaching was about three times as common, and an authentic learning community more than twice as common.[12]
[1874] 45% against 24%: In a Gallup-Strada national study, 45% of graduates of colleges with fewer than 5,000 students said "my professors care about me as a person."[13] At larger institutions, 24% did.[14]
[1875] 3%: In the Gallup-Purdue study of 30,000 graduates, only 3% had had all of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college, such as a professor who cared about them, a mentor, deep involvement in campus activities and an internship.[15]
[1876] Why the odds are different. Liberal arts colleges are built around undergraduates. There are few or no graduate students competing for faculty time, and faculty are hired, reviewed and promoted mainly for teaching and working with undergraduates, not for research reputation. Their small scale makes it easier for professors to know students by name and for students to be part of the community, the experience most consistently linked to every adult outcome. And the whole design, breadth requirements, discussion-based classes, writing, residential life, delivers all six experiences together rather than one at a time.
[1877] Two common worries, and what the evidence says.
[1878] "Fewer majors to choose from." True at many liberal arts colleges, and consistent with the study's finding that major was unrelated to long-term success while breadth was linked to higher income over a career.
[1879] "Only for wealthy families." Many liberal arts colleges discount tuition heavily: at private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students receive grants from the college.[16] In 2019, 29.8% of students at the twelve liberal arts colleges in the study received Pell Grants, close to the national average of 32%.[17] Check the net price, not the posted price.
[1880] When it may not be the right fit. A student who already knows they want a specialized program a liberal arts college doesn't offer, who needs to live at home, or who thrives in a large and varied setting may be better served elsewhere, and can still seek out these experiences through honors programs, small seminars, research with faculty and campus involvement. What matters is getting the experiences, wherever that happens.

[1899] What prestige does and doesn't do
[1900] In plain terms: A famous name can matter, for some students and some careers. For most graduates, the research finds it matters less than people assume. Both findings are below.
[1901] Two columns: what the evidence says prestige does, and what it does not.
[1902] Where the evidence finds an advantage:
[1903] Top earnings and elite careers: Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman found that attending an Ivy-Plus college rather than a flagship public university raised the odds of being among the highest 1% of earners and of working at the most prestigious finance or consulting firms.[22] [Term: Ivy-Plus means the eight Ivy League universities plus Stanford, MIT, Duke and the University of Chicago.]
[1904] Leadership in some fields: The same researchers note that Ivy-Plus graduates are overrepresented among Fortune 500 chief executives; even so, 88.4% of those CEOs did not attend an Ivy-Plus college.[22]
[1905] Some students more than others: Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger found that a more selective college raised later earnings for Black and Hispanic students and for students whose parents had less education.[23]
[1906] A few narrow fields recruit heavily from a small set of colleges, such as some corporate law firms, investment banks and consulting firms.
[1907] Where the evidence finds little or no advantage:
[1908] Average earnings: In the Chetty, Deming and Friedman study, the effect of Ivy-Plus attendance on graduates' average earnings was small and not statistically significant.[22]
[1909] Earnings over a career, for most students: Dale and Krueger followed earnings for more than 20 years. Once students' own abilities and ambitions were taken into account, the earnings effect of attending a more selective college was close to zero for most students.[23]
[1910] Rankings combine measures such as reputation, resources, selectivity and graduation rates. They say a good deal about a college's wealth and standing, and little about what a given student will experience there.
[1911] What both sides agree on: what a student does at college matters wherever they go. The study behind this site measured that directly.
[1912] What does drive long-term outcomes?
[1913] In plain terms: How a student goes to college matters more than where.
[1914] Three large bodies of research point the same way:
[1915] The study behind this site: Among 1,000 graduates ages 25 to 65, an authentic learning community (involvement with faculty and other students outside class) was linked to all six adult outcomes, and broad study and courses that demanded thought were linked to most of them. Major was not. See all the findings: What Matters in College
[1916] Gallup-Purdue: Among nearly 30,000 graduates, supportive professors and experiential learning were tied to much higher odds of engagement at work and thriving in life.[15] Where graduates went to college made little difference.
[1917] Decades of scholarship: Astin, Pascarella and Terenzini, Light, and Tinto each found that what students do in college, and with whom, matters more than institutional type or standing.[24, 25, 26] Gregory Walton and colleagues showed that a sense of belonging affects whether students stay and succeed: brief exercises helping new students see that worries about belonging are common and pass improved grades and first-year completion, most where colleges offered real opportunities to belong.[27, 28]
[1918] The links between these experiences and later success were strongest for graduates from lower-income families.
[1919] On “fit”: A college fits when a student is likely to have the educational experiences related to their long-term life goals, not simply when a campus tour feels right.
[1920] Five things most people get wrong (a grid of five myth-and-fact cards; linked from the top of the page)
[1921] 1.  "Your major determines your career." Major was unrelated to long-term success for most graduates in the study. And people change jobs and careers often: Americans born in the early 1980s held an average of 9.4 jobs between ages 18 and 38, and those born from 1957 to 1964 held 12.7 jobs between 18 and 56 (12.8 for college graduates), according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.[29, 30]
[1922] 2.  "Rankings tell you which college is best." Rankings weigh reputation, resources and selectivity. They say little about what a given student will experience.
[1923] 3.  "A big university offers more." It has more offers. Whether a given student gets any is a different question.
[1924] 4.  "The sticker price is the price." Most students pay less than published tuition. Compare the net price after grants.[31]
[1925] 5.  "Liberal arts graduates can't get jobs." Employers say the opposite, and long-term income evidence favors breadth.[32]

[2009] What stands out
[2010] An authentic learning community was linked to every outcome. Close relationships with professors outside class, a mentor, being known by name, and real involvement in campus life were the only kind of college experience substantially linked to all six parts of adult life. For personal success, the link appears among graduates well established in their careers (ages 55 to 65).
[2011] The educational context mattered as much as the content. How students were taught and the community they were part of were linked to as many outcomes as what they studied.
[2012] Continued learning and cultural involvement were linked to all six kinds of college experience, and fulfillment to five. The largest single difference in the study was in fulfillment: graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely, about two and a half times as likely, to describe their lives as highly fulfilled.[1]
[2013] Major didn't predict long-term success. A major not tied to one occupation had no effect, positive or negative, on income or position. Breadth did: studying widely was linked to higher income, especially over a long career.
[2014] The benefits were often larger for graduates from lower-income families. Among graduates aged 55 to 65 from lower-income families, those who took more than half their courses outside their major were 72% more likely to report greater success and 75% more likely to report a higher income. Those who experienced an authentic learning community were 64% more likely to report greater success.
[2015] Background mattered for income and position, not for the rest. Gender, race and ethnicity, and family income were linked to income and position, but generally not to leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement or fulfillment. Over a long career, only family income remained linked to success, and the college experiences above were linked to success for graduates from lower- and higher-income families alike, most strongly for those from lower-income families.
[2016] What is an authentic learning community?
[2017] In the study, an authentic learning community meant a college where faculty, staff and students are involved with one another outside class as well as in it. It was measured by experiences such as:
[2018] talking often with professors outside class, about coursework and about other things;
[2019] having a college mentor whose influence lasted;
[2020] most professors knowing the student's first name;
[2021] taking an active part in, and leading, campus organizations;
[2022] living in campus housing for three or more years;
[2023] taking part in campus activism or causes.
[2024] Other research points the same way. Vincent Tinto found that students who are connected to both the academic and social life of a college are more likely to stay and finish.[2] Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen found that a one-hour exercise helping first-year students see that worries about belonging are common and pass reduced the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation; a 2023 study with nearly 27,000 students at 22 colleges and universities found a similar exercise raised first-year completion, most for students from groups that had historically progressed at lower rates, and most at colleges that offered real opportunities to belong.[3, 4]
[2025] The detail, outcome by outcome
[2026] Six sections, each a short list: the composite finding for each kind of experience, then the specific experiences within it.
[2027] Leadership
[2028] Leading groups, teams and organizations at work and in the community.
[2029] Developing larger perspectives: 28%. Discussing issues such as peace, justice and human rights with other students outside class (27%); frequent conversations with people of different religious beliefs, political opinions or personal values (26%).
[2030] An authentic learning community: 25%. Having a college mentor with lasting impact (26%); often talking with professors about academic subjects outside class (22%); often talking with professors about other subjects outside class (21%).
[2031] Major, breadth, intellectual skills and engaging teaching were not substantially linked to leadership.
[2032] Civic involvement
[2033] Giving, volunteering and voting. Voting was not linked to any college experience; giving and volunteering were.
[2034] An authentic learning community: 26% overall; 56% more likely to donate a larger share of income; 42% more likely to spend more time volunteering.

[2064] Position and household income. Across all ages, none of the six kinds of college experience was linked to overall success, positive or negative; gender, race and ethnicity, and family income were. Two findings stand out:

[2086] Methodology & Evidence
[2087] Where this comes from
[2088] Everything on this site starts from one study, reported in Richard A. Detweiler's The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs: Lives of Consequence, Inquiry, and Accomplishment (MIT Press, 2021). Here is how it was done and what it can and cannot tell you.
[2089] The comparison that matters (a highlighted callout at the top of the page)
[2090] This study compares experiences, not kinds of colleges. Every finding compares graduates who had more of a college experience (such as a mentor, broad study or discussion-based classes) with graduates who had less of it, whatever college they attended.
[2091] Why that matters: studies that compare kinds of colleges can't separate what a college does from who chooses to go there; students who pick a small college may differ from those who pick a large university. Comparing experiences avoids that problem, and it makes the findings useful to everyone. The experiences can be found, and built, at every kind of college: research universities, regional universities, community colleges and liberal arts colleges alike.
[2092] How the study was done
[2093] Four tiles in a row.
[2094] Who: Interviews with 1,000 college graduates from every kind of American college and university.[1]
[2095] Ages: Three groups, about ten, twenty and forty years after graduation: 25 to 35, 36 to 45, and 55 to 65.
[2096] How: Individual telephone interviews by trained interviewers.
[2097] What was asked: What people did and experienced in college, and how they live now. The questions were about behavior, not opinions about whether college helped.
[2098] The research was carried out by Art & Science Group, an education research and consulting firm, which developed and pilot-tested the interview questions, drew the sample, and managed the interviews and the coding of answers. It was funded by the Teagle Foundation (New York) and the Spencer Foundation (Chicago), with additional support from the Great Lakes Colleges Association (Ann Arbor, Michigan).
[2099] Methods in detail
[2100] Shown open, as a two-column block on laptops: the four headings on the left, the text on the right.
[2101] The sample. The 1,000 graduates came from two random draws:
[2102] 600 from a national panel. A panel is a large group of people, recruited by a polling organization to match the national population, who have agreed to take part in research; participants for a study are drawn from it at random. Graduates in the three age groups were selected at random until 600 interviews were complete. This group includes graduates of public and private, large and small, research and undergraduate, selective and non-selective institutions.
[2103] 400 additional alumni. Because most graduates of a typical college have had only some of the experiences studied, a national sample includes few people who had all of them. To widen the range of experience, 400 more graduates were selected at random from the 84,000 living alumni of twelve liberal arts colleges, where these experiences are common: Albion, Allegheny, Antioch, Denison, DePauw, Earlham, Hope, Kalamazoo, Kenyon, Ohio Wesleyan, Wabash and Wooster.
[2104] The model. The study measured six features of the American approach to undergraduate education, which is rooted in the liberal arts, in two groups:
[2105] What is studied (content): study not tied to one occupation; study across the span of knowledge; developing intellectual skills such as writing and reasoning.
[2106] How it is studied (educational context): engaging teaching; contact with larger perspectives, people and ideas unlike one's own; and an authentic learning community, in which faculty and students are involved with one another outside class as well as in it.
[2107] Statistical analyses assessed whether there were significant relationships between these aspects of educational content and context and six adult outcomes: leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and personal success (position and income). All questions were about behavior, not attitudes or opinions. For example, participants were asked "How often did you talk with professors about academic subjects outside of class?", "How frequently did you have conversations with others of different religious, political or social beliefs?" and "Did you take more than half of your courses outside your major?" Examples of questions about adult life were "How many hours do you spend volunteering each month?", "How often do you mentor less experienced people?" and "In a typical day, how many hours do you spend listening to, watching or reading the news?"
[2108] The analysis. (Reminder, repeated here: each comparison is between graduates with more and less of an experience, at every kind of college, never between types of colleges.)
[2109] Higher and lower groups. Because the questions used different kinds of answers (yes or no, frequency, hours), each answer was coded as above or below the median for the whole sample. The answers for each feature and each outcome were added into a composite score, which was split at the median again into a "higher" and a "lower" group. This approach loses some detail, which makes it harder, not easier, to find an effect.
[2110] Testing. The share of graduates with a higher outcome was compared between those with higher and lower levels of each experience, using a chi-square test.
[2111] Size of the difference. The difference is reported as an incident risk ratio: the share with the outcome in one group divided by the share in the other.
[2112] Three tests for reporting a result. A finding is reported only if it is (1) statistically significant at the 5% level, two-tailed (p < .05; most were p < .001); (2) at least a 20% difference between the groups; and (3) consistent across all three age groups. Some statistically significant but smaller effects are therefore not reported.
[2113] Background variables. Before the main analyses, the study tested whether four background characteristics (gender, race and ethnicity, family socioeconomic status while in college, and SAT or ACT score) predicted the adult outcomes and so might explain the findings. They were generally not predictive of the outcomes reported, except personal success (income and position), which family background and test scores did predict. Because they rarely acted as confounders, most analyses are reported without statistical controls; the demographic results are in appendix 4.
[2114] What the design cannot do. The study is cross-sectional: each person was interviewed once, about the present and about college years earlier. Some questions (such as use of technology or service learning) could not be asked because they would not apply to older graduates. Appendix 1 of the book discusses the question of causation.
[2115] Pull-out (collapsed): Sample composition and demographics
[2116] Where the graduates went to college: 46% graduated from bachelor's colleges, 16% from master's universities and 33% from doctoral universities. 35% attended public institutions and 65% private ones. Enrollment ranged from under 1,500 students (13%) to over 20,000 (14%). 27% attended the most selective institutions, 58% moderately selective ones and 15% non-selective ones. Entrance test scores ranged from under 1100 on the SAT (27%) to over 1510 (7.5%).
[2117] Who they were: 58% women and 42% men; 89% white and 11% people of color. Full details are in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, chapter 5 and appendixes 2, 3 and 4.[2]

[2144] What it found
[2145] How to read these numbers: A figure such as "24% more likely" compares two groups. For example, if 57% of one group reports a higher income compared with 46% of another, the first group's rate is 24% higher than the second's (57 divided by 46). Statisticians call this the incident risk ratio, and the increase expressed as a percentage (24%) is called the percent relative effect. All reported effects are statistically significant at the p < .05 level or better; most are significant at p < .001. (Shown as plain text; the stray dollar signs on the live page are removed.)
[2146] The complete results, every substantial finding for the six college experiences and six adult outcomes, are on What Matters in College. In summary:
[2147] 1. An authentic learning community was linked to every outcome: leadership (25%), civic involvement (26%; 56% for giving a larger share of income and 42% for volunteering), continued learning (31%), cultural involvement (40%) and fulfillment (46%), and, among graduates aged 55 to 65, personal success (32%; 64% for graduates from lower-income families).[3]
[2148] 2. Educational context was linked to leadership and civic involvement: developing larger perspectives (28%) and an authentic learning community (25%) were linked to leadership; an authentic learning community, to giving and volunteering. Voting was not linked to any college experience.
[2149] 3. All six experiences were linked to continued learning and cultural involvement, from 22% to 42% for the six composite measures, and up to 56% for specific experiences such as discussing issues of significance with other students.
[2150] 4. Content and teaching were linked to fulfillment: intellectual skills development (66%), span of study (59%), an authentic learning community (46%), a major not tied to one occupation (44%) and engaging teaching (33%). Being made to examine the strengths and weaknesses of one's own arguments was linked to graduates being 149% more likely to report highly fulfilled lives.
[2151] 5. Breadth was linked to higher income, while major was not: graduates who took more than half their courses outside the major were 24% more likely to report a higher income across all ages (33% for graduates from lower-income families; 34% for those with lower entrance test scores). Among graduates aged 55 to 65, they were 53% more likely to report greater success and 72% more likely to report a higher income; for those from lower-income families, 72% and 75%.

[2158] What it cannot show
[2159] A callout beside the findings.
[2160] The study interviewed people once and asked them to look back. It could not do a true experiment by assigning people to colleges at random. It shows an association between these experiences and outcomes, not a causal relationship. The book takes up this question directly and compares the findings with other widely cited and respected research.
[2161] Other research points the same way
[2162] Cards, three or four across.
[2163] Alexander Astin, What Matters in College? (1993).[4] About 25,000 students at 309 institutions. The peer group is the single most powerful influence on development; faculty interaction is next.
[2164] Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, How College Affects Students (1991, 2005).[5] Decades of review. What students do in college matters far more than which college they attend.
[2165] Richard Light, Making the Most of College (2001).[6] Harvard interviews over years. Students who do best get to know faculty, study in groups, write, and learn from unlike peers outside class.
[2166] Vincent Tinto, Leaving College (1987, 1993).[7] Students stay and persist when connected to both the academic and social life of a college.
[2167] Gallup and Purdue University, Great Jobs, Great Lives (2014).[8] 30,000 graduates. Caring professors, mentors, long projects and deep involvement were linked to more than double the odds of later well-being and engagement at work.
[2168] Scott Freeman et al., "Active Learning in STEM," PNAS (2014).[9] 225 studies. Students in traditional lectures were 1.5 times as likely to fail as those in active learning classes.
[2169] George Kuh, High-Impact Educational Practices (AAC&U, 2008).[10] Seminars, research and writing benefited low-income and underrepresented students the most.
[2170] Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen, "A Brief Social-Belonging Intervention," Science (2011), and Walton, Mary Murphy, Christine Logel and others, Science (2023).[11] A brief exercise helping new students see that worries about belonging are common and pass cut the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation, and, in a study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 institutions, raised first-year completion, most where colleges offered real opportunities to belong.[12]
[2171] Why this matters now
[2172] Education in the liberal arts tradition has always aimed at the common good: lives that are good for the people living them and good for the society around them. This study shows what that looks like in practice. The experiences it examined were linked not only to graduates' income and careers but to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural lives and fulfillment.
[2173] Those are the capacities people need in a world that keeps changing. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.[13] The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[14]

While the study was under way, the Council of Independent Colleges asked graduates to write about their undergraduate education; 85 graduates of 49 colleges wrote open-ended narratives, and the book uses excerpts to illustrate the interview findings.

[2274] What stays on your device
[2275] When you take a quiz or use a tool (such as the College Scorecard or the One-Semester Plan), your answers are saved in your own web browser, using a feature called local storage. This lets you see your result again when you come back, and keeps what you typed if you leave a tool and return.
[2276] This information stays on your device. It is not sent to us or to anyone else.
[2277] To delete it: use the "Retake the quiz" link or a tool's "Reset" button, or clear this site's data in your browser's settings. Using a private or incognito window means nothing is kept after you close it.
[2278] Share links
[2279] If you use "Share my result", the link you copy contains the name of your result (for example, "Fulfillment"), so the person you send it to can see the same result card. It does not contain your individual answers or anything that identifies you. You choose whether and with whom to share it.

[2577] Parent Checklist: Choosing Well and Paying for College
[2578] What College Is Really For · Based on interviews with 1,000 college graduates, ages 25 to 65 (Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, MIT Press, 2021)
[2579] Start with what you hope for your child: to stand on their own, to have work they enjoy, to be good to other people, and to have a life that feels worthwhile. Then find colleges that can help, at a price you can manage. You don't need to have gone to college to do this well. Most families pay less than the posted price: at private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college. A grant is money that does not have to be paid back.
[2580] What to do, and when
[2581] ☐ Junior year: Meet your child's school counselor together. Say: "We want to plan for college. What should we be doing this year?" No counselor available? Ask a teacher or coach, a TRIO (Upward Bound or Talent Search) or College Advising Corps adviser, or the admissions counselor each college assigns to your area.
[2582] ☐ Junior year: Try three net price calculators together (on every college's website; they estimate what you'd pay after grants). Include one private college you assumed was too expensive.
[2583] ☐ Junior and senior years: Visit several colleges, in person if possible and online if not, to get a feel for the kind of college that feels right. Each of you bring two questions; ask students when a professor last talked with them outside class.
[2584] ☐ Summer before senior year: Help your child make a balanced list: colleges they are likely to get into and can afford, some good matches, and a few reaches.
[2585] ☐ Summer before senior year: Ask the counselor about application and test fee waivers if money is tight.
[2586] ☐ Fall of senior year: File the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the free form for federal grants, work-study and loans, also used by most states and colleges) at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens. Check whether any college also asks for the CSS Profile.
[2587] ☐ Spring of senior year: Compare aid offers side by side on net price (cost minus grants and scholarships), not loans. If an offer falls short, call the aid office and ask for a review.
[2588] ☐ The final choice: Don't decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to your child's life goals, and ask which college is most likely to give them the experiences that have a lifelong impact.
[2589] Six questions to ask every college
[2590] 1.  How many professors will know my child by name by the end of their first year?
[2591] 2.  How much room do students have to take courses outside their major and still graduate in four years?
[2592] 3.  What share of students complete a research project or internship, and is there funding for unpaid placements?
[2593] 4.  Where are your graduates ten and twenty years after graduation, not just six months out?
[2594] 5.  What share of students from families like ours graduate, and what support is there in the first six weeks?
[2595] 6.  How do students and professors spend time together outside class, and what share of students are actively involved in campus life?
[2596] Three questions to leave with your child
[2597] 1.  What kind of person do you want to be at 30?
[2598] 2.  What is one thing you believe now that you'd like a college class to challenge?
[2599] 3.  What would you study if jobs were not an immediate concern?
[2600] Why these questions: in the study, what graduates experienced in college (professors who knew them, a mentor, study across many fields, teaching that made them think, campus involvement) mattered more for their adult lives than which college they attended or what they majored in. Professors who know students and real involvement in campus life, which the study calls an authentic learning community, were linked to every part of adult life. Several links were stronger for graduates from lower-income families.

[2663] About loans, honestly
[2664] Rule of thumb: keep your child's total borrowing below what they expect to earn in their first year of work.
[2665] Federal loans first: federal Direct Loans have fixed rates and income-driven repayment (monthly payments set by what the borrower earns). Avoid private loans with variable rates.
[2666] Finishing on time saves money: a college where students finish in four years often costs less than a cheaper one where they take five or six.
[2667] Loans are not aid. They are cost moved later.
[2668] Other paths
[2669] Starting at a community college and transferring can cut costs sharply; check the transfer agreements a community college has with four-year colleges (which courses they accept) in advance. Apprenticeships, trade certifications and military benefits are real routes too.
[2670] Where to find the numbers: net price calculator (each college's website) · collegescorecard.ed.gov (cost at your income level, graduation rates, earnings) · studentaid.gov (the FAFSA) · the college's Common Data Set (class sizes, who teaches).

[2675] A 60-minute session for a department meeting, teaching and learning center or new-faculty orientation. Based on Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021).
[2676] Contents: facilitator notes (this page) · handout: the five practices · the course self-audit · discussion prompts and adaptations
[2677] Facilitator notes
[2678] Before the session: ask participants to take the two-minute Faculty quiz on the website for the course they teach most often, and to bring that course's syllabus. Print the handout and the self-audit for everyone.
[2679] Goal: each participant leaves with one change to one course for next term, and a colleague to check in with.
[2680] Time
[2681] Activity
[2682] 0–10 min
[2683] Open with a question. "Think of a professor who still matters to you. What did they do?" Take five answers and write them up. (Most will be names, conversations, being pushed to think.)
[2684] 10–20 min
[2685] The evidence. Walk through the handout. The study interviewed 1,000 graduates, ages 25 to 65, from every kind of college, and linked specific college experiences to adult leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and income.
[2686] 20–40 min
[2687] Self-audit. Each participant completes the self-audit for one course (10 min), then pairs compare (10 min): which practice is strongest, which is "Not yet," and what is one change?
[2688] 40–55 min
[2689] Discussion. Use two or three prompts from the last page. Make sure large-course, online and part-time faculty hear adaptations for their situation.
[2690] 55–60 min
[2691] Commit. Each participant writes one change and the date they'll try it, and names a colleague to check in with at midterm.
[2692] Equity note for facilitators: mentoring and advising are not shared evenly. Women faculty and faculty of color often carry more of this work, and it is rarely counted in tenure and promotion. Raise it directly, and ask what the department could do to share and recognize it.
[2693] Handout: Five Practices That Make a Lasting Difference
[2694] In the study, these practices were linked, decades later, to graduates who led, volunteered and gave, kept learning, took part in culture, described their lives as fulfilled and, over a long career, were successful. Several links were stronger for graduates from lower-income families. Together, knowing students and talking with them outside class are part of what the study calls an authentic learning community, the only kind of experience linked to all six adult outcomes.
[2695] Practice
[2696] What the study found
[2697] Ask yourself
[2698] 1. Know their names
[2699] Professors who mostly knew students' first names: 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled; 51% more likely to give a larger share of income; 31% more likely to keep learning; 22% more likely to volunteer; 21% more likely to earn an advanced degree.
[2700] How many names do I know by week four?
[2701] 2. Talk with students outside class, and not only about the course
[2702] A lasting mentor: 26% more likely to be leaders; 28% to volunteer; 38% to give a larger share of income; 23% to keep learning; 31% to be culturally involved; 34% to feel fulfilled; over a long career, 24% to report greater success. Frequent non-course conversations with professors: 37% more likely to volunteer; 31% to keep learning; 39% to be culturally involved; 25% to feel fulfilled; over a long career, 25% to report greater success. Frequent conversations about academic subjects: 22% to lead; 29% to keep learning; 32% to feel fulfilled; over a long career, 39% to report greater success.
[2703] Which students have I spoken with one-to-one this term?
[2704] 3. Make class time active
[2705] Discussion seminars in the first two years: 25% more likely to keep learning; 37% to be culturally involved; 61% to feel fulfilled. A large student role in discussion in most classes: 47% to be culturally involved; 51% to feel fulfilled. Small first-year classes: 23% to keep learning; 20% to feel fulfilled.
[2706] Who did most of the talking in my last class?
[2707] 4. Give assignments with no single right answer
[2708] 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled; 37% to be culturally involved; 25% to keep learning; 21% to earn an advanced degree. Writing papers in most classes: 51% to feel fulfilled; 49% to be culturally involved; 32% to keep learning.
[2709] Which of my assignments could two good students answer differently?
[2710] 5. Ask students to examine their own thinking
[2711] Examining the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments: 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled; 37% to keep learning. Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes: 59% to feel fulfilled; 30% to be culturally involved; 29% to keep learning.
[2712] Do my students ever have to argue against themselves?
[2713] How to read the numbers: "67% more likely" means the outcome was 67% more common among graduates who had more of the experience than among those who had less. Findings were reported only if statistically significant (p < .05), at least a 20% difference, and consistent across three age groups.
[2714] Other research agrees: Alexander Astin (25,000 students at 309 institutions) found student-faculty interaction among the strongest influences on development. Scott Freeman and colleagues (225 studies) found students in traditional STEM lectures about 1.5 times as likely to fail as those in active-learning classes. George Kuh found high-impact practices benefited low-income and underrepresented students most.

[2743] Discussion Prompts and Adaptations
[2744] Prompts
[2745] 1.  Which of the five practices does our program make easiest? Which does it make hardest?
[2746] 2.  Where in our major do students first have to argue against their own position?
[2747] 3.  Does anyone in our department carry more than their share of the mentoring and advising? Is it counted?
[2748] 4.  What would it take for every student in our major to have one faculty member who knows them well by the end of the second year?
[2749] 5.  How could our assignments bring students into real conversation with us: a proposal discussed in office hours, a draft conference, a question they bring to class from their own reading? (Assignments built this way are also harder to hand off to AI.)
[2750] Adaptations by teaching situation
[2751] Large lecture: photo roster; learn the names of students who speak; small-group office hours of eight to ten; pose a question, have students answer, discuss with a neighbor, answer again; one-minute paper at the end.
[2752] Online or hybrid: greet students by name in live sessions and reply to posts by name; a ten-minute video check-in with each student or small group in the first month; breakout rooms of three or four with one question; discussion that requires students to change or defend a position after reading replies.
[2753] Commuting and working students: arrive five minutes early and greet students by name; video office hours at early or late times; build the reading into class.
[2754] Part-time faculty: names, an opening question and one added line on every assignment need no office. Ask the department for a space to meet students.
[2755] Science, engineering and mathematics: design problems; "explain why your approach is better than another that also works"; "where could this calculation or design go wrong?"; "How might this topic contribute to the progress of humanity, or help address a problem that matters to people?"
[2756] At a research university: bring two or three undergraduates into a project with a graduate student or postdoctoral researcher; teach one course built around a real research question; record mentoring in your tenure file as teaching.

[2761] Dean's Briefing: One-Page Summary
[2762] What College Is Really For · For deans, chairs and academic leaders · Based on Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021)
[2763] The study
[2764] Interviews with 1,000 college graduates, ages 25 to 65, from every kind of college: public and private, large and small, selective and not. It linked what graduates experienced in college to six adult outcomes: leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment, and personal success (position and income). The analysis compares graduates who had more of an experience with those who had less, regardless of institution type. It shows association, not causation.
[2765] What it found
[2766] An authentic learning community (faculty contact outside class, mentoring, campus involvement) was the only experience linked to all six outcomes: leadership 25%, civic involvement 26% (giving a larger share of income 56%, volunteering 42%), continued learning 31%, cultural involvement 40%, fulfillment 46%, and success over a long career 32% (64% for graduates from lower-income families).
[2767] A mentor whose influence lasted: 26% more likely to be leaders, 34% to feel fulfilled, 38% to give a larger share of income.
[2768] More than half of courses outside the major: 24% more likely to report a higher income; over a long career, 53% more likely to report greater success and 72% more likely to report a higher income (72% and 75% for graduates from lower-income families). Major was unrelated to longer-term success.
[2769] Examining the strengths and weaknesses of one's own arguments: 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Professors who knew students' first names: 67% more likely.
[2770] Developing larger perspectives (discussing issues of significance, conversations across differences): 28% more likely to lead, 42% more likely to keep learning.

[2825] State brief
[2826] State Brief: The Outcomes a State Needs from College, and How to Measure Them
[2827] What College Is Really For · For legislators, governors' offices, state higher education boards and staff · Based on Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021)
[2828] The issue
[2829] What does a state need from its investment in higher education? Leaders for its workplaces and communities; citizens who volunteer, give and take part; a workforce that keeps learning as jobs change; people engaged in its cultural life; residents whose lives feel purposeful; and graduates who are successful over a whole career. State accountability systems most often count enrollment, completion and earnings in the first few years after college. The public's return on college comes over decades, so outcomes should be measured ten, twenty and thirty years out. Early measures set a floor: no student, and no taxpayer, should pay for a program that leaves graduates worse off. But first-job earnings measure an entry point, not a working life. Americans born in the early 1980s held an average of 9.4 jobs between ages 18 and 38 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026).
[2830] The evidence
[2831] Interviews with 1,000 college graduates, ages 25 to 65, from every kind of college, linked what they experienced in college to their adult lives ten, twenty and forty years later.
[2832] Specialized preparation helped with the first job, but the advantage faded. While some majors have higher first-job income, over time that advantage disappears, and major was unrelated to long-term success. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income across all ages; over a long career, 53% more likely to be successful and 72% more likely to report a higher income.
[2833] The students the state most wants to help benefited most. Over a long career, graduates from lower-income families who studied broadly were 72% more likely to be successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income; those who had been part of an authentic learning community were 64% more likely to be successful.
[2834] An authentic learning community (faculty contact outside class, mentoring, campus involvement) was linked to every outcome a state needs: leadership (25%), civic involvement (26%; 56% more likely to donate a larger share of income and 42% more likely to volunteer), continued learning (31%), cultural involvement (40%), fulfillment (46%) and long-term success (32% over a long career). A lasting mentor was linked to graduates being 26% more likely to be leaders.
[2835] Much of this is not expensive. Faculty time, advising and room in the curriculum are matters of policy and planning, and the same experiences are linked to students staying and finishing.
[2836] The study shows association, not causation; it interviewed graduates once and asked them to look back.
[2837] Five levers already in state hands
[2838] 1.  Report requests: ask the higher education agency for graduates' outcomes ten, twenty and thirty years after completion, not only at graduation or in the first few years.
[2839] 2.  Outcomes-based funding: if the formula rewards completion, add a measure of the student experiences linked to long-term outcomes, such as faculty contact outside class, mentoring, campus involvement or completion of a research project or internship.
[2840] 3.  Transfer and credit rules: ensure breadth (general education) courses transfer, so community college students don't lose them.
[2841] 4.  Need-based aid: protect state grants that let lower-income students take unpaid internships and research positions.
[2842] 5.  Data systems: link and keep education and wage records so the state can follow graduates across decades.
[2843] Model report-request language
[2844] The [agency] shall report to the [committee] by [date], for each public institution: (a) median earnings of graduates ten and twenty years after completion, using state wage records, and thirty years as records allow; (b) results of alumni surveys of the same graduates on leadership, civic involvement and continued learning; (c) the share of graduates who completed more than half of their credits outside their major; and (d) results of a student engagement survey on students' contact with faculty outside class, alongside the completion and earnings data already reported. Existing records and surveys shall be used wherever possible.

[2886] Measuring the long-term value of college: evidence and federal options
[2887] Bottom line
[2888] Federal accountability now looks at earnings four years after completion. That sets a sensible floor. Evidence from interviews with 1,000 graduates suggests the experiences most linked to graduates' lives decades later, and to the success of lower-income students, are not visible in early earnings. Several low-cost federal options would add the long view without discarding existing measures.
[2889] Background
[2890] Under current federal accountability rules, programs risk losing Direct Loan eligibility if graduates' median earnings four years out fall below those of high school graduates aged 25 to 34 (U.S. Department of Education, June 29, 2026). The measure captures the entry point of a working life. Americans born in the early 1980s held an average of 9.4 jobs between ages 18 and 38 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026).
[2891] The evidence
[2892] Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021): telephone interviews with 1,000 graduates ages 25 to 65, from every kind of institution, comparing graduates with more and less of specific college experiences.
[2893] Specialized preparation helped with the first job; the advantage faded, and major was unrelated to long-term success. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income across all ages; over a long career, 53% more likely to be successful and 72% more likely to report a higher income. Graduates from lower-income families who studied broadly were 72% more likely to be successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income by those ages.
[2894] An authentic learning community (faculty contact outside class, mentoring, campus involvement) was linked to all six outcomes: leadership (25%), civic involvement (26%; 56% for donating a larger share of income, 42% for volunteering), continued learning (31%), cultural involvement (40%), fulfillment (46%) and success over a long career (32%; 64% for graduates from lower-income families).
[2895] A lasting college mentor was linked to graduates being 26% more likely to be leaders and 34% more likely to report fulfilled lives.
[2896] Limits: the study is cross-sectional (one interview, looking back) and shows association, not causation. Findings are consistent with Gallup-Purdue (30,000 graduates), George Kuh's work on high-impact practices, and decades of research reviewed by Pascarella and Terenzini.

[2922] 1. The study in one page
[2923] Richard A. Detweiler's The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021) reports interviews with 1,000 college graduates, ages 25 to 65, from every kind of college: public and private, large and small, selective and not. It linked what they experienced in college to six adult outcomes: leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment, and personal success (position and income). These results are supported by extensive research by others, as documented in the book and on the website's What Matters in College page.
[2924] What it means for your students:
[2925] The study compared experiences, not kinds of colleges. What students experienced mattered more than which college they attended or their major. Positive life outcomes are associated with having professors who know them, a mentor, studying across many fields, teaching that makes them think, and involvement in campus life. Professors who know students and campus involvement together, an authentic learning community, were linked to all six outcomes.
[2926] These experiences exist at every kind of college. At small colleges they are often built in; at large universities students may need to seek them out through honors programs, office hours and campus involvement; at community colleges, through small sections and transfer-ready general education.
[2927] Lower-income students had the most to gain. Among graduates from lower-income families, over a long career, taking more than half their courses outside their major was associated with being 72% more likely to be successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income.
[2928] The posted price is rarely the price. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself.
[2929] On prestige, the research is mixed. For most graduates, less than people think: the earnings advantage of a more selective college was small once students' own abilities were taken into account (Dale and Krueger). For some, attending an Ivy-Plus college rather than a flagship public university raised the odds of being among the highest 1% of earners and of working at the most prestigious finance or consulting firms; while Ivy-Plus graduates are overrepresented among Fortune 500 chief executives, 88.4% of these CEOs had not attended an Ivy-Plus college, and attending one had little effect on average earnings (Chetty, Deming and Friedman).
[2930] Methods of this research are on the website's Research page.

[2963] 3. Can't visit? How to find out from home
[2964] Virtual tours and online information sessions: most colleges offer both. Ask the six questions in the chat.
[2965] Ask for a current student: many admissions offices will connect you with one by email or video. Ask: "What is something students here decided that actually changed?" and "When did a professor last talk with you outside class?"
[2966] Watch a class online: some colleges allow it. Count the minutes students speak.
[2967] Read what students write: the student newspaper, club pages and student government minutes.
[2968] Check the numbers: collegescorecard.ed.gov (cost at your income level, graduation rates); the college's net price calculator; the Common Data Set (search the college's name and "Common Data Set") for class sizes.
[2969] Fly-in programs: some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit. Search the college's name and "fly-in program."
[2970] 4. Family handout: the real price, and what to do when
[2971] Most families pay less than the posted price. Use each college's net price calculator to see your price after grants. A grant is money you don't pay back.
[2972] When
[2973] What to do
[2974] Junior year
[2975] Meet the school counselor with your child. Try three net price calculators, including one private college you assumed was too expensive.
[2976] Summer before senior year
[2977] Make a balanced list: likely and affordable, matches, a few reaches. Ask about fee waivers.
[2978] Fall of senior year
[2979] File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens. Check whether any college also asks for the CSS Profile.
[2980] Spring of senior year
[2981] Compare aid offers on net price (cost minus grants), not loans. Ask the aid office for a review if an offer falls short.
[2982] Ask every college: How do students and professors spend time together outside class? How many professors will know my child by name by the end of the first year? How much of the degree can be taken outside the major? Where are graduates ten years out? What share of students from families like ours graduate?
[2983] Ask your child: What kind of person do you want to be at 30? What would you study if jobs were not an immediate concern?
[2984] Loans: keep total borrowing below expected first-year earnings; use federal loans before private ones.
[2985] The final choice: don't decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to your child's life goals, and ask which college is most likely to give them the experiences that have a lifelong impact.
[2986] 5. First in your family to go to college? Five steps
[2987] You're not behind. Many students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it.
[2988] 1.  Talk to your school counselor this year. Say: "I want to go to college, and I'm the first in my family. What should I be doing now?" If you can't get time with a counselor, ask a teacher, coach or librarian; look for a free program such as TRIO Upward Bound or Talent Search or College Advising Corps; or email the admissions counselor each college assigns to your area.
[2989] 2.  Try three net price calculators. Include at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
[2990] 3.  Ask about fee waivers. If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free.
[2991] 4.  File the FAFSA early, in the fall of senior year as soon as it opens. Some aid runs out. The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the free form for federal grants, work-study and loans; most states and colleges use it too. Help is at studentaid.gov.
[2992] 5.  Keep every path open. Starting at a community college and transferring is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home.
[2993] "Since a child, I wanted to become a doctor, but many were not supportive." Her liberal arts education, she writes, "paved the way for me to sustain and ultimately become a physician." — Theressa, physician, in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 112
[2994] 6. A 30-minute group session
[2995] For a junior seminar, an advisory period or a college night.
[2996] Time
[2997] Activity

[3009] 7. Words colleges use
[3010] Major: the subject you study most.
[3011] Elective: a course you choose, not one your major requires.
[3012] General education: courses every student takes across different fields.
[3013] Seminar: a small class built on discussion.
[3014] Liberal arts: not a list of majors; a way of being educated, with broad study across the arts and sciences, discussion, writing and close contact with professors.
[3015] Adviser: the person who helps you choose courses and plan.
[3016] Mentor: an adult who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals.
[3017] Authentic learning community: a college where professors and students know one another and spend time together outside class as well as in it.
[3018] Office hours: set times when a professor is available to any student.
[3019] Internship: a short job in a field you're considering.
[3020] Net price: the published price minus grants and scholarships.
[3021] Grant: money you don't pay back. Loan: money you do.
[3022] FAFSA: the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the free form for federal grants, work-study and loans (studentaid.gov).
[3023] Work-study: a part-time campus job paid partly by federal aid.
[3024] Fee waiver: lets you skip an application or test fee you can't afford.
[3025] Graduation rate: the share of students who finish, usually within six years.
[3026] Transfer agreement: says which community-college courses a four-year college will accept.
[3027] Common Data Set: a standard report most colleges post online, with class sizes, costs and graduation rates.
