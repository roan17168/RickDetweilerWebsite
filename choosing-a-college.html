For Students Choosing a College

Rankings can't tell you what college will do for you.

Majors, price, location, dorms. They all matter. None of them tell you who you'll be at 28 or in the years that follow. Start with what you want out of college, then find the schools that can deliver it.

Financial Reality

The price on the website is not the price you'll pay.

Most students pay less than the published price, often much less. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the published tuition.[3] Some colleges with high published prices cost students from lower-income families less than a public university does. You can find your real price before you apply:

  • Net price calculator: Every college must post one on its website. Enter your family's information and it estimates what you would pay after grants. Search for the college's name and "net price calculator."
  • College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov): From the US Department of Education. It shows each college's average yearly cost for families at your income level, and how many of its students graduate.
  • Federal Student Aid (FAFSA - studentaid.gov): Fill out the federal financial aid form that colleges use. It is needed for most grants, work-study jobs and loans.
First in your family to go to college? Read this

You're not behind. Many students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it. These five steps matter most:

  1. Talk to your school counselor this year: Say: "I want to go to college, and I'm the first in my family. What should I be doing now?" Counselors expect this question.
  2. Try three net price calculators: Include at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
  3. Ask about fee waivers: If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free. Your counselor can request the waivers.
  4. File the FAFSA early: File in the fall of your senior year as soon as it opens. File early, because some aid runs out.
  5. Keep every path open: Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home. The questions on this site work for all of them.

Can't visit? Most colleges offer virtual tours and online information sessions. Many will put you in touch with a current student by email. Some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit; search for the college's name and "fly-in program."

Before you fall for a campus: Dealbreakers & Good signs

Dealbreakers first:

  • Can my family and I afford this through graduation, after grants and scholarships?
  • Does it have what I might study, with room to change my mind?
  • Do students like me finish here? (Vincent Tinto finds that feeling connected is a big part of finishing.)
  • Will I feel safe, supported and like I belong?
  • Are housing, health, disability and advising services good enough for what I need?

Good signs at any college:

  • Professors know students and talk with them outside class.
  • In class, students speak, write, argue and solve problems.
  • You have room to study outside your major.
  • Students work with people who think and live differently.
  • You get to do real things: research, projects, internships, performances, service.
Words colleges use (Plain translations)
  • Major: The subject you study most.
  • Seminar: A small class built on discussion.
  • Humanities: Literature, history, philosophy, languages and the arts.
  • Adviser: The person who helps you choose courses and plan.
  • Mentor: An adult who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals.
  • Office hours: Set times when a professor is available to any student, no appointment needed.
  • Internship: A short job in a field you're considering.

A meaningful life in a world of change

College is more than getting through classes to get a job. It's where you find out what you care about, who you want to become and what you have to give. In the study behind this site, the same experiences were linked to higher income and to leadership, volunteering, continued learning and a fulfilled life.

The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[2] An education that teaches people to learn, to think and to work with others prepares them for all of it.
