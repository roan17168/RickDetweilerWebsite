Understanding College The Research About
For College Leaders

Your mission statement makes a promise. Does your budget keep it?

An analysis of 241 colleges' mission statements found them promising much the same things: leaders, engaged citizens, lifelong learners, fulfilled and successful people. Interviews with 1,000 college graduates show which experiences are linked to those outcomes. Many depend on how the institution uses its time and what it rewards. In the study, several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families.

Check your priorities

Five questions. Two minutes. Answer for how your institution behaves, not how it describes itself.

Three things worth knowing

  • Major matters less over time than what students experience. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher adult incomes; the figure was 33% for graduates from lower-income families.
  • The experiences linked to good lives are also linked to staying. Connection to academic and social life is associated with persistence and completion.
  • Many practices depend on how time and requirements are organized. Protecting faculty time requires deciding what other work can be reduced, not simply adding new duties.
The retention case

The experiences linked to graduates' lives are also linked to whether students stay. Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to persist when connected to both the academic and social life of the institution. George Kuh found that first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research and service learning were associated with better grades and returning for a second year, with the largest benefits for underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students.

Every student who stays is a student who can graduate, and tuition the institution does not have to replace.

Other research agrees

Alexander Astin's study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found peer influence to be a powerful influence on development, with faculty interaction next. Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini's review of decades of research concluded that what students do in college matters more than which college they attend. Gallup-Purdue found that only 3% of graduates strongly agreed they had received all six key supportive and hands-on experiences.

It has been done, at every kind of institution

George Kuh and colleagues studied 20 institutions whose graduation rates and student engagement were higher than predicted for the students they enrolled. They included research universities, regional universities, liberal arts colleges and historically Black universities. They described shared conditions such as a lived mission, focus on student learning, clear pathways to success and shared responsibility for educational quality.

Examples from Student Success in College (2005) describe those institutions at the time of the study; they are not claims about their current practices.

Institutional Checklist

Eight practices, many of which cost little in new money

Estimate how many students get each experience, name one accountable owner, identify the usual cost of improvement, and choose a next step. Unknown is a finding. Where possible, break results out for commuters, transfers, part-time and first-generation students.

Use Widespread for roughly three-quarters or more, Uneven when access depends on major or individual staff, Rare for roughly one-quarter or fewer, and Unknown when reliable data are unavailable. These are suggested starting points, not standards from the study.

One-page Board Summary

What the evidence means for us

  • Graduates with a mentor whose influence lasted were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults.
  • Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income; the figure was 33% for graduates from lower-income families.
  • The same experiences are linked to students staying and graduating, and matter most for students who arrive with the least.
A message for legislators and donors

A graduate's first-year earnings tell you about a first job, not a working life. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The experiences studied—professors who know students, a mentor, broad study and teaching that makes students think—are linked to how graduates' lives turn out. We are measuring how many of our students get them, and working to make sure every student does.

Alumni survey: a template

Ask alumni about adult life and about their college experiences. Adapt and test these questions before use; they are modeled on the kinds of questions in the study, not its exact wording.

  • In the past five years, have you led a team, organization, project or group?
  • In the past year, how much have you volunteered or given to charities or causes?
  • Have you taken a course, earned a credential, or read or studied regularly to learn something new?
  • How often have you attended arts events or discussed the arts and ideas?
  • How meaningful and satisfying is your life today?
  • What is your current position level and household-income range?
  • Did you have a mentor? Did most professors know you by name? How often did you speak with faculty outside class? How much did you study outside your major?
Sample promotion and tenure language
Teaching includes work with students outside class: advising and mentoring, supervising research and creative projects, and supporting learning beyond the course. Candidates should describe this work and its results, and reviewers should weigh it as part of teaching, not as service.
For enrollment and admissions leaders

Prepare tour guides and counselors to answer questions with specific, recent stories: who does the talking in class, what a first-year student can be in charge of, how many professors know students by name, and graduation rates for students who receive Pell Grants. Be ready to explain price and financial aid clearly.

On the student scorecard, a website claim alone counts as "Don't know yet." Cite the research as Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021).

A Meaningful Life in a World of Change

The experiences linked to graduates' income are also linked to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning and fulfillment. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030. Aligning mission, budget, promotion criteria and reporting with these experiences helps prepare graduates for a whole life and gives the public evidence of the promise colleges make.
