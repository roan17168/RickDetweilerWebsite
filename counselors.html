This page is built for the time you actually have: a way to help each student start with the life they want, and the evidence to back you up.

Section 1

The study in one page

Richard A. Detweiler’s The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021) reports interviews with 1,000 college graduates, ages 25 to 65, from every kind of college: public and private, large and small, selective and not.1 It linked what they experienced in college to six adult outcomes: leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment, and personal success (position and income). These results are supported by extensive research by others, as documented in the book and on the website’s What Matters in College page.

What it means for your students: The study compared experiences, not kinds of colleges. What students experienced mattered more than which college they attended or their major. Positive life outcomes are associated with having professors who know them, a mentor, studying across many fields, teaching that makes them think, and involvement in campus life. Professors who know students and campus involvement together, an authentic learning community, were linked to all six outcomes. These experiences exist at every kind of college. At small colleges they are often built in; at large universities students may need to seek them out through honors programs, office hours and campus involvement; at community colleges, through small sections and transfer-ready general education.

Lower-income students had the most to gain. Among graduates from lower-income families, over a long career, taking more than half their courses outside their major was associated with being 72% more likely to be successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income.2

The posted price is rarely the price. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself.

On prestige, the research is mixed. For most graduates, less than people think: the earnings advantage of a more selective college was small once students’ own abilities were taken into account (Dale and Krueger).3 For some, attending an Ivy-Plus college rather than a flagship public university raised the odds of being among the highest 1% of earners and of working at the most prestigious finance or consulting firms; while Ivy-Plus graduates are overrepresented among Fortune 500 chief executives, 88.4% of these CEOs had not attended an Ivy-Plus college, and attending one had little effect on average earnings (Chetty, Deming and Friedman).4 Methods of this research are on the website’s Research page.

Student handout: six questions to ask any college

Ask in person, in an online session, by email, or find the answer yourself.

Questions to ask any college, with space to write answers for three colleges
Question College 1 College 2 College 3
How many professors will know me by name by the end of my first year?      
How much of my degree can I take outside my major and still finish in four years?      
In a typical first-year class, who does the talking?      
What share of students do research, an internship or a long project, and is there money for those who can’t work unpaid?      
What share of students like me graduate, and what will it cost me each year after grants?      
Do students and professors spend time together outside class?      
What would I be part of every week?      

A website claim alone counts as “Don’t know yet.” Ask a student, read a syllabus, check the numbers.

Section 3

Can’t visit? How to find out from home

  • Virtual tours and online information sessions: most colleges offer both. Ask the six questions in the chat.
  • Ask for a current student: many admissions offices will connect you with one by email or video. Ask: “What is something students here decided that actually changed?” and “When did a professor last talk with you outside class?”
  • Watch a class online: some colleges allow it. Count the minutes students speak.
  • Read what students write: the student newspaper, club pages and student government minutes.
  • Check the numbers: collegescorecard.ed.gov (cost at your income level, graduation rates); the college’s net price calculator; the Common Data Set (search the college’s name and “Common Data Set”) for class sizes.
  • Fly-in programs: some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit. Search the college’s name and “fly-in program.”
Section 4

Family handout: the real price, and what to do when

Most families pay less than the posted price. Use each college’s net price calculator to see your price after grants. A grant is money you don’t pay back.

What to do and when
WhenWhat to do
Junior year Meet the school counselor with your child. Try three net price calculators, including one private college you assumed was too expensive.
Summer before senior year Make a balanced list: likely and affordable, matches, a few reaches. Ask about fee waivers.
Fall of senior year File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens. Check whether any college also asks for the CSS Profile.
Spring of senior year Compare aid offers on net price (cost minus grants), not loans. Ask the aid office for a review if an offer falls short.

Ask every college:

  • How do students and professors spend time together outside class?
  • How many professors will know my child by name by the end of the first year?
  • How much of the degree can be taken outside the major?
  • Where are graduates ten years out?
  • What share of students from families like ours graduate?

Ask your child:

  • What kind of person do you want to be at 30?
  • What would you study if jobs were not an immediate concern?

Loans: keep total borrowing below expected first-year earnings; use federal loans before private ones.

The final choice: don’t decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to your child’s life goals, and ask which college is most likely to give them the experiences that have a lifelong impact.

Section 5

First in your family to go to college? Five steps

You’re not behind. Many students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it.

  1. Talk to your school counselor this year. Say: “I want to go to college, and I’m the first in my family. What should I be doing now?” If you can’t get time with a counselor, ask a teacher, coach or librarian; look for a free program such as TRIO Upward Bound or Talent Search or College Advising Corps; or email the admissions counselor each college assigns to your area.
  2. Try three net price calculators. Include at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
  3. Ask about fee waivers. If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free.
  4. File the FAFSA early, in the fall of senior year as soon as it opens. Some aid runs out. The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the free form for federal grants, work-study and loans; most states and colleges use it too. Help is at studentaid.gov.
  5. Keep every path open. Starting at a community college and transferring is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home.

Section 6

A 30-minute group session

For a junior seminar, an advisory period or a college night.

A 30-minute group session, by time and activity
TimeActivity
0–5 min Ask: “What do you want to be true of your life at 30?” Take a few answers.
5–12 min Quiz: students take the Choosing a College quiz on their phones (two minutes, no sign-up) and read their result.
12–22 min Pairs: compare results. Each student picks one question from their result to ask a college.
22–28 min Whole group: the real price. Show one net price calculator live, at a private college students assume is out of reach.
28–30 min Exit question: each student writes one college they’ll ask, and the question they’ll ask.

Families’ night version: parents take the Parents quiz while students take theirs, then compare results together.

Section 8

Words colleges use

Read all 19 terms
Major:
the subject you study most.
Elective:
a course you choose, not one your major requires.
General education:
courses every student takes across different fields.
Seminar:
a small class built on discussion.
Liberal arts:
not a list of majors; a way of being educated, with broad study across the arts and sciences, discussion, writing and close contact with professors.
Adviser:
the person who helps you choose courses and plan.
Mentor:
an adult who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals.
Authentic learning community:
a college where professors and students know one another and spend time together outside class as well as in it.
Office hours:
set times when a professor is available to any student.
Internship:
a short job in a field you’re considering.
Net price:
the published price minus grants and scholarships.
Grant:
money you don’t pay back.
Loan:
money you do.
FAFSA:
the Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the free form for federal grants, work-study and loans (studentaid.gov).
Work-study:
a part-time campus job paid partly by federal aid.
Fee waiver:
lets you skip an application or test fee you can’t afford.
Graduation rate:
the share of students who finish, usually within six years.
Transfer agreement:
says which community-college courses a four-year college will accept.
Common Data Set:
a standard report most colleges post online, with class sizes, costs and graduation rates.
