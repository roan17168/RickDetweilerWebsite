For Legislators & Policymakers

"Why should public money support 'broad' education instead of direct workforce training?"

The fear of looking anti-jobs or anti-family is real. But the evidence shows that narrow workforce training is a risky investment. A broadly educated citizenry produces stronger economies, healthier communities, and more resilient democracies.

The Short Answer

Liberal arts graduates are more civically active, more entrepreneurial, and more economically resilient than narrowly trained peers. They drive innovation, build businesses, and remain employable across decades of industry change. The "state's interest" — economic strength, civic stability, democratic health — is best served by an education that produces thinkers, not just workers.

"...assess whether there is a statistical relationship between liberal arts content of study and educational context and the long-term life outcomes of graduates, utilizing a large sample of people from a diverse range of types of institutions."
— Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs

Detweiler's data shows that liberal arts graduates are significantly more likely to start businesses, lead innovation, and drive economic development in their communities. They are not just employees — they are creators of jobs.

Public investment in broad education yields exponential returns: not just in tax revenue, but in entrepreneurship, leadership, and community vitality across an entire career.

The research found that liberal arts graduates are markedly more likely to volunteer, lead nonprofits, vote, and engage in civic life. These are the people who hold communities together — who serve on school boards, lead religious congregations, and run for local office.

Funding broad education is funding the social fabric of your district.

"Workforce training" sounds practical, but the historical evidence is sobering. The Prussian model of narrow vocational tracking produced citizens highly vulnerable to economic disruption. When industries shift — and they always do — narrowly trained workers struggle to adapt.

Detweiler's data shows the opposite for liberal arts graduates: they are more resilient to economic change, retrain more easily, and are less reliant on state social services over a lifetime. Broad education is workforce insurance.
