For Faculty

Which parts of my teaching will still matter to students in thirty years?

Most professors did not choose this work only to cover content. They chose it to make a difference in students' lives, and the evidence says they do. In this study, specific teaching and mentoring practices were linked, decades later, to graduates who led, volunteered, kept learning, and described their lives as fulfilled.

Evidence-Based Teaching

Five practices that make a lasting difference

These practices have a significant relationship to important life outcomes for all students, and they matter even more for students with the least. In the study, several links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families. George Kuh's research on high-impact practices found the same pattern: underrepresented and low-income students benefited most from practices like these.[16]

1. Know their names

Why it matters: Graduates whose professors mostly knew their first name were 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Knowing students by name was also linked to adult civic involvement.

Ask yourself: How many names do I know by week four?

Adaptations across teaching contexts

Small class: Use name cards or a seating chart for the first month.

Large course: Use the photo roster, learn the names of students who speak, and ask teaching assistants to learn their sections. Use a name every time you call on someone.

Students who work or commute: Arrive five minutes early and greet students by name as they come in.

Part-time faculty: Names cost nothing and need no office. This is the most portable of the five.

2. Talk with students outside class, and not only about the course

Why it matters: Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Graduates who often talked with professors outside class about non-academic subjects were 37% more likely to volunteer more as adults. Alexander Astin's study of 25,000 students found student-faculty interaction to be among the strongest influences on development.

Ask yourself: Which students have I spoken with one-to-one this term?

Adaptations across teaching contexts

Small class: Require one ten-minute office-hours visit from every student in the first three weeks.

Large course: Hold small-group office hours for eight to ten students at a time, and ask teaching assistants or learning assistants to do the same.

Students who work or commute: Offer video office hours, early-morning or evening times, and the five minutes after class. When a student asks about the course, ask one question about their plans.

Part-time faculty: Use a short video meeting or the minutes after class, and ask the department for a space to meet students.

3. Make class time active

Why it matters: Graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively as adults, and 37% more likely to stay closely involved in arts, ideas, and other cultures. In science, engineering, and mathematics, a review of 225 studies by Scott Freeman et al. found that students in traditional lectures were about 1.5× as likely to fail as students in classes that used active learning.[17]

Ask yourself: Who did most of the talking in my last class?

Adaptations across teaching contexts

Small class: Open each class with a question, and take answers before you give yours.

Large course: Pose a question, have students answer, discuss it with a neighbor, and answer again. Richard Light's work popularized the one-minute paper at the end of class as a simple way to hear from everyone.

Students who work or commute: Build the reading into class. Read a short passage together, then discuss it, rather than assuming it was done at home.

Part-time faculty: All of these work in any room, with no extra preparation beyond the question.

4. Give assignments with no single right answer

Why it matters: Graduates whose coursework often involved questions with no single right answer were 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, and 25% more likely to keep learning actively. Being required to write papers in most classes showed similar links.

Ask yourself: Which of my assignments could two good students answer differently?

Discipline-specific adaptations

Humanities & Social Sciences: Replace one quiz with a short paper that takes a position.

Science, Math & Engineering: Set a design problem, or ask students to explain why their approach is better than another one that also works.

Professional Programs: Use a case with competing goods. In nursing, a patient's wishes against a protocol. In business or accounting, a judgment call in reporting.

5. Ask students to examine their own thinking

Why it matters: Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled: about two and a half times as likely. This was the largest difference in that part of the study.

Ask yourself: Do my students ever have to argue against themselves?

Discipline-specific adaptations

Any course: Add one line to every assignment: "State the strongest objection to your answer."

Science, Math & Engineering: "Where could this calculation or design go wrong?"

Professional Programs: "What would a skeptical colleague say about this decision?"

Who carries this work: Mentoring loads & cultural taxation

Mentoring, advising, and conversations outside class take time, and that time is not shared evenly. Studies of faculty workload find that women faculty do more of this kind of service than men,[18] and faculty of color often describe extra mentoring and service demands, sometimes called "cultural taxation." The work is rarely counted in tenure and promotion reviews. If these practices matter, departments and institutions need to recognize, share, and reward them. The College Leaders checklist includes rewarding this work.

A meaningful life in a world of change

Gallup found that graduates who had a professor who cared about them, made them excited about learning, and encouraged their hopes were more than twice as likely to be engaged at work and to be thriving in their lives.[19] In this study, the same kind of teaching was linked, decades later, to graduates who led, volunteered, kept learning, and found their lives fulfilling.

The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[20] What your teaching builds in them will not: the ability to learn, to think, to work with people unlike themselves, and to contribute to the common good.
