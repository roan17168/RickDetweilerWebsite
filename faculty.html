For faculty

Which parts of your teaching will matter in thirty years?

We asked 1,000 graduates about their experiences with professors. Then we asked how their lives turned out. A handful of practices stood out, and most of them are about how you teach and whether you know your students, not which subject you teach. They matter even more for students who arrive with the least: several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families.

Whatever your setting, a seminar of fifteen, a lecture of three hundred, a section of students who work and commute, or a course you teach part-time, each practice has a version that fits.

The five practice cards

The five practices

These practices have a significant relationship to important life outcomes for all students, and they matter even more for students with the least. In the study, several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families. George Kuh’s research on high-impact practices found the same pattern: underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students benefited most from practices like these, and were the least likely to take part. Vincent Tinto’s research ties students’ connection with faculty to whether they stay and finish.

1. Know their names

Why it matters: graduates whose professors mostly knew their first name were 67% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Knowing students by name was also linked to adult civic involvement.

Ask yourself: how many names do I know by week four?

Small class: use name cards or a seating chart for the first month.

Large course: use the photo roster, learn the names of students who speak, and ask teaching assistants to learn their sections. Use a name every time you call on someone.

Students who work or commute: arrive five minutes early and greet students by name as they come in.

Part-time faculty: names cost nothing and need no office. This is the most portable of the five.

Other settings
2. Talk with students outside class, and not only about the course

Why it matters: graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Graduates who often talked with professors outside class about non-academic subjects were 37% more likely to volunteer more as adults. Alexander Astin’s study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found student-faculty interaction to be among the strongest influences on students’ development.

Ask yourself: which students have I spoken with one-to-one this term?

Small class: require one ten-minute office-hours visit from every student in the first three weeks.

Large course: hold small-group office hours for eight to ten students at a time, and ask teaching assistants or learning assistants to do the same.

Students who work or commute: offer video office hours, early-morning or evening times, and the five minutes after class. When a student asks about the course, ask one question about their plans.

Part-time faculty: use a short video meeting or the minutes after class, and ask the department for a place to meet students. Whether part-time faculty have the time and space for this is an institutional question; see the College Leaders checklist.

Other settings
3. Make class time active

Why it matters: graduates who took small discussion classes in their first two years were 25% more likely to keep learning actively as adults, and 37% more likely to stay closely involved in arts, ideas and other cultures. In science, engineering and mathematics, a review of 225 studies found that students in traditional lectures were about one and a half times as likely to fail as students in classes that used active learning.

Ask yourself: who did most of the talking in my last class?

Small class: open each class with a question, and take answers before you give yours.

Large course: pose a question, have students answer, discuss it with a neighbor and answer again. Richard Light’s work with Harvard faculty popularized the one-minute paper at the end of class as a simple way to hear from everyone.

Students who work or commute: build the reading into class. Read a short passage together, then discuss it, rather than assuming it was done at home.

Part-time faculty: all of these work in any room, with no extra preparation beyond the question.

Other settings
4. Give assignments with no single right answer

Why it matters: graduates whose coursework often involved questions with no single right answer were 61% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled, and 25% more likely to keep learning actively. Being required to write papers in most classes showed similar links.

Ask yourself: which of my assignments could two good students answer differently?

Humanities and social sciences: replace one quiz with a short paper that takes a position.

Science, math and engineering: set a design problem, or ask students to explain why their approach is better than another one that also works.

Professional programs: use a case with competing goods. In nursing, a patient’s wishes against a protocol. In education, a classroom dilemma. In business or accounting, a judgment call in reporting.

Other settings
5. Ask students to examine their own thinking

Why it matters: graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled: about two and a half times as likely. This was the largest difference in that part of the study.

Ask yourself: do my students ever have to argue against themselves?

Any course: add one line to every assignment: “State the strongest objection to your answer.”

Science, math and engineering: “Where could this calculation or design go wrong?”

Professional programs: “What would a skeptical colleague say about this decision?”

Other settings

Bring the big questions in, whatever you teach. Graduates who discussed philosophical and ethical questions in most of their classes, regardless of major, were 59% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. This is about what happens in a chemistry, nursing or accounting course as much as a humanities one. In an education course, ask what school is for. In nursing, ask who should decide.

Who carries this work. Mentoring, advising and conversations outside class take time, and that time is not shared evenly. Studies of faculty workload find that women faculty do more of this kind of service than men, and faculty of color often describe extra mentoring and service demands, sometimes called “cultural taxation.” The work is rarely counted in review. If these practices matter, departments and institutions need to recognize, share and reward them. The College Leaders checklist includes rewarding this work.

Course self-audit

One course, five checks

What it’s for. The audit looks at one course you teach against the five practices in the study, and produces one or two changes to try next term. It is for your own use and is not a performance review.

When to use it. Near the end of a term, when the course is fresh, or while planning the next one. It takes about 20 minutes.

How to use it

  1. Choose one course, ideally one you teach often.
  2. For each practice, describe what happens now. Use evidence, not intentions. The prompts below suggest what to look at.
  3. Rate each practice: Strong, Partway or Not yet.
  4. Note any real constraint. Class size, a required syllabus, a lab schedule, students’ work hours, or no office. The practice cards give versions for small classes, large courses, students who work or commute, and part-time faculty.
  5. Choose one or two changes, not five. Start with the practice that is lowest and easiest to move. Write exactly what you will do.
  6. Decide how you’ll know it worked. For example: names known by week four, or minutes per class students spend talking.
  7. Ask your students. At midterm, ask two anonymous questions: “Does the professor in this course know your name?” and “In this course, how often do you do the thinking, not just listen?” Compare their answers with your own ratings.
  8. Share it with one colleague. Comparing audits is the fastest way to find good ideas that work in your discipline.
  9. Repeat next term with the same course or another one.
