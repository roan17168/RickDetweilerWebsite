For Current College Students

Am I getting the experiences that last?

Class is the required part of college. The research says the part that shapes your life mostly happens around it: professors who know you, courses outside your major, discussions that make you think, and campus involvement.

Five High-Impact Habits

The five moves that matter most

These links held for every kind of major, engineering and professional majors included, and were strongest for students from lower-income backgrounds.

1. Time with a professor outside class

What the research found: Graduates with a mentor were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Talking with professors outside class about non-academic topics was linked to higher income, volunteering, and fulfillment.

Start small: Go to office hours once with a real question: "Can you help me understand one thing from Tuesday's lecture?"

Adaptations for your situation

At a big university: Start with your TA; they are often the bridge to the professor. Go to office hours in the first weeks of the term before lines form.

Short on time / commuter: Stay two minutes after class or email a thoughtful question. Ask for a 5-minute video check-in.

Already doing this? Ask a professor to supervise an independent study or research project.

2. Taking courses well outside your major

What the research found: Taking more than half of courses outside the major was linked to a 24% greater likelihood of higher income (and 33% for graduates from lower-income families). Major was unrelated to long-term success.

Start small: Save one elective slot next semester for a subject you are genuinely curious about.

Adaptations for your situation

In set-requirement majors (e.g. engineering, nursing): Use your required general education electives deliberately rather than treating them as a checklist.

3. Classes where you examine your own arguments

What the research found: Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own views were 149% more likely to report fulfilled lives.

Start small: In your next paper or problem set, add one paragraph that begins: "The strongest argument against my view is..."

4. Being part of something on campus

What the research found: Graduates closely involved with faculty and other students outside class were 56% more likely to give a larger share of their income and 42% more likely to volunteer more as adults.

Start small: Pick one student group, campus job, lab, or volunteer project and show up consistently every week.

5. Real conversations with people different from you

What the research found: Spending time with peers from different cultures and beliefs was linked to cultural involvement and lifelong curiosity.

Start small: Have lunch or work on a group project with a classmate whose background or perspective is unfamiliar to you.

When college gets hard: Three things to do first

If you're failing a class, overwhelmed by work, or wondering if you should drop out:

  1. Tell one professor or adviser immediately: Say: "I'm struggling this term and I don't want to fall behind. What are my options?" They deal with this every week.
  2. Use tutoring and academic support: Tutoring is free at almost every college. Students who use it are the ones who finish.
  3. Protect your credits: Dropping one class before the deadline is far better than failing three. Talk to financial aid before withdrawing.

A meaningful life in a world of change

College is more than getting through classes to get a job. It's where you find out what you care about, who you want to become and what you have to give. Graduates in this study who got to know professors, studied widely, were pushed to think and got involved were more likely, decades later, to earn more, to lead, to volunteer, to keep learning and to say their lives felt fulfilled.

The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[7] What lasts is the person college helps you become.
