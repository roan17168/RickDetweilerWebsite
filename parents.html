For Parents

College is a big investment. What should it buy?

Whatever your family can pay, start with two things: what college will really cost you, and what your child should get for it. The research points to things most college comparisons leave out: whether professors will know your child, how widely your child will study, and how much they will be asked to think.

Paying for College

The price on the website is rarely the price families pay.

Most families pay less than the published price. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the published tuition.[8]

If money is tight:

You may pay little or nothing. Some selective private colleges cost lower-income families less than a regional state university. Use Net Price Calculators early.

If paying most of it:

Look for merit scholarships. Many colleges offer substantial discounts based on academic record, regardless of family income.

First time doing this? A parent's timeline
  • Junior year: Talk with your child's school counselor. Try three net price calculators together, including one at a private college you assumed was too expensive.
  • Summer before senior year: Help your child make a balanced list: affordable probables, matches, and reaches. Ask about fee waivers.
  • Fall of senior year: File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens. Tax information is brought in directly from the IRS.
  • Spring of senior year: Compare financial aid award letters side by side. Look at the net price (cost minus free grants), not the loans.
About student loans, honestly

Borrowing can make sense if kept within reasonable bounds:

  • Rule of thumb: Keep your child's total borrowing below what they expect to earn in their first year of work.
  • Federal loans first: Federal Direct Loans have fixed rates and income-driven repayment protections. Avoid private loans with variable rates whenever possible.
  • Four-year completion rate: A college where students finish in four years often costs less than a cheaper college where they take five or six.
Other paths: Community college transfer & apprenticeships

Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year institution can dramatically cut degree costs. Verify articulation agreements in advance. Apprenticeships, trade certifications, and military benefits are also viable routes. The questions on this site apply to any college your child considers along any of these paths.

Questions Worth Asking

Questions to ask colleges & your child

Questions to ask the college:

  1. How many professors will know my child by name by the end of their first year?
  2. How much room do students have to take courses outside their major and still graduate in four years?
  3. What share of students complete a research project or internship, and is there funding for unpaid placements?
  4. Where are your graduates ten years after graduation, not just six months out?

Questions to leave with your child:

  1. What kind of person do you want to be at 30?
  2. What is one thing you believe now that you'd like a college class to test?
  3. What would you study if jobs were not an immediate concern?

A meaningful life in a world of change

Ask parents what they want most for their children as adults, and roughly nine in ten say to be financially independent and to have work they enjoy. Two-thirds say it is extremely important that their children be honest and ethical.[14]

The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[15] The best preparation is not a narrow ticket to a first job. It is an education that helps your child stand on their own, find work worth doing and become someone others can count on.
