For Parents

"Is this investment going to give my child a secure, independent, and meaningful future?"

Your fears are real — debt, unemployment, failure to launch. The evidence offers a different conversation, one where "starting salary" is exposed as the wrong metric for what really matters.

The Short Answer

Decades of empirical research show that liberal arts graduates do not just "catch up" to their vocationally-trained peers — they often surpass them in income, leadership, civic engagement, and life satisfaction by mid-career. Your child's college experience matters more than their first job title.

"Do the content and educational context of a liberal arts education actually make a difference in the ways that people live their lives? It is to this question that we now turn."
— Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs

One of the most striking findings in Detweiler's research is that early-career salary comparisons paint a misleading picture. While vocationally-trained graduates often earn slightly more in their first jobs, liberal arts graduates demonstrate stronger income growth, leadership advancement, and adaptability over the long term.

This happens because the skills cultivated by a liberal arts education — critical thinking, communication, ethical reasoning, and the ability to learn quickly across domains — become increasingly valuable as careers progress and industries change.

As automation and AI reshape the job market, the skills most resistant to disruption are precisely those a liberal arts education cultivates:

  • Complex, non-linear problem solving across disciplines
  • Ethical and contextual reasoning
  • Empathy and human-centered communication
  • The capacity to learn and adapt rapidly

A narrow, vocational degree may train your child for today's job — but a liberal arts education prepares them for the job that doesn't exist yet.

The conversation around "Is college worth it?" is too often reduced to a single number: starting salary versus tuition. Detweiler's research reframes this calculation by tracking life outcomes 10, 20, and 40 years post-graduation.

What he found: graduates exhibit higher rates of life satisfaction, civic leadership, independence, and meaningful careers. These outcomes don't appear on a salary spreadsheet — but they define whether your child builds a life of consequence.
