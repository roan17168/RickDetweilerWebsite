How should government measure what a college is worth?

State and federal accountability systems count enrollment, completion, and earnings in the first years after college. Those measures set a floor: no student, and no taxpayer, should pay for a program that leaves graduates worse off. But a floor is not a ceiling. Interviews with 1,000 graduates found that what students experienced in college is linked to how their lives went ten, twenty, and forty years later.

Policy Foundations

Three things worth knowing

  • Major matters only in the short term. Experience matters over a career: Specialized preparation helped graduates get their first jobs, but that advantage faded. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher adult incomes.[29]
  • The students who benefit most get these experiences least: Among graduates from lower-income families, broad study was linked to a 33% greater likelihood of higher income.[30] Contact with faculty and campus involvement show the same pattern.[31]
  • Most of what matters for life impact is not expensive: Faculty time, advising, and room in curricula cost policy and planning rather than new capital buildings, and they are directly linked to college completion.

In Washington: The federal floor & long-term data

The federal floor: Under federal accountability rules, programs risk losing Direct Loan eligibility if graduates' median earnings four years out fall below high school graduates aged 25–34.[45] But early earnings measure only the entry point.

Four ways to add the long view:

  1. Support the College Transparency Act: Bipartisan legislation creating secure student-level data systems to track earnings across 10- and 20-year horizons.[47]
  2. Fund high-impact student experiences: Protect Federal Work-Study, TRIO, and McNair Scholars programs that fund faculty mentorship and research for lower-income students.
  3. Incorporate longitudinal data in Baccalaureate & Beyond surveys: Add metrics on faculty contact and curricular breadth to existing federal 10-year tracking.[48]
  4. Require engagement metrics in accreditation reviews: Supplement completion rates with verified measures of faculty contact and experiential learning.
Questions for legislative hearings & agency oversight
  1. "How will we know whether programs that pass the 4-year earnings test serve graduates well at ten and twenty years?"
  2. "Do state data show how many students have a faculty mentor or take courses outside their major?"
  3. "Which state or federal programs pay for faculty contact and campus involvement for low-income students, and what is happening to their funding?"

Model Legislative Report Request Language:

"The [agency] shall report to the [committee] by [date], for each public institution: (a) median earnings of graduates ten years after completion, using state wage records; (b) the share of graduates who completed more than half of their credits outside their major; and (c) results of a student engagement survey on students' contact with faculty outside class, alongside the completion and earnings data already reported. Existing records and surveys shall be used wherever possible."

For a hearing or floor statement: "A first job is a floor, not a ceiling. A meaningful life in a world of change

Whether you lead with workforce needs, civic health, or long-term resilience, the public return on higher education compounds over a graduate's forty-year career, not the first year out. The study found that the same experiences serve all three priorities: they are linked to higher career earnings, community leadership, and lifelong learning.

The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.[50] Measuring and funding educational practices that build adaptability protects the public's investment.
