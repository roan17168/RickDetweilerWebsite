For legislators and policymakers

How should government measure what a college is worth?

State and federal accountability systems count enrollment, completion and earnings in the first years after college. Those measures set a floor: no student, and no taxpayer, should pay for a program that leaves graduates worse off. But a floor is not a ceiling. Interviews with 1,000 graduates1 found that what students experienced in college is linked to how their lives went ten, twenty and forty years later, including their income, leadership, civic involvement, and continued learning.

The case

Three things worth knowing

Major matters only in the short term.

The educational experience matters in both the short and the long term. Specialized preparation helped graduates get their first jobs, and that advantage faded. Over a career, major was unrelated to success in most cases. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults.

The students who benefit most get these experiences least.

Among graduates from lower-income families, the same experience was linked to a 33% greater likelihood of higher income. Other research finds that low-income, first-generation and underrepresented students gain the most from close contact with faculty and similar practices, and are the least likely to have it.

Most of what matters most for life impact is not expensive.

The experiences in question are professors who know students, a mentor, room for courses outside the major and involvement in campus life. They cost faculty time and planning more than buildings, and they are also linked to students finishing college, which governments already pay for.

Under every result

The completion case

Governments already pay for completion. The experiences linked to long-term outcomes are linked to finishing college too. Vincent Tinto’s research found that students who are connected to faculty and to other students are more likely to stay. George Kuh found that first-year seminars, writing-intensive courses, undergraduate research and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year, and that low-income, first-generation and underrepresented students benefited most but were the least likely to take part.4 Every student who stays protects the tuition, state aid and federal aid already spent on them.

What the study covers, and what it does not

Who was interviewed. 1,000 people with bachelor’s degrees, ages 25 to 65, interviewed once by telephone. Six hundred came from a nationally representative sample of graduates; 400 more came from the alumni of twelve liberal arts colleges, added so that graduates with many of these experiences would be well represented. The findings held within both groups.

Different kinds of students. The links held across gender, race and ethnicity, entrance test scores and family income. Graduates of color were 11% of the sample, so findings for specific groups are less precise.

Who is not covered. The study did not include students who left college without a degree, students who attended only a community college, or adults who returned to finish a degree later. For those students, research on persistence (Tinto) and on high-impact practices (Kuh), and community college engagement surveys, are better guides.

Cause. The study shows that these experiences and outcomes go together, and other research affirms these findings. The Research page explains the checks the book made.

Other research

Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, reviewing decades of research on college impact, concluded that what students do in college matters more than which college they attend. Measures of student engagement tell a state more than rankings or selectivity.

Vincent Tinto’s research on why students leave found that students who are connected to faculty and peers are more likely to finish.

Alexander Astin’s study of about 25,000 students at 309 institutions found that student involvement, with peers and with faculty, drove development more than institutional type or resources.

Gallup and Purdue University’s survey of 30,000 graduates concluded that what students did in college, and how they experienced it, related more strongly to their later work and well-being than where they went.

Why first-year earnings are a floor, not a ceiling

A graduate’s first-year earnings tell you about a first job, not a working life. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.7 The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.8 Measures built on the first years after graduation therefore describe a small part of what a degree is for.

Where students go matters less than what they do there. Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger followed earnings for more than twenty years and found that the advantage of a more selective college fell to close to zero once students’ own abilities were accounted for.9 Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman found that attending an Ivy-Plus college raised a student’s chance of reaching the top 1% of earners, but that the effect on average earnings was small, and that 88.4% of the chief executives of Fortune 500 companies did not attend one.10

What states can measure instead: whether students get the experiences linked to long-term outcomes.23 In the study behind this site, graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 72% more likely to report greater success and 75% more likely to report a higher income, and the largest single difference in the whole study was in fulfillment.3

Employers already say this. In the Graduate Management Admission Council’s 2026 survey of corporate recruiters, communication, problem-solving and adaptability ranked among the skills most in demand.6 The federal accountability provisions in the Working Families Tax Cuts Act measure a first job; they do not measure these capacities.5

In Washington

In Washington

The federal floor. Under the 2025 federal budget law, a program loses eligibility for federal Direct Loans if, in two of three years, its graduates’ median earnings four years after completion are below those of working adults aged 25 to 34 with only a high school diploma. The Department of Education will first calculate the test in early 2027. But it measures only the first years of a working life.

Four ways to add the long view

Data that follows graduates longer.

Federal law bars the Department of Education from keeping a database of individual student records, so federal earnings data come mostly from the first years after college. The College Transparency Act, introduced in the Senate in July 2025 with sponsors from both parties, would create a secure student-level data system that could report outcomes over longer periods.

Fund the experiences.

Federal Work-Study pays for campus jobs that put students beside the same people every week. TRIO programs and the McNair Scholars Program pay for mentoring and undergraduate research for low-income and first-generation students. These are the experiences the research links to long-term outcomes.

Answer the cause question.

This study could not prove cause; a study that follows students from entry can. Federal surveys such as Baccalaureate and Beyond already follow graduates for about ten years. Adding questions about faculty contact, breadth of study and civic life would let them test these findings.

Count what students experience.

Ask that federal reporting or accreditation reviews include a measure of student engagement beside earnings and completion.

Questions for hearings

  • How will we know whether programs that pass the four-year earnings test serve graduates well at ten and twenty years?
  • Do any federal data show how many students have a faculty mentor or take courses outside their major?
  • Which federal programs pay for faculty contact, mentoring and campus involvement for low-income students, and what is happening to their funding?
One-page brief

Five measures to add to early-earnings data

What it’s for. The brief helps you find out what your state already measures about college outcomes, decide which longer-term measures to ask for, and put the request on one page for an agency, a committee or a system board.

When to use it. Before a budget hearing, a performance-funding review, or a meeting with your state’s higher education agency or system office.

How to use it

  1. Ask what is already collected. Your state’s higher education agency or system office can tell you. Many states link college records to state wage records through a statewide longitudinal data system, which can show earnings ten years out. Many public colleges also give a student engagement survey, such as the National Survey of Student Engagement or, at community colleges, the Community College Survey of Student Engagement, which asks how often students talk with faculty outside class.
  2. Mark each measure Yes, Partly or No.
  3. Note who could collect it. The agency, the institutions, or a survey partner.
  4. Choose your priorities. Pick one or two measures to ask for first. Earnings at ten years and share of students with a mentor are often the easiest to start. Define “mentor” with an existing survey question, such as Gallup’s “I had a mentor who encouraged me to pursue my goals and dreams,” so colleges report the same thing.
  5. Fill in the request at the bottom of the form.
  6. Use the questions from your result in the hearing or meeting.
  7. Follow up in writing with the brief attached, and ask when the first report will be available.

Our request

We ask to report for by , alongside the completion and early-earnings data already reported, so that the state can see the long-term return on its investment.

Language for a report request (adapt to your state)

“The [agency] shall report to the [committee] by [date], for each public institution: (a) median earnings of graduates ten years after completion, using state wage records; (b) the share of graduates who completed more than half of their credits outside their major; and (c) results of a student engagement survey on students’ contact with faculty outside class, alongside the completion and earnings data already reported. Existing records and surveys shall be used wherever possible.”

For a hearing or floor statement

“A first job is a floor, not a ceiling. Graduates who studied widely and had professors who knew them earned more, led more and gave more decades later, and the gains were largest for students from families with little money. We should keep holding colleges to the floor, and start measuring the rest.”
