For parents

College is a big investment. What should it buy?

Whatever your family can pay, start with two things: what college will really cost you, and what your child should get for it. The research points to things most college comparisons leave out: whether professors will know your child, how widely your child will study, and how much they will be asked to think.

What college really costs

The price on the website is rarely the price families pay.

Most families pay less than the published price. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the published tuition. Some grants are based on what a family can pay, and some on a student’s record.

If money is tight

  • You may pay little or nothing. Some colleges with high published prices give the most aid, and can cost families with low incomes less than a nearby public university.
  • Find your real price in about fifteen minutes with each college’s net price calculator. Every college must post one; search for the college’s name and “net price calculator.”
  • College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov), from the US Department of Education, shows each college’s average yearly cost for families at your income, its graduation rate and its graduates’ typical debt.
  • Be wary of any college where many students leave with debt and no degree. College Scorecard shows both.

If you will pay most of the price

  • Look for merit scholarships. Many colleges offer them to strong students whatever a family’s income. Many net price calculators include them; where one doesn’t, ask the financial aid office.
  • Look at honors colleges within public universities: small classes and close contact with professors, at a public university’s price.
  • Compare the full four-year cost, not only the first year, and ask whether a scholarship continues if grades dip.
  • Take a generous offer from a less famous college seriously. For a capable, motivated student, the research below suggests the name on the diploma matters less than what happens there.

First time doing this? A parent’s timeline

Read more

You don’t need to have gone to college to help your child get there. Here is what parents do, and when.

  1. Junior year. Talk with your child’s school counselor, in person, by phone or by email. You can say: “We’re new to this. What should we be doing this year?” Try three net price calculators together, including one at a college you assume is too expensive.
  2. Summer before senior year. Help your child make a balanced list: a few colleges where they are likely to be admitted and that you can afford, several in the middle, and a few reaches. If money is tight, ask the counselor about fee waivers; most students who qualify can apply to colleges for free.
  3. Fall of senior year. File the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens for the next school year, usually by October 1. Some aid runs out. You and your child each create an account, and with your permission your tax information is brought in directly from the IRS. Some private colleges also ask for the CSS Profile. A parent without a Social Security number can still create an account and complete their part.
  4. Spring of senior year. Compare financial aid offers side by side: grants are free money, work-study is a campus job, and loans must be repaid. If an offer seems low, call the financial aid office and ask whether it can be reviewed. Colleges do adjust offers.
  5. By May 1. Most colleges ask for an enrollment decision by this date.

About loans, honestly

Read more

Student loans are in your child’s name. For 2026–27, a first-year student can borrow up to $5,500 in federal loans, and up to $31,000 over all of college. They have lower interest rates and more protections than other loans.

Parent PLUS loans are in the parent’s name. For loans made on or after July 1, 2026, parents can borrow up to $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total for each child, at a fixed interest rate of 9.07% plus a fee of about 4.2%. The parent must repay them; they cannot be moved to the child later. Borrow only what you are confident you can repay before you retire.

A common rule of thumb: keep your child’s total borrowing below what they expect to earn in their first year of work.

The cheapest offer is not always the cheapest college. A college where students finish in four years can cost less than one where they take five or six.

Other paths

Read more

College is not the only good route. Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college can lower the cost of a degree; check in advance that credits will transfer. Apprenticeships and trade programs lead to skilled work, often with pay while training. Military service can help pay for college later. The questions on this site apply to any college your child considers, on any of these paths.

What “liberal arts” means.

You will see “liberal arts” on this site. It does not mean art, and it has nothing to do with politics. It means an education that includes study across many subjects, sciences included, alongside a major. Most American bachelor’s degrees include some of it. Understanding College explains more, and it is worthwhile to take a little time to understand the differences among colleges and the experiences they offer that will have a positive impact on your child’s adult life.

For every parent

Check these first, and how to find out

Can we afford this through graduation, after grants and scholarships?

Ask the college about the scholarships, grants and work-study available to a student like yours. Look at the college’s net price calculator and College Scorecard.

Do students like ours finish here, and in four years?

College Scorecard or College Navigator. Ask admissions for the graduation rate for students like yours.

Will our child be safe and supported, and will faculty take an interest in them?

Ask the questions in the “Well-being” panel above, by visit, call or email.

If you can’t visit. Nearly every question on this page can be asked by email. A short note to the admissions office works: “We’re helping our child choose a college and have a few questions. [Your question.] Could you point us to the right person?” Many colleges will also put families in touch with a current student or parent.

Worth knowing: the name matters less than many parents fear. Economists Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger followed graduates’ earnings for more than 20 years. Once students’ own abilities and ambitions were taken into account, attending a more selective college made close to no difference to earnings, except for Black and Hispanic students, for whom it did. A capable, motivated student does not need to attend a prestigious college to have a great future. Choose the one where your child will be known, challenged and supported.

A conversation to have before the decision

  1. Ask your student to take the Choosing a College quiz and show you the result.
  2. Share yours.
  3. Where they differ, do not settle it. Use both sets of questions when you talk with colleges, by visit, call or email.
