Understanding college

Four thousand colleges. One idea underneath almost all of them.

American colleges look wildly different from one another: a community college down the road, a state university with 40,000 students, a college of 1,500 on a hill. Underneath, most of them were built on the same idea about what an education is for. Knowing that idea, and knowing which colleges still practice it, is the most useful thing a person can learn before choosing one, working at one, or funding one.

The kinds of colleges

In plain terms: Colleges are built for different jobs. The label tells you what the place was designed to do, though of course they do vary. It does not tell you what a student will experience there.

The kinds of colleges, what each was built to do and what to know
Kind What it is Built mainly to Worth knowing
Community college Two-year public college, open to nearly everyone, low cost Start a degree, train for a local career, or transfer to a four-year college Small classes are common. Most students commute and many work, so life outside class takes effort to find.
Liberal arts college Smaller four-year college, usually residential, primarily undergraduate Teach undergraduates broadly, in close contact with professors Fewer majors than a university. Emphasis on the total educational community. Professors are rewarded for the quality of their teaching and out-of-class involvement with students; a moderate emphasis on research.
Regional or comprehensive university Mid-sized public or private university with bachelor’s and master’s programs Educate the region’s teachers, nurses, managers and other professionals Generally teaching-centered, often with more working and transfer students. Classes may be smaller, so professors can get to know students. Because many students work and commute, experiences outside class take deliberate planning by the university and by students.
Research university Large university with doctoral programs, laboratories and professional schools Produce new knowledge as well as teach Many courses and majors. Professors balance teaching with research and graduate training, and large introductory courses often use teaching assistants. Honors colleges and smaller programs can offer a close community inside the large university.
Specialized college Art, music, engineering, health sciences, business, religious study, military academies Prepare students deeply for one field The choice of major is largely made before arriving.
Career and for-profit college Programs aimed at a specific job, often short and often online Deliver a credential quickly Outcomes vary widely. Check completion rates and debt.

Other labels cut across these kinds: public or private, religious or secular, historically Black colleges and universities, tribal colleges, women’s colleges, online universities. An historically Black college can be a liberal arts college or a research university.

The point that matters: the experiences linked to good lives after college can be found at every kind of institution. The odds of finding them without having to hunt are not the same everywhere: at liberal arts colleges these experiences are usually built into how the college works, and in the study liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. At larger or more complex institutions they are often available too, but students may need to seek them out through honors programs, knocking on professors’ doors, and pursuing involvement in cocurricular programs.

One idea, refined over millennia and nearly four centuries old in America

In plain terms: The American college was never designed as job training. It was designed to prepare people to think, to take part in public life and to keep learning as the needs of the individual and of humanity change over time. That design is called the liberal arts, an education for the common good which benefits both the individual and society, and it is the root of almost every American bachelor’s degree.

Read the full history

Ancient roots. Liberal arts education began in Greece about 3,500 years ago and evolved through Rome, North Africa and the Middle East, and Europe. Its purpose was to educate people for the common good: to benefit both the individual and important needs of society. Over that long span the subjects studied steadily grew, but the approach always involved a close relationship between teachers and students.

1636 to 1769: the first American colleges. Nine colleges were founded in colonial America, beginning with Harvard. Their founders brought the model of Oxford and Cambridge: broad study, in a community where students and teachers lived and learned together.

1810: a different model appears. Prussia created a new kind of university to serve the state. Students chose a specialty early and trained for it. The model spread across Europe and, through its empires, most of the world. It is why students in many countries still apply to study one subject from the first day.

1828: America decides. Yale’s faculty asked whether American colleges should copy the Prussian universities. Their answer, known as the Yale Report, was no. A republic like the United States, they argued, needs widely educated citizens, not a trained or specialized few:

“Our republican form of government renders it highly important that great numbers should enjoy the advantage of a thorough education.… Merchants, manufacturers, and farmers, as well as professional gentlemen, take their places in our public councils. A thorough education ought therefore to be extended to all these classes.”

This “thorough education” was to include the full range of human knowledge, and it was to happen in a learning community where contact between students and professors was “frequent and familiar.” Their report became the template for the hundreds of colleges founded as the country moved west.

1862 onward: the model stretches. The Morrill Acts created the land-grant universities to advance agriculture and engineering, and still required “liberal and practical education.” Johns Hopkins, founded in the 1870s, brought research and doctoral study to America, and placed them after an undergraduate liberal arts degree, not in place of it.

The result is something close to unique in the world: an education that comes before a person specializes or starts a profession. In the United States a future engineer, nurse or accountant was still expected to spend part of a bachelor’s degree on writing, history, science and ideas outside the field. Unlike in most of the world, an aspiring physician or lawyer had to complete an undergraduate course of study before beginning professional education. General education requirements at a comprehensive or research university are the liberal arts tradition, surviving under another name.

What “liberal arts” means, and what it doesn’t

In plain terms: “Liberal arts” is not a list of majors. It is a way of being educated: what you study, and how.

Three common mistakes

  • “Liberal arts means the humanities.” It never did. The tradition has always included mathematics and the sciences. A physics major at a liberal arts college is getting a liberal arts education.
  • “Liberal arts means an education for nothing in particular.” Liberal arts means an education not tied to one job, which is a different thing. In the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.
  • “Liberal arts has something to do with politics.” “Liberal” here comes from the Latin for “free.” It meant the education suited to a free person who must make their own judgments.

What it is: content plus context

Liberal arts education: what is studied and how it is studied
Part In practice
What is studied (content)
Not tied to one occupation A major in a field of knowledge, not a job title
The full span of knowledge Substantial study outside the major; seeing how fields connect
Intellectual skills Writing, reasoning, information seeking, working on questions with no single right answer
How it is studied (context)
Engaging teaching Discussion, seminars, students doing the thinking
Larger perspectives Serious contact with people and ideas unlike one’s own
An educational community Faculty, students and staff involved with one another outside class as well as in it

And one purpose runs through all six: the education should serve the common good. That is, it benefits both the individual and society.

The research behind this site found that the second half of that table, the context, was the most consistently linked to how graduates’ lives turned out. A college can list every subject in the catalog and still not deliver an education that has a long-lasting impact on graduates’ lives.

The pressure to make college pay off fast

In plain terms: College is expensive, so people reasonably ask what it pays. The trouble starts when the only answer anyone measures is the paycheck.

Families are encouraged to compare starting salaries by major. Rankings count earnings soon after graduation. Lawmakers tie funding to job placement. Colleges respond the way any organization responds to what they are measured on: they add narrow career programs, enlarge majors until there is little room for anything else, and trim the parts of college that are hard to count, such as student time with faculty.

Each step is understandable. Together they move American colleges toward the model the Yale faculty turned down in 1828.

The evidence suggests the trade is a poor one. In the study behind this site, specialized preparation helped with the first job, but that advantage faded. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher incomes later. Richard Detweiler has put the point this way: “Strengthening graduates’ readiness for the job market is not only appropriate, it is essential. But it is a floor, not a ceiling.”

I asked a group of students to describe the person they hoped to be ten years from now. One answered quietly: “I want to be someone my community can count on. Someone who gives back what was given to me.” Around the table, nearly 20 students offered their own versions of the same answer, and not one mentioned salary, status or job title.

— Richard A. Detweiler, “What Students Know About College That Critics Don’t,” Inside Higher Ed, September 2, 2026

The case for the liberal arts college

In plain terms: The experiences that matter can happen anywhere. At a liberal arts college, they are the standard, not the exception.

The study behind this site interviewed graduates of every kind of institution. The experiences linked to leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and success were linked to them everywhere. A student at a large university who seeks out professors, studies widely and gets involved can have all of them.

The difference is in the odds:

  • Twice as likely. In the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide the six experiences that have a long-term impact on graduates’ lives.
  • 45% against 24%. In a Gallup-Strada national study, 45% of graduates of colleges with fewer than 5,000 students said “my professors care about me as a person.” At larger institutions, only 24% did.
  • 3%. In the Gallup-Purdue study of 30,000 graduates, only 3% strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.

The reasons are structural. A liberal arts college doesn’t have graduate students competing for faculty time. Professors are rewarded for teaching and advising. The scale is small enough that students and faculty cannot avoid one another. As the book puts it:

“…the odds of having these experiences with a long-term life impact is far higher at the smaller liberal arts college.”

For a student who will persistently knock on doors and ask, a large university can deliver the same educational experiences. But that is hard for most young people; it is time-consuming and invariably intimidating. For the many who will not, a place where it happens without asking is a real advantage.

What employers say

In plain terms: Employers keep asking for the same things: people who can communicate, solve problems, think critically and adapt. Those are the abilities a broad education builds, and as AI takes over routine technical work, employers expect them to matter more.

Communication comes first. In the 2026 Corporate Recruiters Survey of more than 600 recruiters of business-school graduates in 39 countries, the skills employers weighed most in hiring were communication (64%), problem-solving (62%) and adaptability (60%). Graduates were least prepared in AI skills, grit, managing people and emotional intelligence.

The widest gaps are in thinking and communicating. The QS Global Employer Survey 2026 found that the largest gaps between what employers need and what graduates bring were in problem solving, communication and critical thinking. QS’s summary of its employer data puts it plainly: graduates “struggle most with critical thinking and judgement, not subject knowledge.”

Analytical thinking is the core skill. In the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on more than 1,000 employers representing over 14 million workers, seven in ten employers called analytical thinking essential. Technology skills such as AI and big data are expected to grow fastest by 2030, but creative thinking, resilience and flexibility, and curiosity and lifelong learning are also expected to rise in importance. Employers expect 39% of workers’ existing skills to be transformed or become outdated in the same period.

Human skills are hardest to automate. The Forum’s New Economy Skills report found that tasks drawing on empathy, creativity, leadership and curiosity have only about a 13% potential for transformation by AI. Fewer than half of executives believed schools develop creativity, curiosity or resilience well.

Breadth counts. In a 2023 survey of 1,010 executives and hiring managers, nearly nine in ten agreed that exposure to a wide range of topics and viewpoints is an important contributor to workforce preparedness. In an earlier survey cited in the book, 80% said every student should acquire broad knowledge in the liberal arts and sciences, whatever the major. And 93% agreed that a candidate’s capacity to think critically, communicate clearly and solve complex problems matters more than the undergraduate major.

The gap is in “soft” skills. In a 2025 survey of 651 company vice presidents and directors in the United States and Britain, 56% blamed weak soft skills for entry-level workers being unprepared, up from 50% a year earlier; among U.S. leaders it was 58%. The National Association of Colleges and Employers found the same gap: about 80% of graduating seniors rated themselves proficient in communication and critical thinking, but only about 54% to 56% of employers rated new graduates proficient.

Why prestige and rankings are a poor guide

In plain terms: A famous name opens some doors at the start. For most graduates it changes little after that. And the rankings that measure fame are measuring fame.

What prestige does do

  • It may open early doors. A well-known name on a first résumé may get attention for jobs or graduate school.
  • It matters in a few fields. The most selective law firms, investment banks and consulting firms recruit heavily from a short list of colleges.
  • It feels good. It’s nice to have bragging rights, especially for parents.

What prestige does not do

It is not where most leaders come from. In 2023, 88% of Fortune 100 chief executives had not attended an Ivy League college as undergraduates.

Its advantage is early and narrow. The most cited recent study of elite colleges is by economists Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman (2023). It found that about ten years after graduation, students who attended an Ivy-Plus college were more likely to reach the top 1% of earners, since they were more likely to have positions in high-paying fields such as prestigious law or consulting. However, across all graduates, the effect on average earnings was small and not statistically significant. Studies that follow graduates longer find no lasting advantage. Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger tracked earnings for more than 20 years. They found that the effect of attending a more selective college fell to close to zero once students’ own abilities and ambitions were accounted for, though it remained for Black and Hispanic students. Jack Mountjoy and Brent Hickman found that a selective college’s earnings premium faded to zero after a few years in the workforce. In the study behind this site, what graduates experienced in college, not where they went, was linked not only to a higher long-term income but, importantly, to how their lives turned out more broadly.

It does not predict a good life. Gallup-Purdue found little to no difference in well-being or engagement at work between graduates of highly ranked colleges and everyone else. A Stanford-affiliated review, A Fit Over Rankings (2018), concluded that selectivity is not a reliable predictor of learning, job satisfaction or well-being.

What the rankings measure

  • Reputation is built in. In the U.S. News formula, a fifth of a college’s score has come from a survey asking presidents, provosts and admissions deans to rate other colleges, most of which they have never visited.
  • The data can be wrong. In 2022 Columbia fell from 2nd to 18th after one of its own professors showed that figures it had submitted were inaccurate. Since then, leading law and medical schools, including Yale and Harvard, have stopped taking part.
  • They leave out what matters. No major ranking measures whether professors know students, how much students write, or how graduates are living at 45.

Sadly, three-quarters of college-bound students believe rankings give useful information about colleges. They are being told who is famous, by the famous.

What does drive long-term outcomes

In plain terms: How a student goes to college matters more than where.

Three large bodies of research point the same way:

The study behind this site

Among 1,000 graduates aged 25 to 65, involvement with faculty and other students outside class, broad study, and teaching that demanded thought were linked to all six adult outcomes. Major was not.

Gallup-Purdue

Among nearly 30,000 graduates, those who had a professor who cared about them, made them excited about learning and encouraged their goals more than doubled their odds of being engaged at work. Those who had an internship that used their learning, a long project and deep involvement in activities doubled their odds as well. Where they went to college made little difference.

Fifty years of scholarship

Alexander Astin, Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, Richard Light and Vincent Tinto, studying different students with different methods, each concluded that what students do in college, and with whom, outweighs the type or standing of the institution.

And this matters most for students with the least. In the study behind this site, the link between these relationships and later success was strongest for graduates who grew up in lower-income families.

On “fit.” Fit is often described as a feeling on a campus tour. A better definition: a college fits when the student is likely to have the educational experiences that are related to their long-term life goals.

Five things most people get wrong

  1. “The more selective, the better the education.” Selectivity measures who gets in. It says nothing about what happens next.
  2. “Pick the major that pays.” The majors with the highest starting salaries change from decade to decade, and in the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.
  3. “A big university has more opportunity.” It has more offers. Whether a given student gets any, or whether having a huge buffet of choices is actually beneficial to the student, is a different question.
  4. “The sticker price is the price.” Most students pay less than the published tuition, sometimes far less. The figure to compare is the net price after grants and scholarships, and colleges are required to provide a calculator for it.
  5. “Liberal arts graduates can’t get jobs.” Employers say the opposite, and the long-term income evidence favors breadth.

How to read any college in ten minutes

Whatever kind of college it is, five questions get past the brochure:

  1. How many professors will know a first-year student by the end of the year?
  2. How much of the degree can be taken outside the major?
  3. In a typical class, who does the talking?
  4. What share of students do research, an internship or a long project, and is there money for those who cannot work unpaid?
  5. What share of students like this one finish, and what does it cost them after aid?

Closing

Start at the end.

Most people choose a college, or judge one, or fund one, by starting with what is easiest to see: the name, the rank, the price, the first salary. The evidence says to start at the other end.

01

First, decide what the education is for.

A life with leadership in it, involvement in a community, curiosity that lasts, work that matters and the means to live well.

02

Second, ask what the evidence says produces that.

Professors and students involved with one another outside class. Study across many fields. Teaching that makes students think.

03

Third, look for the colleges where that happens, and only then choose.

That is what college is really for.
