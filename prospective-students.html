For Prospective Students

"Who will I become, and will this education help me build a meaningful, authentic life?"

You're not afraid of unemployment in the abstract — you're afraid of becoming the wrong version of yourself. Every message you've heard frames college as a credential transaction. The evidence offers something different: an encounter with a future self that feels real and traceable.

The Short Answer

The "best" college isn't the one with the highest ranking — it's the one that helps you become the person you want to be. Detweiler's research tracked 1,000 graduates over 40 years and found that the way you learn matters more than what you major in. Broad, inquiry-based education produces lives of consequence, fulfillment, and adaptability.

"Do the content and educational context of a liberal arts education actually make a difference in the ways that people live their lives? It is to this question that we now turn."
— Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs

Universities love to advertise starting salaries — but starting salary is the wrong metric for the rest of your life. Detweiler's research shows that while vocational graduates may earn slightly more in their first job, liberal arts graduates often surpass them in income, leadership, and fulfillment by mid-career.

Why? Because the world changes. Your major prepares you for today's job. Your way of thinking prepares you for the next forty years of jobs that don't exist yet.

AI is rapidly automating technical, repetitive tasks. What it cannot replicate is empathy, ethical judgment, cross-cultural communication, and the ability to ask the right questions.

These are exactly the skills cultivated by a liberal arts education. Graduates report not only more career success but a deeper sense of purpose — which research links to long-term life satisfaction.

According to Detweiler's data, the most powerful predictors of a "life of consequence" aren't rankings or amenities. They are:

  • Small class sizes that allow real discussion
  • Faculty who engage with students outside class
  • Encouragement to explore across disciplines
  • A culture of asking big questions, not memorizing answers

When you tour a campus, ask: "Will I be known here? Will I be challenged to think, not just to perform?"
