For Students Choosing a College

Rankings can’t tell you what college will do for you.

Majors, price, location, dorms. They all matter. None of them tell you who you’ll be at 28, or whether you’ll love your work, have people who count on you, or feel like your life means something. Start with the life you want, then find the colleges that can help you build it.

Does a famous name matter?

Researchers disagree, and both sides have evidence:

  • For most graduates, less than people think. Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger followed earnings for more than 20 years. The earnings advantage of a more selective college was small once students’ own abilities were taken into account.1
  • For some. Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman did find that attending an Ivy-Plus college rather than a flagship public university increases a student’s chances of reaching the top 1% of earners and of working at a prestigious finance or consulting firm. Ivy-Plus graduates are also overrepresented among Fortune 500 chief executives. However, the great majority of those CEOs, 88.4%, did not attend an Ivy-Plus college, and graduating from an Ivy-Plus college had little effect on average earnings.2
    Ivy-Plusmeans the eight Ivy League universities plus Stanford, MIT, Duke and the University of Chicago.

Two things you might be wondering about

The price on a college’s website is almost never the price you’ll pay, and you can find your real price before you apply.

What will it really cost?

Most students pay less than the posted price, often much less. At private nonprofit colleges, 9 in 10 first-year students get grants from the college itself, and on average those grants cover more than half of the posted tuition.3

grantis money you don’t pay back.
Some colleges with high posted prices cost students from lower-income families less than a public university does.

  • Net price calculator: Every college has one on its website. Put in your family’s information and it estimates what you’d pay after grants.
    net priceis the posted price minus grants and scholarships.
    Search for the college’s name and “net price calculator.”
  • College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov): From the US Department of Education. It shows what families at your income level actually pay at each college, and how many students graduate.
  • FAFSA (studentaid.gov): The Free Application for Federal Student Aid, the free form that decides federal grants, work-study jobs and loans; most states and colleges use it for their own aid too. What is the FAFSA?
    work-studyis a part-time campus job paid for partly by federal aid.

You’re not behind. Lots of students figure this out without anyone at home who has done it, and five steps matter most.

First in your family to go to college?
  1. Find someone to help you this year: If you have a school counselor, say: “I want to go to college, and I’m the first in my family. What should I be doing now?” Counselors expect this question. If you can’t get time with a counselor, see the box below this list.
  2. Try three net price calculators: Include at least one private college you assumed was too expensive.
  3. Ask about fee waivers: If money is tight, you can usually apply to colleges and take the SAT or ACT for free. Your counselor can request the waivers.
    fee waiverlets you skip a fee you can’t afford.
  4. File the FAFSA early: File in the fall of senior year as soon as it opens. Some aid runs out.
    FAFSA(Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the free form that decides federal grants, work-study jobs and loans; most states and colleges use it for their own aid too. File it at studentaid.gov, which also has step-by-step help. It opens each fall for the following school year.
  5. Keep every path open: Starting at a community college and transferring to a four-year college is a real path. So is a four-year college close to home. The questions on this site work for all of them.

No counselor, or one with hundreds of students? You can still get help, free:

  • A teacher, coach, librarian or family friend who went to college. Ask: “Can you help me figure out where to apply and how to pay for it?” Most people are glad to be asked.
  • Free college advising programs. The federal TRIO programs Upward Bound and Talent Search help students from lower-income families and first-generation students prepare for and apply to college. College Advising Corps places recent college graduates as advisers in many high schools. Ask at school whether either is there, or search for “college access program” and your town.
  • The colleges themselves. Every admissions office has a counselor assigned to your area. Email them your questions; answering them is their job.
  • A nearby community college. Its advising and financial aid offices will often help anyone in the community.
  • Trusted free websites: studentaid.gov (federal financial aid) and collegescorecard.ed.gov (costs and graduation rates).

What graduates told us

We talked with 1,000 people who finished college ten, twenty and forty years ago.4 The ones who were leading things, giving back, still learning, doing well and happy with their lives weren’t mostly the ones from famous colleges or “safe” majors. They were the ones who had certain kinds of experience in college:

  • Professors who knew them. Someone noticed them, talked with them outside class, and stuck with them. Many had a mentor whose influence lasted for years.
  • Room to explore. They took lots of classes outside their major, including ones they never expected to like.
  • Classes that made them think. They talked, wrote and argued, and they had to question their own ideas, not only memorize.
  • Being part of something. A team, a club, a lab, a paper, a cause. Something they showed up for every week.
  • People unlike themselves. Friends and classmates who saw the world differently.

The first and fourth of these, professors who know you and being part of something, are what the study calls an authentic learning community: a college where students and faculty are involved with one another outside class as well as in it. Of everything the study measured, it was the experience most consistently linked to every part of adult life.

“I got to study the sciences while also jumping into the theater. I got to write my thesis on religion while studying abroad in the U.K. I was surrounded by an eclectic mix of peers who pushed me.” — Steve, actor (The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, p. 139)

These experiences happen at every kind of college. At some, they’re built in; in the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them. At others, especially large universities, they’re there, but you have to go looking for them.

What these experiences were linked to:

  • An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were more likely to be leaders, to be civically involved, to keep learning, to be involved in the arts and culture, to feel fulfilled and, over a long career, to be successful.
  • A mentor whose influence lasted: more likely to lead, to volunteer, to keep learning, to take part in culture and to feel fulfilled.
  • Room to explore: more likely to earn more and, over a long career, to be successful. Major didn’t predict long-term success.
  • Classes that made them think: far more likely to feel their lives were fulfilling, and more likely to keep learning.
  • People unlike themselves: more likely to lead and to keep learning.

Want the specific percentages?

The numbers behind this
  • An authentic learning community (professors who knew them, a mentor, being part of campus life): graduates were 25% more likely to be leaders, 42% more likely to volunteer and 56% more likely to give a larger share of their income, 31% more likely to keep learning, 40% more likely to be involved in the arts and culture, and 46% more likely to say their lives felt fulfilling. Finally, over a long career, they were also 32% more likely to say they were successful.
  • A mentor whose influence lasted: 26% more likely to be leaders, 28% more likely to volunteer, 23% more likely to keep learning, 31% more likely to be involved in culture and 34% more likely to feel fulfilled.
  • Room to explore (more than half of classes outside the major): 24% more likely to earn more as adults, and 53% more likely to say they were successful and 72% more likely to earn more over a long career. Major didn’t predict long-term success.
  • Classes that made them think: graduates whose professors made them question their own arguments were 149% more likely (about two and a half times as likely) to say their lives felt highly fulfilling and 37% more likely to keep learning.
  • People unlike themselves: frequent conversations with people of different beliefs and values: 26% more likely to be leaders and 38% more likely to keep learning.

“26% more likely” means leadership was 26% more common among graduates who had more of that experience than among those who had less of it. See all the findings: What Matters in College · Read how the study was done

These college experiences made an even bigger difference for students from families with the least money.

If money is tight, this matters even more

Among graduates well into their careers who grew up in lower-income families, those who took more than half their classes outside their major were 72% more likely to say they were successful and 75% more likely to report a higher income. Those who had been part of an authentic learning community (close relationships with professors outside class, and a mentor) were 64% more likely to say they were successful.5 So when you compare colleges, don’t just ask what it costs. Ask whether you’ll get these experiences there.

Your college timeline: what to do, and when

A printable version is in the parent checklist.

  1. Junior year: find your helpers. Talk with your school counselor, or if you can’t get time with one, a teacher, coach or one of the free programs in the “First in your family” box. Take this site’s quiz, and try three net price calculators, including one at a private college you assumed was too expensive.
  2. Junior and senior years: visit. Visit several colleges, in person if possible and online if not, to get a feel for the kind of college that fits you. Bring the questions from your quiz result, and ask students when a professor last talked with them outside class.
  3. Summer before senior year: make your list. Build a balanced list: colleges you’re likely to get into and can afford, some good matches, and a few reaches. Ask about fee waivers if money is tight.
  4. Fall of senior year: apply and file the FAFSA. Send applications and file the FAFSA at studentaid.gov as soon as it opens; some aid runs out. Check whether any college also asks for the CSS Profile.
  5. Spring of senior year: compare offers. Compare financial aid offers side by side on net price (cost minus grants and scholarships), not loans. If an offer falls short, ask the aid office to review it.
  6. The final choice. Don’t decide on cost, reputation or job preparation alone. Go back to the life you want, and ask which college is most likely to give you the experiences that make a lifelong difference.

Can’t visit? You can still find out

Most of what you need to know, you can find from home.

  • Virtual tours and online info sessions: most colleges have both. Ask your quiz questions in the chat.
  • Talk to a real student: many admissions offices will connect you with one by email or video. Ask the student, not the brochure.
  • Sit in online: some colleges let you watch a class. Ask.
  • Read what students write: the student newspaper, club pages and student government notes show what students actually do.
  • Fly-in programs: some colleges pay travel costs for lower-income students to visit. Search for the college’s name and “fly-in program,” or ask your counselor.

Before you fall for a campus

Dealbreakers first:

  • Is this a place that will help me become who I want to be, not just get a degree?
  • Can my family and I afford it all the way to graduation, after grants and scholarships?
  • Does it have what I might study, with room to change my mind?
  • Do students like me finish here?
    graduation rateis the share of students who finish in four years, and within six years. Ask for the rates for students like you.
  • Will I feel like I belong, safe and supported?
  • Are housing, health, disability and advising services good enough for what I need?

Why belonging is a dealbreaker: feeling that you belong isn’t a luxury; it’s essential. When you visit, in person or online, ask students: “When did you first feel at home here, and what helped?” Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen found that a one-hour exercise, in which first-year students read how older students’ worries about belonging had faded, reduced the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation. A 2023 study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 colleges and universities found a similar exercise raised the share who completed their first year full-time, especially for students from groups that had historically progressed at lower rates, and most at colleges that gave students real opportunities to belong.67

Good signs at any college:

  • Professors know students and talk with them outside class.
  • In class, students talk, write, argue and solve problems.
  • You have room to take classes outside your major.
  • Students spend real time with people who think and live differently.
  • You get to do real things: research, projects, internships, performances, service.
Words colleges use (plain translations)
  • Major: The subject you study most.
  • Elective: A class you choose to take, not one your major requires.
  • General education: Classes every student takes in different subjects, whatever the major.
  • Seminar: A small class built on discussion.
  • Humanities: Literature, history, philosophy, languages and the arts.
  • Liberal arts: Not a list of majors. A way of learning: lots of subjects, small discussions, writing, and getting to know your professors.
  • Adviser: The person who helps you choose classes and plan.
  • Mentor: An adult who takes a lasting interest in you and your goals.
  • Office hours: Set times when a professor is free to talk with any student, no appointment needed.
  • Internship: A short job in a field you’re curious about.
  • Common Data Set: A standard report most colleges post online, with class sizes, costs and graduation rates.
