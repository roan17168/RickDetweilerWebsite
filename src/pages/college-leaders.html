For college leaders

Your mission statement makes a promise. Does your budget keep it?

An analysis of the mission statements of 241 colleges found them promising much the same things: leaders, engaged citizens, lifelong learners, fulfilled and successful people. Interviews with 1,000 graduates show which experiences are linked to those outcomes. Many of them cost little. All of them depend on what the institution rewards. And they matter most for the students who arrive with the least: in the study, several of the links were strongest for graduates from lower-income families.

The retention case

The experiences linked to graduates’ lives are also linked to whether students stay. Vincent Tinto found that students are more likely to persist when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of the institution.2 George Kuh found that first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research, service learning and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year,3 with the largest benefits for underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students. Every student who stays is a student who can graduate, and tuition the institution does not have to replace.

Other research

Alexander Astin studied about 25,000 students at 309 institutions. He found the peer group to be the single most powerful influence on students’ development, with interaction with faculty next, and found that a heavy institutional emphasis on research can be associated with lower student satisfaction and weaker development.5

Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini, reviewing decades of research, concluded that differences between colleges explain less than what students do once enrolled. Engagement is the lever.6

Gallup-Purdue found that only 3% of graduates strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.7

It has been done

It has been done, at every kind of institution

George Kuh and colleagues studied 20 colleges and universities whose graduation rates and student engagement were higher than predicted for the students they enrolled. They included research universities, regional universities, liberal arts colleges and historically Black universities.8 Three examples from their study:

A liberal arts college: Ursinus College. Every first-year student took a two-semester Common Intellectual Experience course. Students lived near classmates from their section, some classes met in the residence halls, and advising was shared by teams of faculty and staff.

A research university: the University of Kansas. The university made undergraduate teaching a visible priority, with awards and rewards for good teaching, faculty “ambassadors” linked to its teaching center, and most undergraduate courses were kept at 30 students or fewer.

A small college: Alverno College. Student affairs staff worked as partners in learning, with outcomes for life outside class defined to match the abilities the college expects every graduate to develop.

Other institutions in the study included the University of Michigan, George Mason University, the University of Texas at El Paso, Fayetteville State University and Winston-Salem State University. What the twenty shared was not wealth or selectivity but six conditions: a mission that is lived, not only stated; an unshakeable focus on student learning; clearly marked pathways to success; a habit of continuous improvement; campus settings adapted for learning; and shared responsibility for educational quality.

These examples describe the institutions in the early 2000s, when those studies were done.

For enrollment and admissions leaders

Families will ask harder questions. The student and parent pages of this site suggest questions for visits and calls: who does the talking in class, what a first-year student can be in charge of, how many professors will know a student by name, and the graduation rate for students who receive Pell Grants. Prepare tour guides and counselors with specific, recent stories. On the student scorecard, a website claim alone counts as “Don’t know yet.”

Families will expect plain talk about price. The site tells families that published prices are rarely what they pay, and that they may ask for an aid offer to be reviewed. Be ready to explain your aid clearly.

The evidence

The numbers behind the argument

A graduate’s first-year earnings tell you about a first job, not a working life. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.9 The experiences studied here (professors who know students, a mentor, broad study, and teaching that makes students think) are linked to how graduates’ lives turn out, not only to their first salary.

The accounting behind those lives is well documented. Gallup and Strada found that 45% of graduates of colleges with fewer than 5,000 students said their professors cared about them as a person, against 24% at larger institutions.10 The study behind this site, reported in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, found the same experiences linked to leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural involvement, fulfillment and success.1

Belonging is part of retention, not a separate program. Gregory Walton and colleagues found that helping new students see that doubts about belonging are normal and fade raised the share who completed their first year full-time, most of all at colleges that gave students real opportunities to belong.4

Institutional checklist

Eight practices, many of which cost little in new money

What it’s for. The checklist estimates how many of your students actually get the experiences the research links to graduates’ lives, names who owns each one, and sets one next step for each. Most of these practices depend more on how time and requirements are arranged than on new money.

Who should complete it. A small group works best: the chief academic officer, the chief student affairs officer, the head of institutional research and someone from finance. Allow one meeting to gather data and one to complete the form.

Open the eight-practice checklist Download the dean’s briefing (PDF)

How to use it

  1. Gather the data first. Possible sources for each row are listed below. Where you have no data, say so: “unknown” is a finding. For the row on course practices, sample rather than review every course: for example, one section from each department, or NSSE items alone.
  2. Estimate the share of students who get each experience, as a percentage. If you can, break it out for commuters, transfers, part-time and first-generation students, who are often least likely to get them.
  3. Rate each practice on this scale:
    • Widespread: most students get it, roughly three-quarters or more.
    • Uneven: some do, often depending on major or on a few people.
    • Rare: few students get it, roughly a quarter or fewer.
    • Unknown: no reliable data.
  4. Name an owner. One office or person responsible for each practice, not a committee.
  5. Mark the cost. Is the next step budget neutral, a reallocation of time or money, a policy change, or a new investment? The table below gives the usual answer for each practice.
  6. Choose one next step and a date for each practice.
  7. Report the results to your board once a year, alongside enrollment and placement figures, and repeat the checklist annually. Share them publicly once you can show where you stand and what you are doing about it.

Using it for accreditation. Accreditors ask institutions to show that students achieve the outcomes the institution promises. The checklist, the three senior-survey questions in the Mission-first result and the alumni survey below can all serve as evidence in a self-study or assessment plan.

Note: The rating thresholds above are a suggested starting point, not standards from the study. Adjust them as you prefer.

Board summary

For your board: one page

What the research shows

  • Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults.
  • Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families.
  • The same experiences are linked to students staying and graduating, and they matter most for students who arrive with the least.

Five indicators we will track

  1. Share of seniors who can name a faculty or staff mentor.
  2. Share of students who frequently talk with faculty outside class.
  3. Share of each degree that is courses outside the major, by major.
  4. Share of students who complete an internship, research project or other substantial experience.
  5. First-to-second-year retention, by student group.

Alumni outcomes at ten and twenty years are added as the alumni survey is run.

Alumni survey

Alumni survey (template)

What it’s for. A short survey of alumni ten and twenty or more years after graduation, covering the six adult outcomes in the study and the college experiences linked to them.

Note: These questions are modeled on the kinds of questions the study asked, not its exact wording. Adapt them to your institution and test them before use. Commercial alumni surveys also exist.

Adult life

  • Leadership. In the past five years, have you led a team, organization, project or group at work or in your community?
  • Civic involvement. Did you vote in the most recent national election? In the past year, about how many hours did you volunteer? About what share of your income did you give to charities or causes?
  • Continued learning. In the past year, have you taken a course, earned a credential, or read or studied regularly to learn something new?
  • Cultural involvement. In the past year, how often did you attend concerts, theater, museums or talks, or read about or discuss the arts and ideas?
  • Fulfillment. How meaningful and satisfying is your life today?
  • Personal success. What is your current position level, from entry level to owner or chief executive? What is your household income, in broad bands?

College experiences

  • Did you have a college mentor whose influence has lasted?
  • Did most of your professors know you by name?
  • How often did you talk with professors outside class, about coursework and about other things?
  • About what share of your courses were outside your major?
  • How often did your classes involve discussion, writing, and questions with no single right answer?
Promotion and tenure

Sample promotion and tenure language

Use or adapt in faculty review criteria:

“Teaching includes a faculty member’s work with students outside class: advising and mentoring, supervising research and creative projects, and supporting students’ learning beyond the course. Candidates should describe this work and its results, for example the number of students mentored, projects supervised and what students went on to do, and reviewers should weigh it as part of teaching, not as service.”
