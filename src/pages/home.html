What College Is Really For

What kind of college education lasts a lifetime?

It’s not the major. It’s not the ranking. It’s what happens there.

We asked 1,000 people, ten, twenty and forty years after college, about their college years and about their lives now. The ones who were leading, giving back, still curious, doing well and finding their lives worthwhile had something in common. It had less to do with where they went or what they majored in, and more to do with what happened to them there.

Start at the end

Most people choose a college by starting with what is easiest to see: the name, the rank, the price, the first salary. Start at the other end.

First, picture the life you want: is it work you enjoy, people who count on you, a mind that stays curious, enough to live well, or a sense that it all means something?

Second, ask what kind of college experience leads there.

Third, look for the colleges where that happens, and only then choose.

The Study

One thousand graduates, looking back

  • 1,000 graduates, each interviewed one on one.1
  • Ages 25 to 65: about ten, twenty and forty years after college.
  • Every kind of college: public and private, large and small, selective and not.
  • Published by MIT Press in 2021, and named by Forbes one of the best higher education books of that year.

What we know about what matters in college: all the findings · How the study was done

What a good life after college looked like

The study looked at six parts of graduates’ adult lives:

  • Leading: leading groups, teams and organizations
  • Giving back: volunteering, giving, voting
  • Still learning: reading, studying and learning for the rest of their lives
  • A rich life: arts, ideas and culture
  • Fulfillment: a life that feels meaningful and satisfying
  • Doing well: a good position and income

The book

Richard A. Detweiler, The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs: Lives of Consequence, Inquiry, and Accomplishment (MIT Press, 2021).
