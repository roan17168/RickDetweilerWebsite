The five practices

The five cards

These links held for every kind of major, engineering and other professional majors included, when students had the experiences. And several were strongest for graduates who grew up with less money.

Lines marked “Why it helps now” are practical advice, not findings from the study.

1. Time with a professor outside class

What the research found. Graduates who had a college mentor with a lasting impact were 26% more likely to be leaders as adults. Among graduates late in their careers, the link between these relationships and success was strongest for those who grew up with less money.

Why it helps now. Professors and teaching assistants are how students get help in time to pass a hard course. A professor who knows your work can also write a real recommendation and point you to research, jobs and people.

A graduate says

“…the professors were accessible and could share valuable insights. They also recognized my passion for medicine, so they directed me toward appropriate research opportunities and work experiences.”

Angela, trauma surgeon and director of a surgical residency

Start small. Office hours are set times when a professor is in their office for any student. No appointment needed, and no problem needed either. Go once with a real question: “Can you help me understand one thing from Tuesday’s class?” Next time, try: “I liked last week’s topic. What should I read next?”

At a big university. Start with your teaching assistant; TAs are often the way to the professor. Go to office hours in the first weeks of the term, when there is no line. Look for the small classes, such as first-year seminars, honors sections and writing courses, where professors learn names.

Short on time? Email the same question, or stay two minutes after class. Many professors also hold office hours by video.

Already doing this? Ask one professor to be involved with something that interests you: a project, a research question or an independent study.

Other research agrees. Richard Light, after years of interviewing Harvard undergraduates, gave students one piece of advice above others: get to know one faculty member reasonably well each semester.

2. Courses outside your major

What the research found. Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report a higher income as adults, and 33% more likely if they came from lower-income families. Major had nothing to do with long-term success.

Why it helps now. It’s the cheapest way to find out what you’re good at before you commit to a path.

A graduate says

“…a well-rounded liberal arts education prepared and enabled me to quickly adapt and transition to other career options.”

Theressa, physician

Start small. At your next registration, choose one course only because it sounds interesting.

Engineering, nursing or another program with set requirements? Use whatever electives you have on something far from your field, or sit in on one lecture. The links in the study held for engineering students too, when they had these experiences.

Already doing this? Look for a course that connects two of your interests, or a minor far from your major.

3. Classes that make you think

What the research found. Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled: about two and a half times as likely.

Why it helps now. These are the classes that give you something to say in an interview.

A graduate says

“…taught me three critical things that have made all the difference: How to Learn, How to Think, and How to Communicate.”

Deborah, senior officer of a major corporation, on her professors

Start small. In your next paper, add one paragraph that begins: “The strongest argument against my view is…”

In science, math or engineering. On your next problem set or design, add one line: “Where could this go wrong?” Or explain why your approach is better than another one that also works.

At a big university. When a course offers a choice of sections, discussion groups or labs, take the smaller one, and speak at least once each week.

4. Being part of something on campus

What the research found. Graduates who took an active part in campus organizations were 35% more likely to volunteer more as adults. Leading or joining campus groups was also linked to leadership and fulfillment later in life.

Why it helps now. It’s where most students find their people.

A graduate says

“In retrospect, I probably developed most of my leadership and crisis management skills as a dorm resident assistant.”

Angela, trauma surgeon

Start small. Go to one meeting of one group and ask: “What do you need help with?”

At a big university. Pick two: one group connected to your field, such as a design team or a professional society, and one that has nothing to do with it.

If you work or commute. An on-campus job puts you beside the same people every week. Ask the student employment office, and ask the financial aid office whether you have work-study, a federal program that pays for campus jobs. A regular study group works too. One hour in the same place beats five separate events.

Already doing a lot? Do fewer things more deeply. Lead one group rather than attending four.

Other research agrees. Alexander Astin’s study of about 25,000 students found that the more students put into college, in and out of class, the more they got from it.

5. Real conversations with people who see things differently

What the research found. Graduates who often talked with people whose values and backgrounds differed from their own were 38% more likely to keep learning actively as adults, and 26% more likely to be leaders.

Why it helps now. Every job involves working with people who see things differently.

A graduate says

“[I] learned to interact with students of all socioeconomic, racial, and ethnic backgrounds.”

A graduate interviewed for the study

Start small. Ask a classmate: “How did you come to see it that way?” Then listen for five minutes before you answer.

When disagreement feels risky. Choose a setting where it is expected: a class discussion, a debate or dialogue group, or a project team that agrees on ground rules first.

Short on time? Do it inside a class discussion or group project you already have.

If you need help When college gets hard Open this section

The cards above are about getting more out of college. Sometimes the job is getting through it. The same people, professors, advisers and staff, are how most students do.

Struggling in a class.

Go to the professor or teaching assistant the first week you feel lost, not the week before the exam. Find the tutoring or learning center. It is usually free, and most students who use it are not failing.

Keeping your financial aid.

Federal aid requires “satisfactory academic progress”: usually a minimum grade point average and finishing enough of the credits you attempt. Renew the FAFSA every year. If your grades or your family’s situation change, talk with the financial aid office early.

Money or family emergencies.

Many colleges have emergency grants, food pantries or short-term loans. Ask the dean of students office.

Feeling like you don’t belong.

Many students feel this, especially in the first year. Look for a first-generation student group or a mentoring program; they exist to help with exactly this.

Stress, anxiety or feeling low.

The counseling center is usually free for students. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Thinking of leaving.

Talk with an adviser or a dean first. A lighter course load or a leave of absence can keep your options open, while leaving without a plan can mean losing aid and still owing loans.

Real questions

Questions students ask

“What are you going to do with that?”

If you are studying history, philosophy or another field without one obvious job, here is the answer. Employers say the skills they weigh most in hiring are communication (64%), problem-solving (62%) and adaptability (60%). A major prepares you for a field. These experiences prepare you for the world of change.

“Why not let AI write it?”

Software can write a paragraph arguing against your view in seconds. The research is about what happens when you do it yourself: graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. Use AI the way your professor allows, and do the thinking yourself.

“Can I afford an internship?”

Ask the career office whether the college funds unpaid or low-paid internships and research. Many do. Paid research positions and campus jobs in your field count too.

The plan

My three moves this semester

What it’s for. The plan turns your result into three small, specific actions for this semester, and gives you two moments to check how they went.

Starting mid-semester? That’s fine. Do one move now and plan the other two for next term.

When to use it. Right after you take the check, ideally in the first month of a semester. It takes about ten minutes.

How to use it

  1. Look at your result. Find your two lowest cards. Your moves should come mostly from those.
  2. Choose any three moves from the five cards, or write your own. Be specific. Not “talk to a professor” but “Dr. Rivera, Biology 110.” Not “join something” but “the Tuesday tutoring group.” A teaching assistant, adviser or staff member who knows your work counts as the person you talk with; at a big university, a TA is often the way to a professor.
  3. Write your first step. The very first thing you’ll do, and the words you’ll use. Each card’s “Start small” line gives you a script.
  4. Set a date. When the first step will happen. Earlier is better: most of these are easier in the first weeks of a term.
  5. Write the small version. If work, a commute or a hard week gets in the way, what’s the smallest version you can still do? Each card’s “Short on time?” line helps.
  6. Check in at midterm. Did it happen? What got in the way? Change the move if you need to. A smaller move you do beats a bigger one you don’t.
  7. Look back at the end of the semester. What did you get from each move? What would you do next? Then retake the check before next semester and choose your next three.
