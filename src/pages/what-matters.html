The evidence

What matters in college

One chart, six long-term outcomes, and the college experiences that predicted them. The pattern is more practical — and more hopeful — than the usual rankings debate.

How to read the chart

Each experience is something students can actually have in college. Each outcome is grouped under the three kinds of life the research describes: a life of consequence, a life of inquiry and a life of accomplishment.

  • Bigger circle, stronger link. The percentage inside is how much more likely graduates with more of that experience were to report the outcome. The circle's area is proportional to the number, so the visual difference is honest.
  • A small grey dot means no substantial link. It does not mean the experience is unimportant. It means this study did not find a meaningful relationship with that particular outcome.
  • Read down the columns, then across the rows. The columns show what predicts each outcome. The rows show how far one experience reaches.

What the chart says

The first thing to notice is that no single experience predicts everything. A major not tied to one occupation is linked to continued learning, cultural involvement and fulfillment, but not to leadership, civic involvement or long-term personal success. Intellectual skills reach three outcomes; an authentic learning community reaches all six.

The second is that the content of a college education matters. Breadth of study and intellectual skills are not ornaments around a major; they are among the strongest predictors in the study. Span of study is linked to a 59% higher likelihood of fulfillment and a 53% higher likelihood of long-term personal success.

The third is that context matters just as much. Engaging teaching, larger perspectives and an authentic learning community are not soft extras. An authentic learning community is the only experience in the chart linked to all six outcomes, including a 46% higher likelihood of fulfillment.1

What stands out

Three findings are worth carrying into any conversation about college. The first is that breadth keeps paying. Taking more than half of one’s courses outside the major was linked to a 53% higher likelihood of long-term success and a 59% higher likelihood of fulfillment, and it was strongest for graduates who grew up with less money. The second is that teaching matters more than the subject taught. Graduates whose professors made them examine the strengths and weaknesses of their own arguments were 149% more likely, about two and a half times as likely, to describe their lives as highly fulfilled. The third is that belonging is structural, not decorative: an authentic learning community was the only experience linked to every one of the six outcomes.234

What is an authentic learning community?

The phrase appears throughout this site, so it is worth being concrete. In the study, graduates had an authentic learning community when most of these were true:

  • talking often with professors outside class, about coursework and about other things;
  • having a college mentor whose influence lasted;
  • most professors knowing the student’s first name;
  • taking an active part in, and leading, campus organizations;
  • living in campus housing for three or more years;
  • taking part in campus activism or causes.

That is a description of a place, not a program. It is the reason a small college can provide it almost by default, and the reason a large university can still provide it to a student who seeks it out.

The detail, outcome by outcome

The chart gives the composite figure for each kind of experience. These are the specific experiences inside each composite, for each outcome.

Leadership

Leading groups, teams and organizations at work and in the community.

  • An authentic learning community: 25%.
  • Developing larger perspectives: 28%.
  • Major, breadth, intellectual skills and engaging teaching were not substantially linked to leadership.

Civic involvement

Giving, volunteering and voting. Voting was not linked to any college experience; giving and volunteering were.

  • An authentic learning community: 26% overall; 56% more likely to donate a larger share of income; 42% more likely to spend more time volunteering.
  • Developing larger perspectives: 22%.

Continued learning

Reading, studying, taking courses and keeping up with the world throughout life.

  • A major not tied to one occupation: 29% (36% for majors other than business or accounting).
  • Span of study: 29%. Discussing philosophical or ethical perspectives in most classes: 29%; more than half of courses outside the major: 20%.
  • Developing intellectual skills: 37%. Writing papers in most classes: 49%; open-ended coursework: 37%.
  • Engaging teaching: 29%. Discussion-based seminars in the first two years: 25%; first-year classes of twenty to thirty students or fewer: 23%.
  • Developing larger perspectives: 42%.
  • An authentic learning community: 31%.

Cultural involvement

Taking part in the arts, ideas and other cultures.

  • A major not tied to one occupation: 36%. Humanities and arts majors were 45% more likely to be culturally involved.
  • Span of study: 42%. Many courses in the humanities: 47%; discussing philosophical or ethical perspectives in most classes: 30%.
  • Developing intellectual skills: 40%. Writing papers in most classes: 49%; open-ended coursework: 37%.
  • Engaging teaching: 25%. A large student role in discussion in most classes: 47%; discussion seminars in the first two years: 37%.
  • Developing larger perspectives: 22%.
  • An authentic learning community: 40%.

Fulfillment

Describing one’s life as meaningful and satisfying.

  • A major not tied to one occupation: 44%.
  • Span of study: 59%. Discussing philosophical or ethical perspectives in most classes: 59%; many courses in the humanities: 41%.
  • Developing intellectual skills: 66%. Writing papers in most classes: 57%; open-ended coursework: 55%.
  • Engaging teaching: 33%. A large student role in discussion in most classes: 45%.
  • An authentic learning community: 46%. A mentor whose influence lasted: 51%; most professors knowing the student’s first name: 41%.

Personal success

Professional position level and household income, measured over a working life rather than a first job.

  • Span of study: 53%. More than half of courses outside the major: 24% (72% for income among graduates from lower-income families).
  • An authentic learning community: 32%.
