The evidence

What matters in college

One chart, six long-term outcomes, and the college experiences that predicted them. The pattern is more practical — and more hopeful — than the usual rankings debate.

How to read the chart

Each experience is something students can actually have in college. Each outcome is grouped under the three kinds of life the research describes: a life of consequence, a life of inquiry and a life of accomplishment.

  • Bigger circle, stronger link. The percentage inside is how much more likely graduates with more of that experience were to report the outcome. The circle's area is proportional to the number, so the visual difference is honest.
  • A small grey dot means no substantial link. It does not mean the experience is unimportant. It means this study did not find a meaningful relationship with that particular outcome.
  • Read down the columns, then across the rows. The columns show what predicts each outcome. The rows show how far one experience reaches.

What the chart says

The first thing to notice is that no single experience predicts everything. A major not tied to one occupation is linked to continued learning, cultural involvement and fulfillment, but not to leadership, civic involvement or long-term personal success. Intellectual skills reach three outcomes; an authentic learning community reaches all six.

The second is that the content of a college education matters. Breadth of study and intellectual skills are not ornaments around a major; they are among the strongest predictors in the study. Span of study is linked to a 59% higher likelihood of fulfillment and a 53% higher likelihood of long-term personal success.

The third is that context matters just as much. Engaging teaching, larger perspectives and an authentic learning community are not soft extras. An authentic learning community is the only experience in the chart linked to all six outcomes, including a 46% higher likelihood of fulfillment.1

What this does not say. It does not say that any one experience causes any one outcome, or that a particular college guarantees either. It says that these experiences are consistently associated with lives graduates describe as good — across ages, institutions and majors.
