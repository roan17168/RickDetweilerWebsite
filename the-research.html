Methodology & Evidence

Where this comes from

Everything on this site starts from one study, reported in Richard A. Detweiler's The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs: Lives of Consequence, Inquiry, and Accomplishment (MIT Press, 2021). Here is how it was done and what it can and cannot tell you.

How the study was done

  • Who: Interviews with 1,000 college graduates from every kind of American college and university.
  • Ages: Three groups, about ten, twenty and forty years after graduation: 25 to 35, 36 to 45, and 55 to 65.
  • How: Individual telephone interviews by trained interviewers, using about 100 questions.
  • What was asked: What people did and experienced in college, and how they live now. The questions were about behavior, not opinions about whether college helped.

Study details:

The research was carried out by Art & Science Group, an education research and consulting firm in Ellicott City, Maryland, which developed and pilot-tested the interview questions, drew the sample, and managed the interviews and the coding of answers.

It was funded by the Teagle Foundation (New York) and the Spencer Foundation (Chicago), with additional support from the Great Lakes Colleges Association (Ann Arbor, Michigan).

Sample composition & demographics breakdown

Where the graduates went to college: 46% graduated from bachelor's colleges, 16% from master's universities and 33% from doctoral universities. 35% attended public institutions and 65% private ones. Enrollment ranged from under 1,500 students (13%) to over 20,000 (14%). 27% attended the most selective institutions, 58% moderately selective ones and 15% non-selective ones. Entrance test scores ranged from under 1100 on the SAT (27%) to over 1510 (7.5%).

Who they were: 58% women and 42% men; 89% white and 11% people of color. Full details are in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, chapter 5 and appendixes 2, 3 and 4.[106]

What was measured

Six long-term life outcomes:

Category Outcome Examples of what was asked
Life of Consequence Leadership Leading organizations, teams, and community groups.
Civic Involvement Giving, volunteering, voting.
Life of Inquiry Continued Learning Continuing to read, take courses, and study regularly.
Cultural Involvement Attending arts, concerts, museums; discussing ideas.
Life of Accomplishment Fulfillment Describing life as meaningful and satisfying.
Personal Success Professional position level and household income.

What it found

How to read these numbers: A figure such as "24% more likely" compares two groups. For example, if 57% of one group reports a higher income compared with 46% of another, the first group's rate is 124% of the second group's (57 divided by 46). Statisticians call this the incident risk ratio, and the increase expressed as a percentage (24%) is called the percent relative effect. All reported effects are statistically significant at the $p < .05$ level or better; most are significant at $p < .001$.

1. Setting drove leadership and civic involvement: Graduates who had a mentor or talked often with faculty outside class were 21% to 26% more likely to be leaders. Those closely involved outside class were 56% more likely to give a larger share of income and 42% more likely to volunteer. Voting was not linked; giving and volunteering were.[107]
2. Setting and content drove continued learning and cultural life: Broad study, writing, open-ended questions, and contact with different people were linked to these outcomes by 20% to 56%.
3. Content drove fulfillment: Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes: +59%. Being made to examine the strengths and weaknesses of one's own arguments: +149%.
4. Breadth drove success, while major did not: Taking more than half of courses outside the major: 24% more likely to report a higher income (and 33% to 34% for graduates from lower-income families or lower test scores).

What it cannot show

The study interviewed people once and asked them to look back. It could not do a true experiment by assigning people to colleges at random. So it shows that these experiences and these outcomes go together. It cannot prove that one causes the other. The book takes up this question of causality directly and compares the findings with other widely cited and respected research.

Other research points the same way

Researcher Study What it adds
Alexander Astin What Matters in College? (1993). ~25,000 students at 309 institutions. The peer group is the single most powerful influence on development; faculty interaction is next.
Ernest Pascarella & Patrick Terenzini How College Affects Students (1991, 2005). Decades of review. What students do in college matters far more than which college they attend.
Richard Light Making the Most of College (2001). Harvard interviews over years. Students who do best get to know faculty, study in groups, write, and learn from unlike peers outside class.
Vincent Tinto Leaving College (1987, 1993). Students stay and persist when connected to both the academic and social life of a college.
Gallup & Purdue University Great Jobs, Great Lives (2014). 30,000 graduates. Caring professors, mentors, long projects, and deep involvement double later well-being and engagement.
Scott Freeman et al. Active Learning in STEM, PNAS (2014). 225 studies. Students in traditional lectures were 1.5× more likely to fail than those in active learning classes.
George Kuh High-Impact Educational Practices (AAC&U, 2008). Seminars, research, and writing benefited low-income and underrepresented students the most.

Why this matters now

Education in the liberal arts tradition has always aimed at the common good: lives that are good for the people living them and good for the society around them. This study shows what that looks like in practice. The experiences it examined were linked not only to graduates' income and careers but to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural lives and fulfillment.

Those are the capacities people need in a world that keeps changing. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030.
