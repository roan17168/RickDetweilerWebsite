Methodology & evidence

Where this comes from

Everything on this site starts from one study, reported in Richard A. Detweiler’s The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs (MIT Press, 2021). Here is how it was done and what it can and cannot tell you.

How the study was done

Who:

1,000 college graduates from every kind of American college and university.1

Ages:

Three groups, about ten, twenty and forty years after graduation: 25 to 35, 36 to 45, and 55 to 65.

How:

Individual telephone interviews by trained interviewers, using about 100 questions.

What was asked:

What people did and experienced in college, and how they live now. The questions were about behavior, not opinions about whether college helped.

Study details. The research was carried out by Art & Science Group, an education research and consulting firm in Ellicott City, Maryland, which developed and pilot-tested the interview questions, drew the sample, and managed the interviews and the coding of answers. It was funded by the Teagle Foundation (New York) and the Spencer Foundation (Chicago), with additional support from the Great Lakes Colleges Association (Ann Arbor, Michigan).

How graduates were chosen. Six hundred graduates were randomly selected from a nationally representative panel2 of college graduates in the right age groups. The panel was used so that graduates of every type of institution would be included: public and private, large and small, liberal arts, comprehensive and research, selective and non-selective. Because only a small share of graduates nationally have had many of the experiences the study examined, 400 more were randomly selected from the 84,000 living alumni of twelve liberal arts colleges. This widened the range of experiences studied. It was not done to compare types of colleges, and the findings held when the two groups were analyzed separately.

Who interviewed them. Trained professional interviewers, by telephone, using a structured interview that had been pilot-tested and refined.

How the results were analyzed. The principal analyses did not compare types of institutions. They compared graduates by their educational experiences, wherever they went to college.

Where the graduates went to college. 46% graduated from bachelor’s colleges, 16% from master’s universities and 33% from doctoral universities. 35% attended public institutions and 65% private ones. Enrollment ranged from under 1,500 students (13%) to over 20,000 (14%). 27% attended the most selective institutions, 58% moderately selective ones and 15% non-selective ones. Average entrance test scores ranged from under 1100 on the SAT, or the ACT equivalent (27%), to over 1510 (7.5%).

Who they were. 58% women and 42% men; 89% white and 11% people of color.

Full details3 are in The Evidence Liberal Arts Needs, chapter 5 (research design and analyses) and appendixes 2, 3 and 4.

About the author. The author is a graduate of a liberal arts college and of a major research university. He led liberal arts organizations for many years. The study included graduates of every kind of college—big and small, private and public, selective and non-selective, research and liberal arts and comprehensive, regional and national—and its findings held across all of them.

How the analysis worked

How the analysis worked. For each of the six kinds of college experience, the study combined graduates’ answers to a set of related questions into one indicator, and divided graduates into those with lower and higher levels of that experience. Adult outcomes were divided the same way. The study then statistically compared how often each group reported each outcome. Where the overall difference for a kind of experience was statistically significant, the study went on to examine the individual questions within it, such as having a mentor or talking with professors outside class. The analyses were repeated within groups of graduates defined by gender, race and ethnicity, entrance test scores and family background, including whether they were the first in their family to attend college, to check whether the links held for different kinds of students. A link is reported as substantial only when it was statistically significant and the difference between the groups was at least 20%.

What it found

How to read these numbers

A figure such as “24% more likely” compares two groups. For example, if 57% of one group reports a higher income, compared with 46% of the other group, the first group’s rate is 124% of the second group’s (57 divided by 46). Statisticians call this the incident risk ratio, and the increase expressed as a percentage (24%) is called the percent relative effect. All reported effects are statistically significant at the p<.05 level or better; most are significant at the p<.001 level.

1

For leadership and civic involvement, the educational setting mattered most. Graduates who had a mentor or talked often with faculty outside class were 21 to 26% more likely to be leaders. Those closely involved with faculty and other students outside class were 56% more likely to give a larger share of income and 42% more likely to volunteer more. What they studied made no substantial difference. Voting was not linked to these experiences; giving and volunteering were.

2

For continued learning and cultural involvement, both the educational setting and the content of study mattered. Broad study, writing, open-ended questions, discussion-based classes and contact with different people were each linked to these outcomes, by 20 to 56%.

3

For fulfillment, the strongest links were to intellectual content. Discussing philosophical and ethical questions in most classes: 59%. Being made to examine the strengths and weaknesses of one’s own arguments: up to 149%.

4

For success, breadth mattered and major did not. More than half of courses outside the major: 24% more likely to report a higher income, and 33 to 34% for those with lower family income or lower entrance test scores.

5

The findings were not limited to one kind of college. They held across the types of institutions in the study wherever these experiences were strongly present.

What it cannot show

The study interviewed people once and asked them to look back. It could not do a true experiment by assigning people to colleges at random. So it shows that these experiences and these outcomes go together. It cannot prove that one causes the other. The book takes up this question of causality directly and compares the findings with other widely cited and respected research.

Other research points the same way

Research whose findings point in the same direction as this study.
Researcher Study What it adds
Alexander Astin4 What Matters in College? (1993). About 25,000 students at 309 institutions. The peer group is the single most powerful influence on students’ development, and interaction with faculty is next. Involvement drives results.
Ernest Pascarella and Patrick Terenzini5 How College Affects Students (1991, 2005). Reviews of decades of studies. What students do in college matters more than which college they attend.
Richard Light6 Making the Most of College (2001). Interviews with Harvard undergraduates over many years. Students who do best get to know faculty, study in groups, write a great deal, and learn from classmates unlike themselves, mostly outside class.
Vincent Tinto7 Leaving College (1987, 1993). Students stay when they are connected to both the academic and the social life of a college.
Gallup and Purdue University8 Great Jobs, Great Lives (2014). 30,000 graduates. Caring professors, a mentor, a long project, an applied internship and deep involvement are tied to later engagement at work and well-being.
Scott Freeman and colleagues9 “Active Learning Increases Student Performance in Science, Engineering, and Mathematics,” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (2014). Review of 225 studies. Students in traditional lectures were about one and a half times as likely to fail as students in classes that used active learning, and exam scores rose by about 6%.
George Kuh10 High-Impact Educational Practices (AAC&U, 2008). First-year seminars, writing-intensive courses, undergraduate research, service learning and similar practices were tied to better grades and to students returning for a second year. Underrepresented, low-income and less-prepared students benefited most, and were the least likely to take part.
Gregory Walton and colleagues1112 “A Brief Social-Belonging Intervention” (2011) and “Where and with whom does a brief social-belonging intervention promote progress in college?” (2023). A brief exercise in which first-year students read how older students’ worries about belonging had faded reduced the racial gap in grades by about half through graduation. A later study of nearly 27,000 students at 22 colleges found a similar exercise raised the share who completed their first year full-time, especially for students from groups that had historically progressed at lower rates.
National Survey of Student Engagement High-impact practices Effort, faculty and peer interaction, learning beyond the classroom, and work across difference mark the experiences that matter most.

Why this matters now

Education in the liberal arts tradition has always aimed at the common good: lives that are good for the people living them and good for the society around them. This study shows what that looks like in practice. The experiences it examined were linked not only to graduates’ income and careers but to their leadership, civic involvement, continued learning, cultural lives and fulfillment.

Those are the capacities people need in a world that keeps changing. Americans now in their early forties had already held an average of 9.4 jobs by age 38, about twenty years into their working lives, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.13 The World Economic Forum expects nearly 40% of the skills used at work to change by 2030, and projects that new jobs and lost jobs together will equal 22% of today’s jobs.14

That is the case for these experiences: they prepare people for a meaningful and successful life, and to contribute to the lives of others.
