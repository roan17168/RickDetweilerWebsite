Understanding College

Four thousand colleges. One idea underneath almost all of them.

American colleges look wildly different from one another: a community college down the road, a state university with 40,000 students, a college of 1,500 on a hill. Underneath, most of them were built on the same idea about what an education is for. Knowing that idea, and knowing which colleges still practice it, is the most useful thing a person can learn before choosing one, working at one, or funding one.

The kinds of colleges

In plain terms: Colleges are built for different jobs. The label tells you what the place was designed to do, though of course they do vary. It does not tell you what a student will experience there.

Kind of college What it was built to do What it looks like
Research universities Create new knowledge and train researchers and professionals, alongside teaching undergraduates. Large campuses, thousands of graduate students, faculty evaluated primarily on research and grants.
Regional & comprehensive universities Serve a region with professional and practical degrees, alongside liberal arts programs. Medium to large, often commuter-heavy, with master's degrees in business, education, and health fields.
Liberal arts colleges Focus entirely or mostly on undergraduate education across arts, sciences, and humanities. Small (usually under 3,000), residential, few or no graduate programs; faculty evaluated mostly on teaching and mentoring.
Community colleges Provide accessible two-year degrees, career certificates, and transfer pathways close to home. Non-residential, affordable, serving local communities with open or broad admissions.

Other labels cut across these kinds: public or private, religious or secular, historically Black colleges and universities, tribal colleges, women's colleges, online universities. A historically Black college can be a liberal arts college or a research university.

The point that matters: the experiences linked to good lives after college can be found at every kind of institution. The odds of finding them without having to hunt are not the same everywhere: at liberal arts colleges these experiences are usually built into how the college works, and in the study liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide them.[51] At larger or more complex institutions they are often available too, but students may need to seek them out through honors programs, knocking on professors' doors, and pursuing involvement in cocurricular programs.

One idea, refined over millennia and nearly four centuries old in America

In plain terms: The American college was never designed as job training. It was designed to prepare people to think, to take part in public life and to keep learning as the needs of the individual and of humanity change over time. That design is called the liberal arts, an education for the common good which benefits both the individual and society, and it is the root of almost every American bachelor's degree.

Graphic G2: Evolution of the Liberal Arts in America
1636 — Harvard Founded: The colonial model brings broad study in a close community where students and teachers lived and learned together.[53]
1810 — Prussian Specialized Model Appears: Prussia creates universities designed to serve the state through early, narrow specialization.[54]
1828 — Yale Report Rejection: Yale faculty famously reject the Prussian model, affirming that a republic needs broad minds, not narrow technicians.
1862 — Morrill Land-Grant Act: Expanded practical and agricultural education while preserving liberal arts foundations.
1876 — Johns Hopkins Founded: The modern American research university is born, combining German research with undergraduate broad education.

Read full history: From ancient roots to general education

Ancient roots. Liberal arts education began in Greece about 3,500 years ago and evolved through Rome, North Africa and the Middle East, and Europe. Its purpose was to educate people for the common good: to benefit both the individual and important needs of society. Over that long span the subjects studied steadily grew, but the approach always involved a close relationship between teachers and students.[52]

Even as American higher education expanded to include engineering, business, and agriculture, an engineer, nurse or accountant was still expected to spend part of a bachelor's degree on writing, history, science and ideas outside the field. Unlike in most of the world, an aspiring physician or lawyer had to complete an undergraduate course of study before beginning professional education. General education requirements at a comprehensive or research university are the liberal arts tradition, surviving under another name.

What "liberal arts" means, and what it doesn't

In plain terms: "Liberal arts" is not a list of majors. It is a way of being educated: what you study, and how.

Three common mistakes:

  • "Liberal arts means the humanities." It never did. The tradition has always included mathematics and the sciences. A physics major at a liberal arts college is getting a liberal arts education.
  • "Liberal arts means an education for nothing in particular." Liberal arts means an education not tied to one job, which is a different thing. In the study behind this site, major was unrelated to long-term success.[60]
  • "Liberal arts has something to do with politics." "Liberal" here comes from the Latin for "free." It meant the education suited to a free person who must make their own judgments.
Dimension Part In practice
What is studied (Content) Not tied to one occupation A major in a field of knowledge, not a job title.
The full span of knowledge Substantial study outside the major; seeing how fields connect.
Intellectual skills Writing, reasoning, information seeking, working on questions with no single right answer.
How it is studied (Context) Engaging teaching Discussion, seminars, students doing the thinking.
Larger perspectives Serious contact with people and ideas unlike one's own.
An educational community Faculty, students and staff involved with one another outside class as well as in it.

The research behind this site found that the second half of that table, the context, was the most consistently linked to how graduates' lives turned out.[61] A college can list every subject in the catalog and still not deliver an education that has a long-lasting impact on graduates' lives.

The pressure to make college pay off fast

In plain terms: College is expensive, so people reasonably ask what it pays. The trouble starts when the only answer anyone measures is the paycheck.

Families are encouraged to compare starting salaries by major. Rankings count earnings soon after graduation. Lawmakers tie funding to job placement. Colleges respond the way any organization responds to what they are measured on: they add narrow career programs, enlarge majors until there is little room for anything else, and trim the parts of college that are hard to count, such as student time with faculty.

The evidence suggests the trade is a poor one. In the study behind this site, specialized preparation helped with the first job, but that advantage faded.[62] Graduates who took more than half their courses outside their major were 24% more likely to report higher incomes later.[63]

"Strengthening graduates' readiness for the job market is not only appropriate, it is essential. But it is a floor, not a ceiling." — Richard A. Detweiler[64]
"I asked a group of students to describe the person they hoped to be ten years from now. One answered quietly: 'I want to be someone my community can count on. Someone who gives back what was given to me.' Around the table, nearly 20 students offered their own versions of the same answer, and not one mentioned salary, status or job title." — Richard A. Detweiler, Inside Higher Ed, September 2, 2026[65]

The case for the liberal arts college

In plain terms: The experiences that matter can happen anywhere. At a liberal arts college, they are the standard, not the exception.

The difference is in the odds:

  • Twice as likely: In the study, liberal arts colleges were on average twice as likely to provide the six experiences that have a long-term impact on graduates' lives.[66]
  • 45% against 24%: In a Gallup-Strada national study, 45% of graduates of colleges with fewer than 5,000 students said "my professors care about me as a person." At larger institutions, only 24% did.[67]
  • 3%: In the Gallup-Purdue study of 30,000 graduates, only 3% strongly agreed that they had had all six of the key supportive and hands-on experiences in college.[68]

What employers say

In plain terms: Employers keep asking for the same things: people who can communicate, solve problems, think critically and adapt. Those are the abilities a broad education builds, and as AI takes over routine technical work, employers expect them to matter more.

Why prestige and rankings are a poor guide

In plain terms: A famous name opens some doors at the start. For most graduates it changes little after that. And the rankings that measure fame are measuring fame.

What prestige does do: It may open early doors on a first résumé, and it matters in a few narrow fields such as selective corporate law firms, investment banks, and elite consulting firms.

What prestige does not do:

  • It is not where most leaders come from: In 2023, 88% of Fortune 100 chief executives had not attended an Ivy League college as undergraduates.[87]
  • Its advantage is early and narrow: Raj Chetty, David Deming, and John Friedman (2023) found that while Ivy-Plus attendance helped students reach the top 1% of earners via consulting and finance, across all graduates the effect on average earnings was small and not statistically significant.[88]
  • The Dale & Krueger finding: Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger tracked earnings for more than 20 years. They found that the effect of attending a more selective college fell to close to zero once students' own abilities and ambitions were accounted for.[89]

Five things most people get wrong

  1. "Your major determines your career." Major was unrelated to long-term success for most graduates in the study.
  2. "Rankings tell you which college is best." Rankings measure wealth, selectivity, and reputation—not what happens to students.
  3. "A big university offers more." It has more offers. Whether a given student gets any is a different question.
  4. "The sticker price is the price." Most students pay less than published tuition. Compare the net price after grants.[103]
  5. "Liberal arts graduates can't get jobs." Employers say the opposite, and long-term income evidence favors breadth.[104]
Evaluation Guide

How to read any college in ten minutes

Whatever kind of college it is, five questions get past the brochure:

  1. How many professors will know a first-year student by the end of the year?
  2. How much of the degree can be taken outside the major?
  3. In a typical class, who does the talking?
  4. What share of students do research, an internship or a long project, and is there money for those who cannot work unpaid?
  5. What share of students like this one finish, and what does it cost them after aid?
